Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage

Every number a quant ever reports — a backtest's growth, a fund's Sharpe ratio, a strategy's worst loss — is built from the same handful of return calculations. Get them slightly wrong and you will manufacture performance that doesn't exist, or miss risk that does. This session nails down the arithmetic once, with code and simulations that check every formula against a known truth. The core move: add log returns across time, add simple returns across assets, and never confuse the two.

2.3.1 One trade, four numbers

The same trade can be described four ways, and each description answers a different question. Mixing them up is the most common arithmetic error in finance.

You buy 200 shares at $100 and sell them at $110. Your broker statement shows a P&L — profit and loss in currency — of $2,000. That number is what pays the rent, but it is useless for comparison: the same trade on 2,000 shares would show ten times as much. So quants almost always work with returns, which divide out the size of the position.

The simple return is the percentage change you learned at school: 10.00%. The log return is the natural logarithm of the price ratio: 0.0953. For small moves they are almost identical; for big moves, or over long periods, they diverge — and each has exactly one job it does perfectly.

📌 Key idea — two returns, two jobs

Simple returns add across assets: a portfolio's simple return is exactly the weighted sum of its holdings' simple returns. Log returns add across time: a year's log return is exactly the sum of its daily log returns. Use simple returns to combine positions and log returns to combine periods, and convert between them with r=ln⁡(1+R)r = \ln(1 + R).

🧭 Margin note — an engineer's analogy

Log returns are the decibels of finance. Gains multiply, just as amplifier stages multiply a signal; taking logs turns multiplication into addition, which is why a log-scale chart of wealth shows steady compounding as a straight line (fig 2.3.5).

2.3.2 From prices to a track record

Every performance number — in a backtest, a fund factsheet or your own trading journal — comes out of the same five-step pipeline. Each step has one classic bug.

Step 1 matters more than it looks: returns must be computed on prices adjusted for splits and dividends, or a 2-for-1 split shows up as a −50% crash. Whether dividends are included is the difference between a price return and a total return — you met both in Session 2.2 — Equities, Indices & ETFs, and Session 4.3 — Corporate Actions, Security Master & Continuous Futures builds the adjustment pipeline properly.

2.3.3 The math of returns

Six formulas carry the whole course. Read each one in words first.

Simple and log returns

Price PtP_t at the end of period tt, dividend DtD_t paid during it. The simple return is the fractional change in your money; the log return is its natural logarithm:

Rt=Pt+DtPt−1−1,rt=ln⁡(1+Rt)R_t = \frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad r_t = \ln(1 + R_t)

Aggregating across time and across assets

Over TT periods, simple returns compound (multiply) while log returns add. Across assets with portfolio weights wiw_i, it is the other way round:

1+R0→T=∏t=1T(1+Rt),r0→T=∑t=1Trt,Rp,t=∑iwiRi,t1 + R_{0\to T} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + R_t), \qquad r_{0\to T} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t, \qquad R_{p,t} = \sum_{i} w_i R_{i,t}

Annualising

If daily returns are independent, the mean grows with time and the variance grows with time, so volatility grows with the square root of time. With 252 trading days a year, from a daily mean R‾\bar R and daily standard deviation ss:

μann=252R‾,σann=252s,CAGR=(WTW0)1/Y−1\mu_{\text{ann}} = 252\,\bar R, \qquad \sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{252}\; s, \qquad \text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{W_T}{W_0}\right)^{1/Y} - 1

Here WtW_t is wealth and YY the number of years. The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is what you actually experienced; μann\mu_{\text{ann}} is the average of what happened each day — and they are not the same number (§2.3.4).

Risk-adjusting

The Sharpe ratio is annualised excess return per unit of annualised volatility. The drawdown at time tt is how far wealth sits below its running peak:

SR=252𝔼[Rt−rf]σ(Rt−rf),DDt=Wtmaxs≤t⁡Ws−1\SR = \sqrt{252}\;\frac{\E[R_t - r_f]}{\sigma(R_t - r_f)}, \qquad \mathrm{DD}_t = \frac{W_t}{\max_{s \le t} W_s} - 1
🧭 Margin note — the √252 comes with a condition

Scaling volatility by 252\sqrt{252} assumes daily returns are independent. Strategies whose returns are autocorrelated — trend followers, illiquid assets, anything marked to model — break this, usually making the annualised Sharpe ratio look better than it is. Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie shows how to correct for it.

2.3.4 Compounding, drag & the asymmetry of losses

Averages lie about compounding. The arithmetic mean of your returns is always higher than the rate at which your money actually grows — and the gap is set by volatility.

Start with the asymmetry. Lose 50%, then gain 50%, and you are down -25%, because the gain applies to a smaller base. The deeper the hole, the steeper the climb out:

Averaging hides this. In the lab, twenty years of synthetic prices with a true drift of 8% and volatility of 20% produce an arithmetic mean return of 10.43% a year — but the money compounded at a CAGR of only 8.82%. What compounds is the mean log return, 8.45%, and the gap between the two is volatility drag:

g≈μ−12σ2g \;\approx\; \mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2

The lab measures a drag of 1.98% against a predicted σ2/2\sigma^2/2 of 1.98%. Two percentage points a year, every year, just for being volatile.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the average return was 10%, so I made 10% a year"

No — you made the geometric rate. Fund marketing loves the arithmetic mean because it is always the bigger number. When you compare strategies, compare CAGR or mean log returns, and remember that reducing volatility raises growth even when the average return is unchanged.

Also notice the sampling noise: the true drift was 8% but twenty years of data estimated 10.43%. The standard error of a 20-year mean return is about σ/20≈\sigma/\sqrt{20} \approx 4.5%. Mean returns are the hardest thing in finance to estimate — a theme that returns in every unit.

2.3.5 Sharpe & drawdown, in code

The whole pipeline is a dozen lines of pandas. The companion lab uses the quantnb.returns module, but it is worth writing once by hand.

import numpy as np
import quantnb as qn

lab = qn.Lab("2.3")                                       # seeded: same numbers every run
px = qn.synth.gbm_prices(252 * 20, mu=0.08, sigma=0.20, rng=lab.rng)["price"]

r  = px.pct_change().iloc[1:]                          # simple returns
lr = np.log(px / px.shift(1)).iloc[1:]                  # log returns

wealth = (1 + r).cumprod()                             # compound, never sum
cagr   = wealth.iloc[-1] ** (1 / 20) - 1
excess = r - 0.03 / 252                               # 3% risk-free, per day
sharpe = excess.mean() / excess.std() * np.sqrt(252)
dd     = wealth / wealth.cummax() - 1                   # drawdown path, ≤ 0

assert np.isclose(lr.sum(), np.log(px.iloc[-1] / px.iloc[0]))   # log returns add

On the lab's path the numbers are: CAGR 8.82%, volatility 19.90%, Sharpe ratio 0.37 against a 3% risk-free rate, and a maximum drawdown of -44.5%. One dollar became $5.42.

MeasureWhat it answersBlind spot
CAGRHow fast did the money actually grow?Says nothing about the ride
VolatilityHow big are typical swings?Treats upside and downside alike; misses fat tails
Sharpe ratioHow much excess return per unit of risk?Assumes volatility is the right risk; noisy on short samples

💡 Tip — a Sharpe ratio is an estimate, not a fact

A Sharpe ratio of 0.37 over twenty years has a standard error of roughly (1+SR2/2)/Y≈0.23\sqrt{(1 + \SR^2/2)/Y} \approx 0.23 — you could not rule out zero skill. Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie derives this, and Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO adds what happens when you tried many strategies before picking this one.

2.3.6 Leverage: when more becomes less

Leverage multiplies both the mean and the volatility, so it leaves the Sharpe ratio unchanged. It does not leave growth unchanged — and the difference is where accounts go to die.

Leverage LL means holding LL dollars of exposure for each dollar of capital, borrowing the rest at the risk-free rate. Rebalanced daily, the levered return is Rt(L)=LRt−(L−1)rfR^{(L)}_t = L R_t - (L-1)\, r_f. The mean rises linearly in LL, but the drag rises with L2L^2:

g(L)=rf+L(μ−rf)−12L2σ2,L⋆=μ−rfσ2g(L) = r_f + L(\mu - r_f) - \tfrac{1}{2}L^2\sigma^2, \qquad L^\star = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma^2}

Growth peaks at L⋆L^\star — here 1.25 — and then falls. Past 2L⋆2L^\star you would grow faster holding cash, and a little further on growth turns negative: a levered position in an asset that goes up on average will, typically, lose money.

On this one lucky path, 2× beat 1× (10.5% a year) — at the price of a -74% drawdown. The 3× version compounded at only 7.8% and fell -91.5% from its peak. Few people hold on through a 91% loss.

One path proves nothing, so the lab simulates 2,000 ten-year paths, using the same random shocks at every leverage level so the comparison is fair. The median growth rate traces the theory almost exactly:

LeverageMedian CAGRTheory g(L)P(losing money over 10 y)P(a 90% drawdown)

📌 Key idea — leverage is a volatility decision

Because drag grows with L2L^2, there is always a leverage beyond which more exposure means less wealth. The optimum depends on μ\mu — the number you know least well — so professionals size below it. Overestimate your edge and optimal leverage becomes ruinous leverage. This is the seed of the Kelly criterion (Session 3.6 — Bet Sizing, Kelly & Ruin) and of volatility targeting (Session 9.5 — Sizing, Vol Targeting & Allocating Across Strategies).

2.3.7 Return bugs that fake alpha

None of these raise an exception. All of them change your conclusions. Every one has shipped in a real backtest.

BugSymptomFix
Summing simple returns over timeLab: sum = 209%, true total = 442%(1 + r).prod() - 1, or sum log returns
Weighting log returns across assetsLab: +30%/−20% at 50/50 gives 0.0196 instead of 0.0488Weight simple returns, then take the log
√252 on weekly or monthly dataSharpe inflated by √21 ≈ 4.6× on monthly dataAnnualise with periods per year of that data
Returns on unadjusted pricesFake crashes on splits; dividends missingUse adjusted or total-return series
Forgetting the risk-free rateCash-like strategies look skilful when rates are highAlways compute on excess returns
Percent vs percentage points"Returns fell 2%" when they fell from 10% to 8%Say "2 percentage points"
⚠️ Pitfall — "3× leveraged ETF = 3× the index return"

Only for one day. A daily-rebalanced 3× fund held for a year earns roughly 3μ−2rf−92σ23\mu - 2r_f - \tfrac{9}{2}\sigma^2 in log terms (it borrows two dollars for every one it holds), not 3μ3\mu. If the index ends a volatile year flat, the 3× fund ends it down. This is the arithmetic of §2.3.6 — and why leveraged ETFs are trading tools, not investments.

2.3.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

P&L — profit and loss in currency; what you earned, scaled by position size.
Simple return — Rt=(Pt+Dt)/Pt−1−1R_t = (P_t + D_t)/P_{t-1} - 1; adds exactly across assets.
Log return — rt=ln⁡(1+Rt)r_t = \ln(1 + R_t); adds exactly across time.
Excess return — return minus the risk-free rate over the same period; the part that measures skill.
Compounding — returns multiplying on a growing (or shrinking) base: ∏(1+Rt)\prod (1 + R_t).
Annualisation — rescaling to a yearly figure: mean × periods, volatility × √periods.
Volatility — the standard deviation of returns, usually annualised.
Volatility drag — the gap between arithmetic mean and compound growth, ≈ σ²/2 a year.
Sharpe ratio — annualised mean excess return divided by its annualised volatility.
Drawdown — the fall of wealth below its running peak; the maximum drawdown is the worst one.
Leverage — exposure per unit of capital; multiplies mean and volatility, and drag by its square.

Check yourself

1. A fund reports an average annual return of 12% but a CAGR of 8%. Roughly how volatile is it?
Drag ≈ 12% − 8% = 4% ≈ σ²/2, so σ ≈ √0.08 ≈ 28% a year. A large gap between the arithmetic and geometric returns is a volatility tell.
2. You hold three assets with weights 50/30/20 and have their daily log returns. How do you get the portfolio's daily return?
Convert each to simple returns (R=er−1R = e^r - 1), take the weighted sum, and convert back if you need a log return. Weighting log returns directly is only approximately right — and badly wrong on big moves.
3. A strategy's monthly returns have a Sharpe ratio of 0.30. What is the annualised figure — and what are you assuming?
0.30 × √12 ≈ 1.04. Not × √252: annualise with the number of periods per year of the data you have. The √12 assumes monthly returns are independent; positive autocorrelation makes the true figure lower.
4. The S&P 500 ends a very choppy year exactly flat. What happened to a daily-rebalanced 3× fund on it?
It lost money. A flat index means μ≈σ2/2\mu \approx \sigma^2/2. The 3× fund's log growth is about 3μ−2rf−92σ2=−3σ2−2rf3\mu - 2r_f - \tfrac{9}{2}\sigma^2 = -3\sigma^2 - 2r_f: its drag grows with the square of the leverage, and it pays to borrow. Leverage turns volatility into a certain cost.
5. A colleague's backtest reports a total return of 209% by summing daily returns. Is that too high or too low?
It could be either — summing simple returns ignores compounding. In the lab's 20-year path the true total was 442% against a naive sum of 209%. On a path with big drawdowns the sum can overstate instead. Always compound.
