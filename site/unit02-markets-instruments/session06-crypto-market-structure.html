Crypto Market Structure

Crypto markets use the machinery you have already met — order books, spreads, futures, margin — but rebuilt from scratch, open every hour of every day, and often run by a single company or a single piece of code. The prices are easy to see; the risks that traditional markets bury inside law and institutions are not. The core move: before you model a crypto price, map who holds the coins, who owes whom, and what gets liquidated when the price moves.

2.6.1 One company, four jobs: the centralised exchange

Most crypto trading happens in an app that looks like a brokerage. Behind it, one company does the work that four separately regulated firms do in the markets of Session 2.1.

In Session 2.1 you followed an order through a broker, an exchange, a clearing house and a custodian: four firms, four rulebooks, each checking the others. A centralised exchange (CEX) such as Binance, Coinbase or OKX does all four jobs itself: it takes your order (broker), matches it in its own limit order book (venue), sets your margin and closes your position when it runs out (clearing house), and holds every coin and dollar you deposit (custodian). Many also lend, make markets or issue a token. IOSCO, the global body of securities regulators, lists "conflicts of interest arising from vertical integration of activities and functions" and "custody and client asset protection" among the six key areas of its 2023 crypto recommendations.

Once you deposit, the coins leave your control: your balance is a row in the exchange's database, and you cannot see what stands behind it. In the US, for example, SEC Rule 15c3-3 makes a broker-dealer keep customers' securities and cash segregated from its own, and UK and EU client-asset rules do the same; a crypto exchange's promise of segregation is only as good as whoever checks it.

📌 Key idea — your exchange balance is a claim, not a coin

On a venue that does not segregate client assets — or where no one checks that it does — a CEX balance is in effect an unsecured promise from one company that also matches your trades and decides when to liquidate you. In crypto, custody risk and counterparty risk live inside the venue, so choosing where to trade is a risk decision, not a convenience.

🧭 Margin note — a market that never closes

Crypto trades every hour of every day, so this session annualises with 365 days (8,760 hours), not 252 (annualisation: Session 2.3). Prices differ across venues — Makarov and Schoar (2020) found large, recurring arbitrage gaps between exchanges, far larger across countries than within them — and volume needs a haircut: Cong et al. (2023) estimate wash trading at over 70% of reported volume on the unregulated exchanges they studied (manipulation is Session 15.6).

2.6.2 When the venue is the risk: custody, reserves & FTX

Custody risk and counterparty risk are two faces of one question: if this firm fails tomorrow, what do I get back? In November 2022, FTX's customers found out.

Custody risk is the risk of losing assets because of how, and by whom, they are held: stolen from a hot wallet, locked behind a lost key, or quietly used by the custodian. Counterparty risk is the risk that whoever owes you — a venue, an issuer, a borrower — does not pay. A clearing house caps counterparty risk at about one day's price move (Session 2.4); on a CEX both risks sit in one company, uncapped. Custody can also fail on its own: the FBI attributed the theft of about $1.5 billion of crypto from the exchange Bybit, on or about 21 February 2025, to North Korean hackers. Bybit stayed solvent and, with emergency loans, restored full backing of client assets within about three days (CNBC, 24 February 2025): the loss fell on the firm, not its customers.

What failed was the separation of roles. The SEC alleged in December 2022 that FTX gave Alameda "a virtually unlimited 'line of credit' funded by the platform's customers" and exempted it from key risk controls — the clearing job waived for an insider, paid for by the custody job. John J. Ray III, the new chief executive, told the bankruptcy court he had never seen "such a complete failure of corporate controls". A jury convicted Bankman-Fried on all seven counts on 2 November 2023; he was sentenced to 25 years on 28 March 2024, and prosecutors said he had taken more than $8 billion of customers' money.

Under the plan confirmed on 7 October 2024 (CNBC), customers with claims up to $50,000 — most creditors by number — received about 120% of their allowed claims from 18 February 2025, and larger customer claims had received 105% by the fifth distribution on 31 July 2026 (FTX Recovery Trust). But the claims were fixed in dollars at 11 November 2022 prices, when bitcoin traded near $17,000: a customer who held bitcoin got dollars at the crash price, more than two years later. Counterparty loss is not only whether you are paid, but in what and when.

Proof of reserves, and what it cannot prove

After FTX, exchanges began publishing proof of reserves: their wallet addresses (the assets) plus a Merkle tree of customer balances (the liabilities) in which each customer can find their own. As Buterin (2022) sets out, it is a snapshot, not a stream; coins can be borrowed for the snapshot (exchanges could "shuttle collateral" between each other); other debts never enter the tree; and fiat still needs an auditor. In December 2022 the auditor Mazars paused its proof-of-reserves work for all crypto clients, citing how the public understood the reports.

⚠️ Pitfall — "proof of reserves proves the exchange is solvent"

Solvency means assets cover all liabilities all the time; proof of reserves shows certain coins in certain wallets at one moment. Unless you know the venue segregates client assets and someone checks that it does, treat a CEX balance as an unsecured loan to it: size it, spread it across venues, and withdraw what you are not using.

2.6.3 Decentralised exchanges & the constant-product AMM

A decentralised exchange replaces the company with a program. The most influential design has no order book at all — just two piles of tokens and one line of algebra.

A decentralised exchange (DEX) is a set of smart contracts — programs on a public blockchain — that you trade with straight from your own wallet. Most spot DEX volume runs through automated market makers (AMMs): instead of matching buyers with sellers, an AMM is a pool of two assets, funded by liquidity providers (LPs), that always trades against you at a formula price. Uniswap v2's pool (Adams, Zinsmeister & Robinson 2020) holds xx tokens and yy dollars of a stablecoin (§2.6.5) and accepts any trade that does not reduce the product of the reserves:

xy=k,P=yxx\,y = k, \qquad P = \frac{y}{x}

Sell Δx\Delta x tokens and the pool pays out the Δy\Delta y that keeps the product at kk. The fee ϕ\phi — 0.30% on Uniswap v2, split 0.25% to LPs and 0.05% to the protocol since the "UNIfication" governance proposal was executed on 28 December 2025 (Uniswap documentation, checked 2026) — comes off what you pay in: only (1−ϕ)Δx(1-\phi)\,\Delta x counts toward kk, but all of it stays in the pool, so kk grows with every trade:

Δy=y(1−ϕ)Δxx+(1−ϕ)Δx\Delta y = \frac{y\,(1-\phi)\,\Delta x}{x + (1-\phi)\,\Delta x}

Write the trade's size as a share of the pool, s=Δx/xs = \Delta x / x. Its total cost against the pre-trade price — fee plus market impact — is

cost(s)=1−1−ϕ1+(1−ϕ)s≈ϕ+sfor small s\text{cost}(s) = 1 - \frac{1-\phi}{1 + (1-\phi)\,s} \;\approx\; \phi + s \quad \text{for small } s

That is the rule of thumb to keep: an AMM trade costs the fee plus roughly its own size as a share of the pool. The lab's pool holds 1,000 tokens and $2,000,000, a price of $2,000:

Depth is everything: the same $20,000 sale into a pool 10 times deeper costs 0.40% instead of 1.28%. The swap itself, from the lab:

def swap(amount_in, reserve_in, reserve_out, fee=FEE):
    eff = amount_in * (1.0 - fee)                      # only this part counts toward k
    out = reserve_out * eff / (reserve_in + eff)       # keeps (reserve_in + eff)·(reserve_out − out) = k
    return out, reserve_in + amount_in, reserve_out - out   # the whole input stays: k grows

for s in SIZES:                                  # 0.1%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 25% of the token reserve
    dx = s * X0
    dy, x1, y1 = swap(dx, X0, Y0)
    exec_px = dy / dx
    cost = 1.0 - exec_px / mid0
    assert abs(cost - sell_cost_closed_form(s)) < 1e-12
🧭 Margin note — MEV, in one paragraph

Your swap waits in a public queue before a validator puts it in a block, where anyone can read it. A bot can buy just before your purchase and sell just after — a "sandwich" — pocketing the impact you were about to cause. Value extractable by whoever orders the transactions is called MEV (Daian et al. 2020 coined "miner extractable value"): front-running with the order book replaced by a block. The first defence is a tight minimum output on every swap.

💡 Looking ahead

An AMM is a market maker (a business you met in Session 1.1) with a fixed quoting rule: it never widens its spread when it is being picked off. Session 14.1 builds the order book it replaces, Session 14.5 a market maker who manages inventory risk, and Session 6.5 prices slippage on any venue.

2.6.4 Liquidity providers & impermanent loss

Someone has to fill the pool. Liquidity providers earn the fees — and pay for them by taking the other side of every arbitrageur.

When the price elsewhere moves, arbitrageurs trade against the stale pool until it matches (Angeris et al. show why a constant-product pool must track the outside price) — which means the LP sells the token as it rises and buys it as it falls. Without fees the pool's state depends only on the price PP: x=k/Px = \sqrt{k/P} and y=kPy = \sqrt{kP}, so the LP holds xP+y=2kPxP + y = 2\sqrt{kP}. If the price moves by the ratio θ=Pτ/P0\theta = P_\tau/P_0 over τ\tau years, compare that with holding the deposit:

VτLPV0=θ,VτholdV0=1+θ2,IL(θ)=VτLPVτhold−1=2θ1+θ−1≤0\frac{V^{\text{LP}}_\tau}{V_0} = \sqrt{\theta}, \qquad \frac{V^{\text{hold}}_\tau}{V_0} = \frac{1+\theta}{2}, \qquad \text{IL}(\theta) = \frac{V^{\text{LP}}_\tau}{V^{\text{hold}}_\tau} - 1 = \frac{2\sqrt{\theta}}{1+\theta} - 1 \;\le\; 0

That gap is impermanent loss — "impermanent" because it vanishes if the price returns, permanent once you withdraw. It is symmetric in the ratio: a halving and a doubling both cost −5.7% against holding, ±20% costs −0.6% or −0.4%, and a fourfold rise −20%. In the lab an arbitrageur makes 720 hourly trades against a pool on each of 40 simulated paths, using only the swap function, and the LP's result matches the formula to within 1e-15 on every path: only the final price matters.

How much does it cost on average? If the price has no drift, holding breaks even in expectation, but θ\sqrt{\theta} is concave, so the LP does not: for a lognormal price 𝔼[θ]=e−σ2τ/8\E[\sqrt{\theta}] = e^{-\sigma^2 \tau/8}, with τ\tau in years, and

𝔼[VτLP−VτholdV0]=e−σ2τ/8−1≈−σ2τ8\E\!\left[\frac{V^{\text{LP}}_\tau - V^{\text{hold}}_\tau}{V_0}\right] = e^{-\sigma^2 \tau/8} - 1 \;\approx\; -\frac{\sigma^2 \tau}{8}

At a crypto-like 80% volatility over one year, theory says −7.69%; 200,000 simulated years give −7.72% with a standard error of 0.035%. So fees must earn about 3.1% a year just to match holding at 50% volatility, 7.7% at 80% and 11.8% at 100% (1−e−σ2/81 - e^{-\sigma^2/8} for one year).

📌 Key idea — a liquidity provider is short volatility

The LP's payoff θ\sqrt{\theta} is concave, the shape an option seller holds (options arrive in Session 2.7). Providing liquidity is a bet that fees beat roughly σ²/8 a year. The loss goes to arbitrageurs trading against stale prices — the adverse selection every market maker faces, formalised by Milionis et al. (2022) as "loss-versus-rebalancing". Uniswap v3 (2021) lets LPs concentrate liquidity in a price range: more fees per dollar, more loss per move.

2.6.5 Stablecoins: four designs, four ways to break

Most crypto prices are quoted in a dollar that is not a dollar. How it is built decides how it breaks.

A stablecoin is a token designed to hold a fixed value, usually $1, by being a claim on something: an issuer's reserves, a pile of crypto collateral, a mechanism, or a hedged crypto position.

Design (examples)What stands behind $1How it breaks — and when it did
Fiat-backed (USDT, USDC)cash, Treasury bills and bank deposits held by an issuer that redeems at parUSDC fell to roughly 87–88 cents on 11 March 2023, when $3.3 billion of its roughly $40 billion of reserves sat at the failed Silicon Valley Bank; it recovered once US authorities said on 12 March that all SVB depositors would be protected.
Crypto-collateralised (DAI)more crypto than the stablecoins issued, locked in a contract that liquidates under-collateralised positionsA crash faster than the liquidations leaves the system short of collateral — and the collateral can itself depeg: DAI, then about half backed by USDC, hit 88 cents with it on 11 March 2023 (CoinDesk; Cointelegraph).
Algorithmic (UST, Terra)a mint-and-burn swap with a sister token, and little outside collateralA run began on 7 May 2022; Terra lost about $50 billion of value in three days (Liu, Makarov & Schoar 2023), and UST traded around 12 cents by 13 May (CNBC, citing CoinGecko).
Synthetic dollar (USDe, Ethena)spot crypto plus a short perpetual — the funding trade of §2.6.7, packaged as a tokenIt breaks if funding stays negative long enough to drain its reserve, or if a venue holding the short fails. On 10 October 2025 it hit 65 cents on Binance's own order book while staying near $1 elsewhere; Binance priced collateral off that thin book, and accounts using it as margin were liquidated (CoinDesk).

Read each design as something you already know. A fiat-backed coin is a narrow bank without a banking licence: it carries the credit risk of its reserves and issuer, and few firms can redeem directly — Tether let an average of six agents a month redeem for cash (Ma, Zeng & Zhang 2025). A crypto-collateralised coin is a margin loan with the liquidation rules in code. An algorithmic coin works while people believe it works; Terra's run was visible on-chain, which sped it up, and the wealthier, more sophisticated investors ran first and lost less (Liu, Makarov & Schoar 2023). A synthetic dollar is §2.6.7's basis trade in a wrapper, with that trade's risks.

⚠️ Pitfall — "a stablecoin is a dollar"

It is a claim on an issuer, a contract or a mechanism, and it can trade below $1 exactly when you need it. Your "dollar" P&L is P&L in a token, and stablecoin collateral can be marked down — on one venue's prices — and liquidate a position that was otherwise fine. Haircut it, and know who can redeem at par.

2.6.6 Perpetual futures & the funding rate

The most traded instrument in crypto is a futures contract that never expires. With no expiry there is no convergence — so the venue builds one in, as a payment between longs and shorts every few hours.

In Session 2.4 a futures contract converges to spot because at expiry it is the asset. A perpetual future (a "perp") has no expiry. The idea is older than crypto — Shiller (1993) proposed perpetual contracts on hard-to-trade indices — but it took off when BitMEX introduced the first crypto perpetual in 2016 (He et al.), long offering up to 100× leverage (250× since April 2024, as reported by The Block). By one estimate, perps traded over $60 trillion in 2024 (Chitra 2025).

What replaces convergence is the funding rate. With perp price FtF_t, spot StS_t and premium πt=Ft/St−1\pi_t = F_t/S_t - 1 (π for premium), at each funding time every long pays every short the funding rate times the position's notional — or receives it when the rate is negative; the money moves between traders, not to the venue (Ackerer, Hugonnier & Jermann 2026 derive the resulting no-arbitrage prices). Binance publishes it for its default 8-hour interval, and Hyperliquid uses the same form paid hourly (checked 2026):

FRj=π‾j+clamp⁡(ι−π‾j,−0.05%,+0.05%)\mathrm{FR}_j = \bar{\pi}_j + \operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\iota - \bar{\pi}_j,\; -0.05\%,\; +0.05\%\big)

where π‾j\bar{\pi}_j is the premium averaged over funding interval jj and ι\iota an "interest" component of 0.01% per 8 hours. While the average premium sits between −0.04% and 0.06%, funding is exactly ι\iota — a built-in 10.95% a year from longs to shorts when nothing is happening. Outside that band funding follows the premium, so a gap costs the crowded side about the gap itself, every interval.

What funding does to the premium: a toy model

The lab simulates 2,000 years of hourly trading, with spot at 60% volatility. Speculative demand pushes the premium — a steady long bias plus 2-basis-point hourly shocks, correlated 0.5 with spot because buyers chase rallies — and arbitrage, paid by funding, closes a fraction ψ\psi of the gap each hour (a 8-hour half-life):

πt+1=(1−ψ)πt+η+σπεt+1,sd(π)=σπ1−(1−ψ)2\pi_{t+1} = (1-\psi)\,\pi_t + \eta + \sigma_\pi\,\varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \text{sd}(\pi) = \frac{\sigma_\pi}{\sqrt{1-(1-\psi)^2}}

The counterfactual switches funding off: nobody is paid to close the gap, ψ=0\psi = 0, and the premium is a random walk with drift. The model assumes that funding-paid arbitrage closes ψ\psi of the gap each hour (the funding it computes never feeds back), so it illustrates the mechanism rather than testing it; both regimes match their theory. With funding, the premium after a year averages +0.021% (theory +0.020%) with a standard deviation of 0.049% (theory 0.050%), and 99% of paths sit within 0.14% of spot. Without it, the same shocks leave the perp 14.6% above spot on average, give or take 1.8%.

Funding was exactly ι\iota in 72% of intervals and negative in 6%. A year of funding averaged 13.9% of notional (5th–95th percentile 12.5% to 15.4%), above the 10.95% that ι\iota alone pays, because the premium averages 0.02%, above ι\iota, so more intervals break out above the band — where funding follows the premium up — than below it.

📌 Key idea — funding is a thermostat, not a peg

Funding makes a gap expensive to hold and pays whoever closes it, but it cannot force convergence the way expiry does. He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter find perp prices stray further from their no-arbitrage values in crypto than comparable prices do in currency markets, though less over time. A perp tracks spot only as well as arbitrage capital is paid, and willing, to make it.

2.6.7 Basis trades, liquidation & auto-deleveraging

The funding that longs pay is income for somebody. Collecting it is the crypto basis trade — the cash-and-carry of futures, with two new ways to lose.

The trade

With a dated future it is the cash-and-carry of Session 2.4: buy spot, sell the future, and lock in the futures premium over spot, F/S−1F/S - 1, whatever the price does. Crypto desks call that premium "the basis"; in 2.4's basis convention, S−FS - F, the same gap is negative, so check the sign. Crypto futures premiums often sit far above dollar interest rates: Schmeling, Schrimpf and Todorov (2023) document bitcoin and ether carry averaging above 10% a year and at times 60%, left on the table because arbitrage is risky "due to spikes in margins and liquidations amid drawdowns". Whether it is a risk premium is a question for Session 8.4.

With a perp you buy one unit of spot, short one unit of the perp and collect funding. The legs cancel except for the gap between them, so the year's gain is exactly

gain=∑jFRjFtj−(Sendπend−S0π0)\text{gain} = \sum_j \mathrm{FR}_j\,F_{t_j} \;-\; \big(S_{\text{end}}\,\pi_{\text{end}} - S_0\,\pi_0\big)

— funding received, minus any widening of the premium. After 0.20% of fees the trade earned 14.0% of starting notional on average (5th–95th percentile 6.9% to 23.9%, since the notional moves with the price) and 0% of paths lost money, while the spot leg alone had a standard deviation of 64%. With the short leg at 3×, capital is 1.33 times notional and the return on it 10.5% — assuming the short is topped up and never liquidated, and that funding stays as positive as this model makes it; real funding has turned negative for long stretches. It is not free money, for two reasons.

1 · The hedge leg can be liquidated. Hold the spot in cold storage and the short perp at 3× on an exchange, and a 32.7% rally liquidates the short while the offsetting gain sits in a wallet the exchange cannot see. In the lab that happens within a quarter on 29% of paths and within a year on 54%, leaving you long spot at the top of a rally. 2 · The venue can fail (§2.6.2). Assume, purely for illustration, a 2% annual failure probability with nothing recovered. If both legs sit on the failed venue, the expected return falls from 10.5% to 8.3%, and it breaks even at a failure probability of 9.5%; with the spot in cold storage only the margin, 25% of the capital, is at stake, and the expected return is 9.8%. The average is not the problem; the loss tail is.

How a crypto venue liquidates you

Liquidation is the forced closing of a position by the venue when its margin runs out. In Session 2.4 a margin call asked you for cash, and the position was closed only if you could not pay. A crypto venue checks equity against the maintenance margin continuously, on a mark price built from spot indices rather than its own last trade, and there is no call: the engine takes over. For an isolated-margin long with leverage LL and maintenance rate mm (a fraction of the current notional), that happens when the price falls to

Pliq=P01−1/L1−m,d⋆=1/L−m1−mP_{\text{liq}} = P_0\,\frac{1 - 1/L}{1 - m}, \qquad d^\star = \frac{1/L - m}{1 - m}

— almost exactly the d⋆=1/L−md^\star = 1/L - m of Session 2.4. Then comes a waterfall (fig 2.6.7). At the bankruptcy price the position's equity is exactly zero. If the engine sells before it, part of the leftover margin feeds an insurance fund as a fee; if the price gaps through it, the fund absorbs the loss; and if the fund runs dry, auto-deleveraging (ADL) closes profitable positions on the other side, ranked by profit and leverage (Binance's auto-deleveraging FAQ).

On 10 October 2025 more than $19 billion of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated within 24 hours, the largest such day on record (CoinDesk Research), and on the on-chain venue Hyperliquid, ADL closed $2.1 billion of positions in 12 minutes (Chitra 2025): traders who had been right had their winners shut.

⚠️ Pitfall — "delta-neutral means riskless"

A basis trade has no price exposure only while both legs survive and both venues pay. Size the short leg for the rally you can fund (Session 2.4's lesson), keep collateral where the margin is, and count venue failure as a cost.

2.6.8 The risk stack & regulation

Traditional markets hide most of these risks behind law, institutions and a closing bell. In crypto they are all on your side of the screen, stacked under every position you hold.

For a quant, regulation changes the bottom of the risk stack — an authorised venue must segregate client assets and publish its rules — not the top.

2.6.10 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Centralised exchange — a company that runs a crypto trading venue and is also broker, clearing house and custodian for its customers.
Decentralised exchange — a venue built from smart contracts on a public blockchain; you trade from your own wallet and trades settle on-chain.
Automated market maker — a venue that quotes prices from a formula over pooled reserves, such as xy=kx\,y = k, instead of matching orders in a book.
Perpetual future — a futures contract with no expiry, held close to spot by periodic funding payments between longs and shorts.
Funding rate — the periodic rate longs pay shorts on a perpetual future (shorts pay when it is negative): the perp's average premium over spot, replaced by a small fixed interest rate (0.01% per 8 hours on Binance) whenever the premium is within 0.05% of it.
Stablecoin — a token designed to hold a fixed value, usually $1, backed by fiat reserves, over-collateralised crypto, an algorithm, or a hedged crypto position (a synthetic dollar).
Impermanent loss — a liquidity provider's shortfall against simply holding the deposit, caused by arbitrageurs trading the pool back to the market price; 2√θ/(1+θ) − 1 for a constant-product pool.
Proof of reserves — an exchange's published snapshot of its wallet holdings, often with a Merkle tree of customer balances; it shows assets at one moment, not solvency.
Insurance fund — a venue's reserve, fed by liquidation fees, that absorbs losses on positions closed beyond their bankruptcy price.
Auto-deleveraging — a venue's last resort when its insurance fund is exhausted: profitable opposing positions are closed, ranked by profit and leverage, to cover a bankrupt position.
Custody risk — the risk of losing assets because of how and by whom they are held: hacks, lost keys, commingling or misuse.
Counterparty risk — the risk that whoever owes you — a venue, issuer, borrower or trading partner — fails to pay.
Liquidation — the forced closing of a leveraged position by the venue or broker once its margin falls below the maintenance requirement.

Check yourself

1. Your exchange halts withdrawals and files for bankruptcy while holding your bitcoin. Which two risks just materialised, and what did FTX's customers get back?
Custody risk (the coins may not be where the ledger says) and counterparty risk (you now hold a claim on a bankrupt company). FTX's customers got about 105–120% of their allowed claims, depending on claim size — in dollars at November 2022 prices, more than two years later — so bitcoin holders missed the rebound. Size an exchange balance like an unsecured loan.
2. You sell 100 tokens into the lab's pool of 1,000 tokens and $2,000,000. What does it cost, and what would help?
9.34% against the quote: the fee plus roughly your share of the pool (ϕ+s\phi + s). Split the sale so arbitrageurs refill the pool between pieces, or use a deeper pool — ten times the depth, about a tenth of the impact.
3. You deposit $10,000 as a liquidity provider when the token is at $2,000. It doubles. Ignoring fees, how do you compare with someone who simply held the deposit?
Holding is worth $15,000; the LP position, $10,000 × √2, is worth $14,142: an impermanent loss of −5.7% (−$858). Arbitrageurs bought the token from the pool all the way up.
4. A perp's premium averaged 0.10% over an 8-hour interval. What is the funding rate, who pays it, and what does it add up to if it lasts a year? What would you do?
FR = 0.10% + clamp(0.01% − 0.10%, ±0.05%) = 0.05% per interval, paid by longs to shorts — 54.8% a year over 1,095 intervals if it persisted. Short the perp and buy spot to collect it: exactly the trade that shrinks the premium (fig 2.6.5).
5. You run the basis trade with spot in cold storage and a 3× short perp on an exchange. The price rallies 35% in a week. What happens, and how should you have set it up?
The short is liquidated after a 32.7% rally — its loss realised on the exchange, the matching gain stranded in cold storage — and you are left long and unhedged at the top (within a quarter on 29% of the lab's paths). Use lower leverage on the hedge, keep collateral where the margin is, and automate top-ups.
6. A friend trades 25× longs "with a tight stop". How far can the price fall before liquidation, and how does that compare with an ordinary day?
d* = 3.5% with a 0.5% maintenance margin, against a one-day standard deviation of 3.1% at 60% annual volatility. In the lab 21% of such accounts are liquidated within a day, and the median lasts 81 hours. At that leverage the engine is the stop — and it sells at the market.
