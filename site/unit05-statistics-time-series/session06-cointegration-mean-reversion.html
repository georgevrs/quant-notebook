Cointegration & Mean Reversion

Two prices can each wander forever and still never drift apart — if the right combination of them is stationary. This session builds that combination from scratch: the two-step and full-rank tests that find it, the process that describes how it reverts, and the one number, the half-life, that tells you whether it is worth trading.

5.6.1 One stationary combination of two nonstationary series

Neither price is predictable on its own — but the right combination of the two can be, and that combination is what a pairs trade actually buys and sells.

Take two prices that each follow a random walk: no fixed level, no way to say where either will be next year. Session 3.5 built series exactly like this, and Session 5.4 showed you how to test whether a single series is stationary at all. Now suppose the two share the same wander — one underlying random walk drives both, scaled differently — plus each carries its own private noise on top. Individually, both series are still non-stationary: no fixed mean, variance that grows without bound. But look at one particular combination of the two, say one unit of A minus h units of B, and the shared wander cancels exactly. What is left behaves nothing like either input: it keeps returning to a fixed average, however far A and B individually drift. That is cointegration — two (or more) individually non-stationary series that share enough of their randomness that one specific linear combination of them is stationary.

The weight h that makes the combination stationary is the hedge ratio: for every unit of A you hold, you hold h units of B to cancel the shared drift. The stationary combination itself, A − h·B, is the spread — the thing that actually mean-reverts, and the thing a pairs trade is built to buy low and sell high. This session simulates exactly this setup: two ten-year price paths built from one shared random walk, with a true hedge ratio of 1.5 and a spread whose half-life is 20 trading days — a number this session spends most of its time explaining. After ten years the two paths themselves wander a long way: A goes from 100 to 63.7, B from 50 to 28.1 — both drift down for ten straight years by pure chance, exactly what an unpredictable random walk is allowed to do — yet the spread between them keeps returning to the same place.

Session 5.4's shorthand is worth reusing here: a series that needs one round of differencing to become stationary is I(1) ("integrated of order one"); an already-stationary series is I(0). Cointegration is precisely the case where two (or more) I(1) series combine into an I(0) one. Why would that ever happen with real prices? Because two assets are rarely driven by pure, independent noise — an airline's share price and jet-fuel futures, a gold miner and the spot price of gold, two regional bank indices exposed to the same rate cycle, all share some of the same macro or sector-level drift. Neither leg is individually forecastable, but the gap between them can still be anchored by that shared exposure — which is exactly why a pairs trade is a bet on a relationship surviving, not a bet on either price's direction.

📌 Key idea

Cointegration is not about the two series being predictable — neither is. It is about one specific combination of them being predictable enough to be stationary, while the series themselves are not.

🧭 Margin note — correlation is not cointegration

Correlation measures whether two series move together over a window, usually in changes or returns. Cointegration is about levels sharing a common trend, so a specific combination never wanders. Two series can be highly correlated in their day-to-day moves without being cointegrated (they can still drift apart forever), and — less intuitively — a genuinely cointegrated pair can have unremarkable return correlation. They answer different questions; §5.6.3 shows just how misleading correlation-flavoured evidence (a high R²) can be here.

5.6.2 The Engle-Granger two-step test

Engle and Granger's 1987 test turns the definition in §5.6.1 into two ordinary regressions you already know how to run.

The Engle-Granger test does exactly what the definition suggests, in two steps. Step 1 — regress. Run an OLS regression of A on B with an intercept, At=α+hBt+εtA_t = \alpha + h B_t + \varepsilon_t. The fitted slope is your estimate of the hedge ratio; the residual εˆt\hat\varepsilon_t is your candidate spread. Step 2 — test the residual. Run the augmented Dickey-Fuller test you met in Session 5.4 on that residual. If it rejects a unit root, the residual is stationary, the pair is cointegated, and the residual you already computed is the spread — no extra work.

One wrinkle worth knowing before you reach for a table of critical values: the residual in step 1 was chosen by that very regression to fit as well as possible, so it looks a little more stationary than an ordinary observed series would by chance. Session 5.4's plain Dickey-Fuller critical value for a series like this would be about −2.86 at 5% — but that number is for a series you did not fit anything to. Engle and Granger's own paper flagged the gap, and MacKinnon's response-surface tables give the correct, wider critical value for a residual generated this way — here, −3.34 instead of −2.86, a real difference that could matter close to the margin even though the hero pair's −7.33 clears either bar easily. statsmodels' coint() applies the correct table automatically; a bare adfuller() call on the residual, on its own, does not know the residual came from a regression and will quietly hand you the wrong (too liberal) critical value.

statsmodels packages both steps into one call, coint(A, B), and it agrees with the manual version: stat −7.33, p = 1.3e-09. With ten years of daily data the fitted hedge ratio is essentially exact — the whole gap between A and h·B in fig 5.6.3 is the mean-reverting wobble this session built in on purpose.

5.6.3 Reading the test: size, power, and why R² lies

A test is only useful if it actually discriminates — rare false alarms, reliable true positives — and Engle-Granger's second step exists because the first step, alone, is dangerously easy to fool.

Run the whole two-step procedure 400 times on 3-year samples under two scenarios. Under the null — two independent random walks with no shared trend at all — the test should reject cointegration about as often as its significance level allows: it does, rejecting at 5% in 5% of replications, matching the promise almost exactly. Under the alternative — the true construction from §5.6.1, with a 20-trading-day half-life — it correctly rejects the unit root in 83% of samples. Three years of daily data is enough, most of the time, to catch a spread this fast.

Here is the trap the second step exists to catch. Across those same null replications — two series with nothing whatsoever in common — the plain OLS regression of one on the other has a mean R² of 0.25, and in 16% of samples it exceeds 0.5. A high R² between two random walks is not a coincidence you should feel embarrassed about missing; it is the expected behaviour of unrelated non-stationary series, first demonstrated by Granger and Newbold, and it has a name: spurious regression. Two prices with an eye-catching R² are not thereby cointegrated — only a rejected unit root in the residual tells you that, which is exactly why the test has a second step and not just a first one.

Granger and Newbold offered a quick smell test before you even reach for a formal one: compare the R² to the regression's Durbin-Watson statistic. A genuinely spurious regression tends to have a residual that is itself highly autocorrelated (a Durbin-Watson statistic near zero, since a random-walk-like residual barely changes from one period to the next), while R² stays misleadingly high — one of their own published examples had R² of 0.997 against a Durbin-Watson of only 0.53. R² comfortably above the Durbin-Watson statistic is a warning sign worth a second look, long before you run anything.

Direction matters too, and not just as sampling noise. In this session's construction, B carries none of the idiosyncratic wobble — it is a clean multiple of the shared trend, while A carries all of the mean-reverting noise on top. Regressing the noisy series on the clean one is the right way around; the other way is a textbook errors-in-variables problem, and it costs real power. Over a short, 10-day-half-life, one-year sample, regressing A on B (correct direction) rejects the null in 51% of replications; regressing B on A (wrong direction) rejects in only 17%. The two directions reach different conclusions on 36% of samples. With ten years of data, as in §5.6.2's hero pair, this effect is negligible; with one year, it can decide the outcome.

🧭 Margin note

Which variable carries the noise is rarely obvious in real data — both legs of a real pair usually have their own idiosyncratic moves. The lesson is not "know which one is noisy," it is "don't trust a short sample's verdict on direction," which is one more reason Johansen's symmetric treatment (next) is worth the extra machinery once you have more than a quick pairwise check to make.

5.6.4 Beyond two series: the Johansen test

Engle-Granger tests one candidate combination of exactly two series at a time; the Johansen test finds every combination of any number of series at once, and treats them all symmetrically.

The Johansen test works in a vector-autoregression framework — the natural multivariate extension of the AR(1)/ARMA models Session 5.4 introduced for a single series — and asks a sharper question than Engle-Granger: not "is this one combination stationary?" but "how many independent stationary combinations exist among these n series, and what are they?" That count is the cointegrating rank. With three series that share exactly one common trend, the rank is n − (shared trends) = 3 − 1 = 2: two independent stationary combinations, found in a single pass, with every series treated the same way — no asymmetric choice of which variable goes on the left.

The mechanics run through the eigenvalues of a reduced-rank regression; you do not need the derivation to use the test, only its two common forms, both testing H0H_0: the rank is at most rr, against a general alternative. The trace statistic sums the evidence across all the remaining directions; the max-eigenvalue statistic looks at only the next one, so the two can occasionally disagree at the margin — when they do, most practitioners lean on trace, which is the more commonly reported of the two and the one used throughout this section. Both also need a choice of deterministic term (a constant with no trend fits this session's construction, since the shared factor is a driftless random walk, not a trending one) and a lag order for the underlying vector autoregression, the same kind of specification choice Session 5.4 makes for a single-series AR or ARMA model, just generalised to several series moving together. Built three series from one shared trend — two carrying their own OU wobble, one a pure trend-follower — and statsmodels' trace test gives, at rank 0, a statistic of 54.3 against a 95% critical value of 29.8 (reject); at rank 1, 23.9 against 15.5 (reject); at rank 2, 0.4 against 3.8 (fail to reject). The test stops rejecting at rank 2 — exactly the true rank.

PropertyEngle-GrangerJohansen

That last row is not a footnote. Repeating the three-series construction 150 times, the trace test recovers the exact true rank of 2 in only 65% of replications — most of the misses over-count the rank, a known finite-sample property of the trace test, not a bug in this simulation. Treat a single Johansen run as informative, not infallible, and prefer it for its symmetry and its ability to see the whole structure, not for pinpoint precision on the rank.

5.6.5 The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and its half-life

The spread that cointegration hands you needs its own model — the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is the standard one, and its half-life is the single number that says how fast it reverts.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is the continuous-time version of the AR(1) model from Session 5.4: it pulls back toward a long-run mean θ\theta at a speed κ\kappa, plus noise,

dSt=κ(θ−St)dt+σdWtdS_t = \kappa(\theta - S_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t

Discretised at one period at a time, this is exactly an AR(1): St=θ(1−ϕ)+ϕSt−1+εtS_t = \theta(1-\phi) + \phi S_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, with ϕ=e−κΔt\phi = e^{-\kappa \Delta t}. Fit ϕ\phi by the same OLS you used in §5.6.2 and invert it: κ=−ln⁡(ϕ)\kappa = -\ln(\phi). The half-life of mean reversion is the time for half of any deviation from θ\theta to decay away,

half-life=ln⁡2κ\text{half-life} = \frac{\ln 2}{\kappa}

and it is the number practitioners actually quote, because "half-life of 20 trading days" is far more intuitive than a decay-rate parameter. Every half-life, whatever the starting gap, the remaining gap halves again — 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, and so on — it never fully closes, it just keeps getting small.

This is the same process Vasicek (1977) used to model the short-term interest rate, decades before it became the standard tool for a pairs-trading spread — a process does not care whether the quantity mean-reverting is a rate or a price gap, only that it has a pull toward some level and a source of noise around it. The three parameters have plain readings: θ\theta is the equilibrium the spread is pulled toward (often close to zero, if you have already subtracted off the intercept from step 1); σ\sigma sets how noisy the wobble is around that equilibrium, independent of how fast it reverts; and κ\kappa — equivalently, the half-life — sets the clock speed. Two spreads can share the same σ\sigma and look equally choppy on a chart while reverting at completely different speeds, which is exactly why you fit κ\kappa rather than eyeball the noise.

Fit this estimator on simulated OU paths at three realistic half-lives and it recovers the truth closely: a true 5-day half-life is estimated at 4.9 days, 10 at 9.8, 20 at 18.9 — each within a few percent, from 5 years of daily data and averaged over 400 fits. Push it to a slow, 60-day half-life on the same five years of data — only 5 × 252 / 60 ≈ 21 half-lives of history instead of dozens — and the estimator comes in measurably short: 52.3 days against a truth of 60, a bias of about 13%. This is not a coding error; it is the well-documented small-sample downward bias of the AR(1) coefficient estimator (Kendall, 1954; Marriott & Pope, 1954). The intuition: OLS fits ϕ\phi to whichever sample path you happened to draw, and a path that reverts strongly by chance pulls the fitted line toward less persistence, while a path that happens to wander further before reverting pulls it only a little the other way — the two errors do not average out symmetrically, so the estimate is tilted low on average. It always pushes the estimated half-life short — making a slow spread look faster, and more tradeable, than it really is.

5.6.6 In code: the full pipeline

Chain the three pieces together and you have the machinery this session hands to Session 8.3 — Pairs Trading & Statistical Arbitrage: recover a hedge ratio, recover a spread, recover a half-life.

# step 1-2: Engle-Granger, exactly as in section 2
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, coint

ols = sm.OLS(a, sm.add_constant(b)).fit()
alpha_hat, h_hat = ols.params
resid = ols.resid                              # this IS the spread
adf_stat, adf_p, *_ = adfuller(resid, autolag="AIC")

# section 4: Johansen, for N >= 2 series at once
from statsmodels.tsa.vector_ar.vecm import coint_johansen
j = coint_johansen(Y, det_order=0, k_ar_diff=1)
rank_hat = (j.lr1 > j.cvt[:, 1]).sum()          # trace stat vs 95% critical values

# section 5: half-life, an AR(1) fit on the spread
phi_hat = np.cov(resid[:-1], resid[1:])[0, 1] / resid[:-1].var(ddof=1)
half_life = np.log(2) / -np.log(phi_hat)

Running this whole pipeline — estimate the hedge ratio, form the spread, fit its half-life — 300 times on fresh 5-year samples recovers a hedge ratio of 1.503 ± 0.009 (true 1.5) and a half-life of 18.1 ± 0.2 trading days (true 20) — the same small AR(1) bias as §5.6.5, only slightly larger, since the spread now comes from an estimated hedge ratio rather than the true one. Estimating the hedge ratio first does not fix the bias in the half-life that follows it; it inherits it. Notice too what the pipeline does not need: no risk-free rate, no P&L, no Sharpe ratio anywhere in it. Everything here is about recovering the parameters of a data-generating process — a hedge ratio, a rank, a mean-reversion speed — and it is Session 8.3 — Pairs Trading & Statistical Arbitrage's job to turn those parameters into an actual trading rule with entries, exits and a position size.

5.6.7 Where people go wrong

The single most common mistake is stopping at step 1: an eye-catching R² and a plausible-looking scatterplot, no second step.

⚠️ Pitfall — "a high R² means they're related"

Two independent random walks routinely produce an OLS R² of 0.25 on average, and over 16% of the time above 0.5 — with nothing whatsoever connecting them (§5.6.3). A high R² is not evidence of cointegration; only a rejected unit root in the residual is. This is the entire reason Engle-Granger has a second step, and it is the first thing a fact-checker (or a live P&L) will ask whether you actually ran.

Two smaller pitfalls compound this one. First, don't stop at Engle-Granger once you have more than a pair, or need the full set of relationships — §5.6.4 showed its answer can depend on which variable you happened to put on the left. Second, don't trust a single half-life point estimate on a short sample without asking how many half-lives of history actually support it — §5.6.5's 60-day case was biased by more than ten percent on five years of daily data, and real markets rarely hand you a stable relationship for the decades a slow half-life would need to estimate cleanly.

5.6.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Cointegration — a property of two or more individually non-stationary series: at least one specific linear combination of them is stationary.
Engle-Granger test — the two-step test for cointegration between exactly two series: OLS regress one on the other, then test the residual for a unit root.
Johansen test — a vector-autoregression-based test that finds the full cointegrating rank and every cointegrating vector among two or more series at once.
Hedge ratio — the weight h in a cointegrating combination A − h·B that makes the combination stationary.
Spread — the stationary combination itself, A − h·B; the mean-reverting series a pairs trade is built around.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — the continuous-time mean-reverting process dSt=κ(θ−St)dt+σdWtdS_t = \kappa(\theta - S_t)dt + \sigma dW_t; the continuous analogue of an AR(1).
Half-life of mean reversion — the time for half of any deviation from the long-run mean to decay away, ln⁡(2)/κ\ln(2)/\kappa.

Check yourself

1. Two ETFs have a 90-day return correlation of 0.95. Are they cointegrated?
Not necessarily. Correlation measures co-movement of changes over a window; cointegration is about levels sharing a common trend, so one specific linear combination never wanders far. You would need to run the Engle-Granger or Johansen test on the price levels and check that the residual (or combination) is stationary — high return correlation is neither necessary nor sufficient for that (§5.6.1).
2. You regress A on B and the residual fails to reject a unit root at 5%. A colleague says "let's just flip it and regress B on A." What should you check first?
Whether the sample is short or the signal weak enough that direction is doing the deciding, rather than the truth (§5.6.3's asymmetry result). Flipping the regression and taking whichever answer you prefer is p-hacking with extra steps; better options are a longer sample, or the Johansen test, which treats both series symmetrically and does not require a side to be picked.
3. You have five ETFs you suspect share two common macro factors. Which test tells you how many independent cointegrating relationships exist, and gives you all of them at once?
Johansen. Engle-Granger only ever tests one specific two-series combination at a time and has no natural way to report a rank above 1; Johansen's trace (or max-eigenvalue) test estimates the full cointegrating rank and every eigenvector in a single pass, treating all five series jointly (§5.6.4).
4. A junior researcher shows you two random walks with an OLS R² of 0.82 and calls them "clearly cointegrated." What's wrong?
Spurious regression (Granger & Newbold, 1974): unrelated random walks routinely produce a high R², so R² alone is not evidence of a stationary relationship. The correct next step is to test the residual of that regression (or the combination) for a unit root with Engle-Granger or Johansen — only a rejected unit root supports the claim (§5.6.3, §5.6.7).
5. One spread has an estimated half-life of 5 trading days, another 60. Which is more likely to be practically tradeable, and what's the catch with the 60-day one even if it's "statistically significant"?
The 5-day spread is the more practical candidate: it reverts fast enough to trade at a daily rebalance without the position sitting exposed for weeks. The 60-day spread is harder to trust even with a low p-value, because (a) the half-life estimate itself is measurably biased short in anything less than decades of data (§5.6.5), and (b) the "equilibrium" it depends on — the economic reason the two prices should track each other — has far more time to break before the spread reverts.
