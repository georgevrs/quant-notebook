Bayesian Thinking: Priors, Shrinkage & Updating

You will never know a fund's true Sharpe ratio — only a noisy guess built from a few years of history, and the shorter the history the noisier the guess. Bayesian thinking is a disciplined way to combine that guess with what you already know about the shelf of funds it came from. The core move: don't trust a noisy number at face value — shrink it toward what similar numbers usually turn out to be, by exactly as much as its own uncertainty deserves.

5.3.1 One estimate is never the whole story

A Sharpe ratio computed from a few years of daily returns carries a lot of sampling noise — and you already have information about what Sharpe ratios usually look like, before you ever see this one.

In Session 3.1 — Probability & Expected Value you met Bayes' rule as a way to turn "how likely is the data, given a hypothesis" into "how likely is the hypothesis, given the data." This session applies exactly that idea to a number every quant eventually has to answer for: is a strategy's Sharpe ratio real, or mostly luck? The machinery for doing this honestly has three pieces. The prior is what you believed about a quantity before seeing this particular piece of data — for a fund's true skill, a sensible prior is "most funds on this shelf cluster around a modest number, with only a few genuine standouts," the same instinct behind the base rate reasoning from Session 1.4 — The Engineer's Edge, Blind Spots & Honest Odds, made quantitative. The likelihood is how probable the observed data is for each possible value of the unknown quantity — here, how likely a given noisy Sharpe estimate is if the fund's true skill were some particular number. The posterior is the updated belief once you combine the two: literally prior × likelihood, rescaled to sum to one, which is Bayes' rule again, just applied to a whole distribution of possible skill levels instead of a single yes/no hypothesis.

Concretely: suppose the shelf of strategies you run has true annual Sharpe ratios that look Normal with mean 0.20 and standard deviation 0.30 — call these μ0\mu_0 and σ0\sigma_0 (reusing this course's letters for "a mean" and "a standard deviation," just applied to a cross-section of Sharpe ratios rather than to a return series). A particular fund reports an annualised Sharpe of yy, estimated from YY years of daily excess returns, with standard error ss (the standard error of the Sharpe ratio is derived in Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie; here we only need the number it produces). One particular simulated fund, with 3 years of history and — unknown to any analyst — a true Sharpe of 0.55, happens to report y=y = +0.15, with standard error s=s = 0.58. That standard error is enormous next to the estimate itself — three years of daily data barely constrains an annual Sharpe ratio at all.

📌 Key idea — precision, not vote-counting

Combining a prior and a likelihood is not "average the two numbers." It is a precision-weighted average: whichever source has the smaller variance (the tighter, more confident distribution) pulls the answer further toward itself. A fund with only a few years of data has a wide, imprecise likelihood, so the prior dominates; a fund with a long, calm history has a narrow likelihood and keeps most of its own signal.

5.3.2 How Bayesian updating works

The same five-step recipe handles every conjugate-prior problem in this session, whether the unknown is a Sharpe ratio or a win rate.

A conjugate prior is a prior family chosen so that, once combined with a particular likelihood, the posterior lands back in the same family — a Normal prior updated by Normal noise gives a Normal posterior; a Beta prior updated by Bernoulli trials gives a Beta posterior. That is the whole appeal: step 4 in fig 5.3.2 is arithmetic, not a numerical integration or an MCMC run. The table below covers the two conjugate pairs this session uses, plus a third you will recognise later.

The Beta-Binomial pair is worth one worked pass because it is the conjugate update you will reach for most often outside of means: any time the unknown is a probability. Suppose a new strategy's true (unobservable) win rate is 55%, and before seeing a single trade you hold a weakly informative prior Beta(4, 4) — centred at a coin flip, worth about 4 + 4 = 8 pseudo-trades of conviction. After 20 real trades with 14 wins, the raw hit rate is 70.0%, but the posterior mean — literally (a0+xa_0+x)/(a0+b0+na_0+b_0+n) — is only 64.3%: the prior's 8 pseudo-trades still count for a third of the total evidence at n=20n=20. Run the same strategy for 2,000 trades instead, and the prior's 8 pseudo-trades barely register.

💡 Looking ahead

Every later use of shrinkage in this course is this same conjugate Normal-Normal update wearing a different hat: Ledoit-Wolf covariance shrinkage in Session 9.2 — Covariance Estimation blends a noisy sample covariance matrix with a well-behaved target the same way you are about to blend a noisy Sharpe estimate with a shelf-wide average; Black-Litterman in Session 9.6 — Optimization with Constraints & Costs blends an investor's views with the market's implied returns; a Session 5.7 — Kalman Filters & Regime Models Kalman filter runs this exact update every single time step, on a hedge ratio instead of a Sharpe ratio.

5.3.3 Credible intervals vs confidence intervals

Two intervals from the same data, at the same 95% label, answer two different questions — and mixing them up is one of the most common statistics mistakes in finance.

The posterior from §5.3.1 is itself Normal, with mean and variance given by combining precisions:

μpost=μ0σ02+ys21σ02+1s2,1σpost2=1σ02+1s2\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\dfrac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \dfrac{y}{s^2}}{\dfrac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \dfrac{1}{s^2}}, \qquad \frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{post}}^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{1}{s^2}

which rearranges into the shrinkage form this whole session is built on — a weight k=σ02/(σ02+s2)k = \sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2+s^2), between 0 and 1, that the data keeps:

μpost=μ0+k(y−μ0)\mu_{\text{post}} = \mu_0 + k\,(y - \mu_0)

For the fund in §5.3.1, k=k = 21.3%: only about a fifth of the raw estimate's deviation from the shelf average survives, so the posterior mean is +0.19 — pulled 79% of the way back to the prior. A credible interval is then the posterior's own central range: +0.189 ±\pm 1.96 ×\times 0.266 gives −0.33 to +0.71 — a direct statement that, if the prior is right, there is a 95% chance the fund's true Sharpe lies in that range. The plain 95% confidence interval on the raw estimate, y±1.96sy \pm 1.96s, is −0.98 to +1.28 — 46% wider, because it borrows no information from the shelf at all.

The subtlety practitioners trip over: a 95% confidence interval's guarantee is unconditional — averaged over every sample you might have drawn, before you saw which fund you'd be asked about. It says nothing about THIS particular interval once you have already picked this fund out for looking impressive (§5.3.6 makes that concrete with real numbers). A correctly-specified credible interval, conditional on the stated prior, is the number people actually want when they ask "how sure are we" — but it is only as honest as the prior behind it, which is exactly what empirical Bayes (§5.3.4) tries to make defensible: estimate that prior from data instead of asserting it.

🧭 Margin note — a known cost of "empirical" Bayes

Estimating μ0\mu_0 and σ0\sigma_0 from the same cross-section you are shrinking (§5.3.4) is called empirical Bayes, and it works well in practice, but it quietly treats the estimated σ0\sigma_0 as if it were known for certain — understating uncertainty a little. Across the 200-fund experiment in §5.3.4, the resulting credible intervals cover the true Sharpe about 91.5% of the time, not quite the nominal 95%. A fully Bayesian treatment fixes this with a hyperprior on σ0\sigma_0 itself, at the cost of losing the closed form — a refinement this course does not need for what follows, but worth knowing exists (Morey et al., 2016, make the general point that "95%" is easy to overclaim either way).

5.3.4 Shrinkage across a shelf of funds: empirical Bayes

With one fund, you have to guess a prior. With many funds drawn from the same shelf, the shelf itself tells you the prior — and shrinking every estimate by it beats trusting each one at face value.

Shrinkage is the general move of pulling a noisy estimate part-way toward a more stable target to lower its expected error — and empirical Bayes is the specific trick of estimating that target, and how hard to pull, directly from the same batch of noisy estimates, using each one's own known noise level. James and Stein (1961) proved something startling about this: once you are estimating three or more unrelated-looking quantities at once, shrinking every one of them toward their common average — even quantities that have nothing to do with each other, like a batter's average and a bond's default rate — lowers the *total* squared error, even though no individual quantity is helped by data from the others. Efron and Morris's (1977) illustration is now a classic: shrinking 1970 batting averages toward the league mean cut total squared prediction error from 0.086 to 0.027 — about 0x lower — using nothing but the 18 players' own early-season numbers pulled toward each other. A fund's Sharpe ratio is exactly this kind of quantity: it looks like a fact about one fund, but a shelf of them shares a common, estimable backdrop.

The lab draws N=N= 200 synthetic funds' true annual Sharpe ratios from the same shelf as §5.3.1, Normal(μ0=\mu_0=0.20, σ0=\sigma_0=0.30) — loosely motivated by the finding that most mutual funds show no true skill once luck is priced out (75.4% zero-alpha, versus only 0.6% genuinely skilled, per Barras, Scaillet & Wermers, 2010): a realistic shelf should put most of its mass near a modest number, not assume every fund is equally likely to be a star. Every fund shares the same 15% annual volatility and a risk-free rate of 0, so only track-record length and luck can explain what follows: 50 funds each have 2, 3, 5 and 10 years of daily history. Since we generated the data, we know each fund's true θi\theta_i — which lets us score the estimators honestly, something no real analyst ever gets to do.

Instead of assuming μ0\mu_0 and σ0\sigma_0 are known, empirical Bayes estimates them from the 200 estimates themselves — the grand mean, and the cross-sectional variance left over once each fund's own sampling noise is subtracted out:

μˆ0=1N∑iyi,σˆ02=max⁡(0, sy2−1N∑isi2)\hat\mu_0 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i y_i, \qquad \hat\sigma_0^2 = \max\!\Big(0,\ s_y^2 - \frac{1}{N}\sum_i s_i^2\Big)

where sy2s_y^2 is the plain sample variance of the 200 estimates and sis_i is fund ii's own standard error. In this run, σˆ0=\hat\sigma_0 = 0.293, close to the true 0.30. Every fund is then shrunk by its own weight ki=σˆ02/(σˆ02+si2)k_i = \hat\sigma_0^2/(\hat\sigma_0^2+s_i^2) — and because sis_i shrinks with more years of data, the weight rises with track record:

Track recordStandard error of the Sharpe estimateWeight kept on this fund's own data
2 yearslarge — barely more than noise12%
3 yearsstill large17%
5 yearsmoderate25%
10 yearssmallest here, but still substantial40%

Even a decade of daily returns keeps under half the weight — a blunt reminder of how slowly Sharpe-ratio noise decays (§5.2's standard error of the Sharpe ratio shows why: it falls only with the square root of years). Scored against the true θi\theta_i, the shrunk estimates cut mean squared error from 0.282 to 0.068 — 76% lower. That comparison needs the (synthetic) truth, which real analysts never have; the realistic version scores each fund's shrunk and raw estimates against its OWN next 3 years of live performance instead, and shrinkage still wins, cutting mean squared error by 35%.

📌 Key idea — shrinkage helps on average, not case by case

Shrinkage lowers total error across the shelf; it is not a promise about any one fund. The fund in §5.3.1 happened to have an unusually high true Sharpe (0.55), and its shrunk estimate (+0.19) undersells it badly — an occasional, unavoidable cost. James-Stein shrinkage is a statement about the whole portfolio of estimates, exactly like a portfolio's risk is a statement about the whole book, not any one position.

5.3.5 In code

The empirical-Bayes step is short enough to hold in your head: two summary statistics from the cross-section, then one line per fund.

# estimate the shelf's mean and spread from the cross-section itself
mu0_hat = float(sr_hat.mean())
tau0_hat_sq = max(float(sr_hat.var(ddof=1)) - float(np.mean(se ** 2)), 0.0)

# shrink every fund by its own precision-weighted amount
k = tau0_hat_sq / (tau0_hat_sq + se ** 2)          # weight kept on this fund's own data
sr_shrunk = mu0_hat + k * (sr_hat - mu0_hat)
post_sd = np.sqrt(k * se ** 2)                     # for a credible interval per fund

Two details worth naming because they are easy to get backwards. First, se is computed per fund from standard error of the Sharpe ratio (already implemented; §5.2 derives it), not assumed equal across funds — that heterogeneity is exactly what makes the 2-year and 10-year funds shrink by different amounts. Second, the variance floor at 0.0 matters: with a noisy cross-section, the raw method-of-moments estimate of σˆ02\hat\sigma_0^2 can go slightly negative (the average sampling noise can, by chance, exceed the observed spread of estimates), which is nonsense for a variance — clip it, or every fund's credible interval silently gets a negative width.

The Beta-Binomial update from §5.3.2 is one line shorter, because Beta pseudo-counts add directly (here a0=b0=4a_0=b_0=4, 20 trades, x=14x=14 wins, matching §5.3.2's numbers):

# posterior after observing k_example wins out of N_TRADES trades
post_a, post_b = A0 + k_example, B0 + (N_TRADES - k_example)
post_mean_beta = post_a / (post_a + post_b)

5.3.6 Where people go wrong: the leaderboard illusion

Ranking funds — or backtested strategies — by their raw trailing Sharpe ratio and trusting the top of the list is one of the most common, and most expensive, mistakes in manager selection.

The 200-fund experiment in §5.3.4 makes this concrete. Among the top 10% of funds by raw Sharpe, 39% are from the shortest (2-year) group — far above the 25% you would expect if track-record length made no difference. Shrinking first drops that share to 11%: it specifically un-rewards the estimates most likely to be noise pretending to be skill. The pay-off shows up in the funds actually selected: the raw top-decile's average TRUE skill is only 0.447, versus 0.483 for the shrunk top-decile — a real, if modest, improvement in what you would have actually picked.

The interval story from §5.3.3 gets worse, not better, once you select on the estimate. Restricted to that same top decile, the plain 95% confidence interval — perfectly valid on average, across all 200 funds — covers the true Sharpe only 81% of the time, versus 95% unconditionally. The 95% credible interval, because it already discounted the noisiest estimates before anyone went looking for winners, holds up better among the same selected funds: 91%.

SituationChooseWhy
A fund with a decade of calm historytrust it mostlyat 10 years it still keeps only 40% weight on its own number — light shrinkage, not none
A 2-year fund with a dazzling Sharpeshrink hard before actingits standard error dwarfs the shelf's spread, so most of "dazzling" is noise
Ranking many funds or strategies to pick onerank by the shrunk estimateavoids rewarding exactly the funds most likely to be a lucky draw
⚠️ Pitfall — "the top of the leaderboard is the best fund"

Picking the single best-looking track record out of many candidates and taking its number at face value is a close cousin of the search bias Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO corrects for with the deflated Sharpe ratio when you have tried many BACKTESTS before finding this one. Here the funds already exist — you are not searching, just selecting — but the same regression-to-the-mean arithmetic applies: the estimate that looks best is, on average, the estimate that got the luckiest draw of noise, not the one with the most skill.

5.3.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Prior — what you believe about an unknown quantity before seeing this particular piece of data, expressed as a probability distribution.
Likelihood — how probable the observed data is, for each possible value of the unknown quantity.
Posterior — the updated belief after combining the prior and the likelihood: prior × likelihood, rescaled to sum to one.
Conjugate prior — a prior family that, combined with a given likelihood, yields a posterior in the same family, so updating is algebra rather than numerical integration.
Shrinkage — pulling a noisy estimate part-way toward a more stable target (often a shared average) to lower its expected squared error.
Credible interval — a range from the posterior distribution that is stated to contain the true value with a given posterior probability, conditional on the prior.
Empirical Bayes — estimating a prior's own parameters from the same batch of data it will be used to update, instead of assuming them.

Check yourself

1. Two analysts each estimate a strategy's Sharpe ratio at 0.9 from three years of daily returns. One works at a firm running 200 similar strategies with a well-documented shelf average around 0.2; the other is evaluating a solo strategy with no comparable peer group. Should they trust the 0.9 equally?
No. The first analyst has an estimable prior (the shelf) to shrink toward, and with only 3 years of data the standard error is large enough (§5.3.1: about 0.58 on a comparable fund) that most of "0.9" is likely noise — it should be pulled hard toward 0.2. The second analyst has no comparable shelf to borrow precision from, so the raw estimate — while still noisy — is the best available number; if anything, a weak, generic prior (e.g. "most strategies don't have huge edges") is still wiser than none.
2. Why does insisting on a conjugate prior make Bayesian updating practical, and what do you give up by insisting on one?
A conjugate prior turns "combine a prior and a likelihood" into closed-form algebra (fig 5.3.2, step 4) instead of a numerical integration or a sampling-based method like MCMC — that is the entire reason Beta-Binomial and Normal-Normal updates show up everywhere in quick, practical estimation. What you give up is fidelity: your true belief about a quantity might not have the exact shape of a Beta or a Normal, and forcing it into a convenient family is an approximation. For the mean-estimation problems this course cares about (Sharpe ratios, alphas, hedge ratios), that approximation is usually reasonable.
3. A colleague shows you a 95% confidence interval and a 95% credible interval for the same fund's Sharpe ratio, and they don't overlap much. Which one would you rather hand to a client deciding whether to allocate, and why?
Neither is automatically "more correct" — they answer different questions. The credible interval is the one that directly answers what a client actually wants ("how sure are we the true Sharpe is in this range, given everything we know"), but only if the prior behind it is defensible; a badly-chosen prior gives a confident, precise, wrong answer. An empirical-Bayes prior, estimated from a real shelf of comparable strategies (§5.3.4), is the most defensible version. If no credible shelf exists, the wider confidence interval is the more honest thing to show, precisely because it borrows nothing you cannot justify.
4. A manager-selection committee ranks 40 funds by trailing 2-year Sharpe ratio and interviews only the top 3. What is wrong with this filter, and what would you do instead?
Ranking by the raw trailing Sharpe rewards exactly the funds whose estimates are most likely to be inflated by noise — with only 2 years of data the standard error is large, so the top of any such leaderboard is disproportionately short-history funds that got lucky (§5.3.6: 39% of the top decile here, versus a fair 25%). The fix is to shrink every fund's estimate toward the peer group's average by an amount that reflects its own standard error, THEN rank — which is exactly what cut the true-skill gap and cut the short-history over-representation in the lab.
5. What does "empirical" mean in empirical Bayes, and where do the prior's numbers come from if you don't just assert them?
"Empirical" means the prior's own parameters (here, the shelf's mean and spread of true Sharpe ratios) are estimated from the same data you are about to update with, using a method like the sample mean and a noise-corrected sample variance (§5.3.4), rather than being asserted from outside knowledge or a textbook default. It is a practical compromise: not fully Bayesian (a genuine hyperprior would treat those parameters as uncertain too, §5.3.3's margin note), but far more defensible than guessing.
