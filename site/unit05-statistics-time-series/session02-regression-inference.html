Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie

A regression hands you a slope and a p-value whether or not you earned them. The textbook standard error assumes independent errors, one test, and no memory in the data — finance routinely breaks all three, always in the direction of making you more confident than you should be.

5.2.1 Why a p-value can lie

Session 5.1 — Stylized Facts of Returns catalogued the regularities of returns — fat tails, clustered volatility, near-zero autocorrelation in the level of returns. This session is about what those regularities do to the arithmetic you use to judge a result.

An ordinary least squares regression fits a line and, alongside it, a promise: "the slope is this far from zero, and here is how sure you can be." That promise is a p-value — the probability, if the true effect were exactly zero, of seeing a result at least this extreme by chance alone. The promise is only as good as three assumptions baked into the textbook formula: the errors are independent of each other, each row of data is genuinely new information, and you asked the question once. Financial research routinely violates all three — a stale month-end price echoes into the next day's return, a "21-day forward return" reuses 20 of yesterday's days, and nobody backtests a strategy exactly once. Every one of these violations makes the reported standard error smaller than the truth, never larger, which means the p-value looks better than it should, never worse. That asymmetry is the reason this session exists: undiscovered edges do not disappear when you fix the arithmetic, but a great many discovered ones do.

📌 Key idea — the bias only runs one way

None of the three problems above ever make a result look less significant than it truly is. A naive standard error is a floor you fall through, not a safety margin. Session 1.4 — The Engineer's Edge, Blind Spots & Honest Odds already showed that a one-year Sharpe ratio has a standard error of about 1 — this session shows how much worse that gets once you account for autocorrelation, overlap and search.

5.2.2 Ordinary least squares, properly standard-erred

The mechanics are five minutes of linear algebra. Getting the standard error right is the part of the job that actually takes judgement.

Say a strategy's daily excess returns rtr_t move with a benchmark's excess returns ftf_t: rt=α+βft+εtr_t = \alpha + \beta f_t + \varepsilon_t. Ordinary least squares picks the αˆ\hat\alpha and βˆ\hat\beta that minimise the sum of squared residuals, αˆ,βˆ=arg mina,b⁡∑t(rt−a−bft)2\hat\alpha,\hat\beta = \argmin_{a,b}\sum_t (r_t - a - b f_t)^2 — a closed-form calculation, not a search. Here α\alpha is the part of the return the benchmark doesn't explain and β\beta is the exposure to it (both named and owned by Session 1.2 — Where Returns Come From); this session is not about which benchmark belongs on the right-hand side — Session 7.2 — CAPM, APT & Factor Regressions builds the full CAPM/APT machinery for that — it is about whether you can trust the αˆ\hat\alpha and βˆ\hat\beta a regression like this hands you.

Once you have βˆ\hat\beta you need its standard error to say anything about confidence. A t-statistic is the estimate divided by its standard error, t=βˆ/SE(βˆ)t = \hat\beta / \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta); a p-value converts tt into "how surprising is this, if the true β\beta were zero" using the t-distribution. Every one of those steps is arithmetic — the judgement is entirely in which formula computes SE(βˆ)\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta), because the "classical" textbook formula is only correct when the residuals εt\varepsilon_t are independent and identically distributed.

5.2.3 HAC, Newey-West & overlapping observations

One formula fixes both autocorrelation and volatility clustering — the trick is knowing how far to trust it.

When residuals are autocorrelated, the sample size TT overstates how much independent information you have. HAC standard errors ("heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent") replace the classical variance with one that adds in the covariance between an error and its neighbours, weighted so the estimate stays valid. Newey & West (1987) gave the standard construction, the Newey-West estimator: weight the sample autocorrelations ρˆk\hat\rho_k of the residuals with a Bartlett kernel (weights that taper linearly to zero) out to a chosen number of lags LL, so that the reported variance is inflated by

Ψ=1+2∑k=1L(1−kL+1)ρˆk\Psi = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(1-\frac{k}{L+1}\right)\hat\rho_k

relative to the classical formula — statsmodels computes the full matrix sandwich for a regression with several regressors, but for a single slope this Ψ\Psi is exactly the factor involved: SEHAC(βˆ)≈Ψ⋅SEclassical(βˆ)\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\hat\beta) \approx \sqrt{\Psi}\cdot \mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{classical}}(\hat\beta). Set L=0L=0 and Ψ=1\Psi=1: HAC reduces to the classical formula, which is why it is always safe to prefer HAC when you are unsure — it costs some precision, never correctness.

Overlapping observations are the cleanest way to see why this matters. Build a kk-day return by summing kk consecutive daily returns and rolling the window forward one day at a time — exactly what a "21-day forward return" regression, or a monthly-rebalanced backtest measured daily, does. Two windows that are k−1k-1 days apart share all but one observation, which manufactures autocorrelation out of thin air even when the underlying daily shocks are perfectly independent: the classical formula counts each overlapping window as a fresh, independent data point, when in truth roughly one in every kk is new. The companion lab regresses 2121-day overlapping returns on a synthetic factor with a known true β=\beta =0.8 across 300 independent five-year samples, so the TRUE sampling spread of βˆ\hat\beta is known exactly (the empirical standard deviation of βˆ\hat\beta across samples) rather than guessed at.

EstimatorReported SE of βShare of the true sampling spread
Naive OLS (classical)0.04527%
HAC, Newey-West, 20 lags (k−1)0.13983%
Empirical (Monte Carlo truth)0.167100%

The naive standard error is not "a bit optimistic" — it reports barely a quarter of the true uncertainty, which would make a perfectly ordinary sampling fluctuation look like a t-statistic of nearly 4. HAC with the textbook rule of thumb of k−1k-1 lags recovers most, but honestly not all, of the gap: more lags trade bias for variance, since each additional ρˆk\hat\rho_k is itself estimated with noise, so an over-long window can make the correction worse rather than better.

🧭 Margin note — how many lags?

Two rules of thumb, both defensible: if you built the overlap yourself (a kk-day window), use at least k−1k-1 lags — the source of the autocorrelation is known exactly (Hansen & Hodrick, 1980, used this reasoning first, before Newey-West's kernel made the resulting covariance matrix reliably valid). If you don't know the source, let the data choose: statsmodels defaults to L=⌊4(T/100)2/9⌋L = \lfloor 4(T/100)^{2/9}\rfloor lags (Newey & West, 1994) when none is given. Never tune LL until a result looks significant — that swaps one honesty problem for another.

5.2.4 The standard error of the Sharpe ratio

The same long-run-variance idea that fixes a regression's standard error fixes the Sharpe ratio's — because underneath, they are the same problem.

Session 1.4 — The Engineer's Edge, Blind Spots & Honest Odds and Session 2.3 — Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage used SE(SR)≈1/Y\mathrm{SE}(\SR) \approx 1/\sqrt{Y} for YY years of daily data. That is the small-SR\SR special case of Lo (2002)'s exact i.i.d. result for the standard error of the Sharpe ratio, on the per-period Sharpe ratio SRp\SR_p estimated from TT periods:

SE(SRp)=1+SRp2/2T\mathrm{SE}(\SR_p) = \sqrt{\frac{1+\SR_p^2/2}{T}}

(implemented as quantnb.stats.sharpe_se). This assumes returns are independent — exactly the assumption §5.2.1–§5.2.3 spent three sections breaking. When returns are autocorrelated, the sample mean and sample variance that make up SRp\SR_p are both estimated from fewer independent observations than TT suggests, by precisely the Ψ\Psi inflation of §5.2.3. To first order,

SEHAC(SRp)≈Ψ⋅SE(SRp)\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\SR_p) \approx \sqrt{\Psi}\cdot \mathrm{SE}(\SR_p)

(a fuller treatment also accounts for the autocorrelation of squared returns — volatility clustering — which Lo, 2002, folds into the same GMM derivation; the Ψ\Psi term above is the dominant piece and the one worth carrying by hand). The same Ψ\Psi, applied to aggregating rather than estimating, gives the honest way to annualise a Sharpe ratio computed on autocorrelated returns: a qq-period Sharpe ratio is SR(q)=qSR(1)/Ψ(q)\SR(q) = \sqrt{q}\,\SR(1)/\sqrt{\Psi(q)}, not the naive qSR(1)\sqrt{q}\,\SR(1) — the reason a fund with smoothed, stale month-end marks can report a Sharpe ratio that partly reflects illiquidity rather than skill (Getmansky, Lo & Makarov, 2004).

The lab checks this on a synthetic three-year track record with a modest, known autocorrelation of ρ=\rho=0.15 in daily returns — the kind produced by month-end-marked or lightly smoothed pricing — and a true annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.8. Over 2,000 independent samples, Ψ=\Psi=1.352 (its large-sample limit for an AR(1) process, (1+ρ)/(1−ρ)(1+\rho)/(1-\rho), is 1.353 — indistinguishable here, since three years is long relative to how fast the autocorrelation decays):

EstimatorReported SE of the annualised Sharpe ratioShare of the true sampling spread
Naive (i.i.d., Lo 2002)0.57887%
Autocorrelation-corrected (×√Ψ)0.672101%
Empirical (Monte Carlo truth)0.666100%

Even this mild, entirely plausible amount of autocorrelation understates the true uncertainty by over ten percentage points if you use the textbook i.i.d. formula unmodified. A fund whose reported Sharpe ratio owes something to smoothed marks looks both better and more certain than it is — the two errors compound.

5.2.5 In code

Four lines get you from returns to a defensible standard error — the same four lines run in the companion lab.

# classical OLS, then a HAC-robust refit — same fit, different covariance
import statsmodels.api as sm
ols = sm.OLS(strategy_excess, sm.add_constant(factor_excess)).fit()
hac = ols.get_robustcov_results(cov_type="HAC", maxlags=L)

On the lab's ten-year, non-overlapping factor model, βˆ=\hat\beta=1.17 with a classical standard error of 0.031 and a HAC standard error of 0.031 — the two agree closely, exactly as theory predicts when the underlying shocks really are independent (t=t=37.9, so the exposure is not in doubt). The annualised alpha is a different story: αˆ=\hat\alpha=−2.75% against a true value of 2%, with a standard error of 7.87% (p=p=0.73) — even ten years of daily data barely pins down alpha, which is why the estimate can land on the wrong side of zero without anything being wrong. This is the same lesson Session 2.3 — Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage drew about mean returns, applied to a regression intercept instead of a raw average.

White's SPA test and Hansen's model confidence set are one line each once the losses are laid out as a matrix, one column per candidate:

# benchmark and models are arrays of LOSSES (lower is better) — negative excess return here
from arch.bootstrap import SPA, MCS
spa = SPA(benchmark_loss, model_losses, reps=500).compute()
mcs = MCS(model_losses, size=0.10, reps=500).compute()

Neither test is reimplemented here — both come straight from `arch.bootstrap`, built on the stationary bootstrap of Politis & Romano (1994). §5.2.6 puts them to work on a pile of noise.

5.2.6 Multiple testing: FWER, FDR, SPA & the model confidence set

Test one idea and a 5% threshold means what it says. Test two hundred and it doesn't.

Multiple testing is the general name for the problem: the more hypotheses you test, the more likely at least one clears a fixed bar by chance. The family-wise error rate (FWER) is the probability of at least one false positive among all the tests; for NN independent tests each run at size ss, FWER=1−(1−s)N\mathrm{FWER} = 1-(1-s)^N — with N=N=200 strategies tested at a size of 5%, that is 100.0%: a false positive is nearly certain, not a risk. The classical fix is the Bonferroni correction — test each hypothesis at size s/Ns/N instead of ss — which controls FWER but is famously conservative once NN is large.

The false discovery rate (FDR) asks a gentler question: not "what is the chance of any false positive," but "of the results I call discoveries, what share are false?" The Benjamini & Hochberg (1995) procedure ranks the NN p-values p(1)≤⋯≤p(N)p_{(1)} \le \cdots \le p_{(N)} and finds the largest ii with p(i)≤(i/N)⋅sp_{(i)} \le (i/N)\cdot s; every hypothesis up to that rank is called a discovery. FDR control has more power than Bonferroni — it accepts a controlled rate of false positives among what it flags, which is the right trade when a false lead costs you a week of research rather than a fund's capital.

The lab draws 200 pure-noise return series — zero true skill, by construction — over 5 years, and tests each at a size of 5%. 9 clear the bar (close to the 10 a 5% size predicts — the test is working exactly as advertised for any one of them). Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg both correctly call 0 and 0 discoveries: neither procedure is fooled. The single best trial has p=p=0.0044 and an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.27 — a number that would look outstanding in a pitch deck — and it is pure luck: the maximum Sharpe ratio among 200 skill-less trials matches Session 1.4 — The Engineer's Edge, Blind Spots & Honest Odds's expected-maximum calculation almost exactly (1.24 predicted).

Bonferroni and FDR both need you to test every candidate at the same threshold, which is awkward when what you actually want to ask is narrower: does the single best strategy I found really beat a benchmark, once I account for having looked at all the others? That is White's SPA test ("reality check for data snooping," White, 2000; refined by Hansen, 2005, into the version the lab uses) — a bootstrap test of whether the best of many candidates truly beats a benchmark, rather than merely looking best by chance. Run on the same 200 noise trials against a zero benchmark, its p-value is 0.33 — correctly failing to reject "none of these really beats doing nothing," even though the best trial's own p-value was 0.0044. Hansen's model confidence set (Hansen, Lunde & Nason, 2011) asks a related question for a whole group of candidates at once: which ones can you not statistically tell apart from the best? On the same data, at a target confidence of 10%, it keeps 200 of 200 models and excludes 0 — an honest admission that the data cannot crown a winner, where picking "the best" by eye would have crowned one anyway.

ProcedureControlsAnswers
BonferroniFamily-wise error rate, at a chosen size"Does this p-value survive N tests?"

⚠️ Pitfall — "it backtested great, so it must be real"

Nobody writes down every parameter combination, data window and filter they tried before landing on the one they show you — which means nobody can compute an honest NN after the fact. Pre-register how many variants you will test before you test them, keep every result (not just the survivors), and treat a single great-looking backtest chosen from an unrecorded search as having no p-value at all, however good its own one looks.

5.2.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Ordinary least squares — the estimator that picks the intercept and slope minimising the sum of squared residuals.
t-statistic — an estimate divided by its standard error; how many standard errors it sits from zero.
p-value — the probability of a result at least this extreme if the true effect were zero.
HAC standard errors — standard errors that stay valid when errors are autocorrelated and heteroskedastic.
Newey-West estimator — the standard HAC construction: a Bartlett-weighted sum of sample autocovariances out to a chosen lag.
Overlapping observations — rolling k-period windows that share data with their neighbours, manufacturing autocorrelation.
Standard error of the Sharpe ratio — Lo's (1+SRp2/2)/T\sqrt{(1+SR_p^2/2)/T} under independence, inflated by Ψ under autocorrelation.
Multiple testing — the inflation of false-positive risk that comes from testing more than one hypothesis on the same data.
Family-wise error rate — the probability of at least one false positive among all tests run.
False discovery rate — the expected share of false positives among the results called discoveries.
SPA test — White's bootstrap test of whether the best of many candidates truly beats a benchmark.
Model confidence set — Hansen's procedure for finding the set of models statistically indistinguishable from the best.

Check yourself

1. A colleague reports a regression p-value of 0.03 on daily strategy returns and calls it significant. What's the first question to ask?
Are the residuals actually independent — or is this built from overlapping or autocorrelated returns? If so, the classical p-value understates the true uncertainty and 0.03 may not survive a HAC refit.
2. Why does building a longer overlap window (bigger k) make the naive OLS standard error worse, not better?
More overlap means each new observation shares more of its information with its neighbours, so the number of genuinely independent data points grows much slower than the raw row count. The classical formula only counts rows, so its understatement of the true standard error gets worse as k grows.
3. A three-year track record has an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.8 and mild positive autocorrelation from month-end-marked positions (ρ ≈ 0.15). Is its true uncertainty bigger or smaller than the textbook i.i.d. formula says?
Bigger — the lab found the honest standard error is about √Ψ ≈ 1.16 times the naive one at this ρ. Smoothed or stale marks make a Sharpe ratio look both better and more certain than it is; the two errors compound.
4. You tested 200 parameter combinations and the best one has p = 0.0044. Is that strategy real?
You cannot tell from that p-value alone. With 200 independent tests at a 5% size, the family-wise error rate is essentially 100% — a false positive is nearly guaranteed. Run White's SPA test or Hansen's MCS on the full set; in the lab, the identical data gave an SPA p-value of 0.33 — not significant, once the search is accounted for.
5. What's the practical difference between controlling the family-wise error rate (Bonferroni) and the false discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg)?
FWER guards against ANY false positive among all tests — conservative, appropriate when a single wrong call is costly. FDR tolerates a controlled proportion of false positives among the results you flag — more power, appropriate for a first screen among many candidates where a false lead just costs a closer look.
6. HAC with more lags sounds strictly safer than fewer. Why isn't it?
Each additional lag adds another sample autocorrelation that must itself be estimated, and that estimate gets noisier the further out you go. Too many lags trade a smaller bias for a larger variance in the covariance estimate — which is why even the "correct" k−1 lags in §5.2.3 recovered most, not all, of the true standard error.
