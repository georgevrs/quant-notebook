Unit 5 · Statistics & Time Series of Returns — Session 05 of 07

Modeling Volatility

Returns are close to unpredictable, but their volatility is not: it clusters, it decays at a knowable rate, and a handful of formulas measure it far more precisely than a plain close-to-close estimate. The core move: use the whole bar — open, high, low, close — and let a fitted model, not a fixed rule of thumb, say how fast the world is actually changing.

5.5.1 Why volatility is worth forecasting

Sessions 5.2 and 5.4 showed how little of tomorrow's return you can predict; this session shows how much of tomorrow's risk you can.

Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie and Session 5.4 — Stationarity & ARMA Models spent real effort showing how little of tomorrow's return you can predict: autocorrelation in raw returns is close to zero, and a good forecast of next week's price is barely better than today's price. Volatility — the size of the moves, not their direction — behaves completely differently: a big move today makes a big move tomorrow more likely, whichever way prices go next. In one of this session's own simulated series (§5.5.6's GARCH process, 2,500 trading days), the lag-1 autocorrelation of raw returns is +0.021 — indistinguishable from noise — while the lag-1 autocorrelation of squared returns is +0.153, seven times larger. Tomorrow's sign is close to a coin flip; tomorrow's typical move size is not, and that gap is what the rest of this session measures and forecasts.

This matters because volatility feeds almost everything downstream of a forecast: position sizing, margin, options prices and every risk limit a desk runs. A strategy that cannot predict tomorrow's direction can still size its bet correctly if it knows tomorrow's likely range — halve the position when volatility doubles, and the dollar risk barely moves. Three different tools do three different jobs here (fig 5.5.1): measuring what already happened as precisely as possible, from a single day's own bar (realized volatility), and forecasting tomorrow's from a history of those measurements — a fixed rule (EWMA volatility), a fitted GARCH model, or, when bad news should move volatility more than equally-sized good news, a GJR-GARCH model (§5.5.5–§5.5.6).

📌 Key idea — measured, then forecast

Every number in this session is one of two things: an estimate of a volatility that already happened (§5.5.2–§5.5.4), or a forecast of one that hasn't (§5.5.5–§5.5.6). Quoting a backward-looking estimate as if it were a forecast is the single most common mistake in this space (§5.5.7).

5.5.2 Building realized volatility from OHLC

A close-to-close estimate uses one number per day; a real exchange already gives you four.

Every venue timestamps four prices for each bar: the open, the high and low reached during the period, and the close. A close-to-close estimate — the standard-deviation-of-returns approach fixed in Session 2.3 — Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage — uses exactly one of those four numbers per day and discards the other three. Realized volatility means any estimator of a period's volatility built directly from prices actually observed in that period, rather than from a fitted model; the OHLC formulas in this session are the most common realized-volatility tools, because they need nothing but a single day's own bar.

Table 1 lays out the four estimators this session builds and compares — one that ignores the bar entirely, and three that use progressively more of it.

None of these are the same thing as implied volatility (Session 2.7 — Options Primer), which comes from an option's market price, not from the underlying's own OHLC history — a realized estimator asks "how much did the price actually move", an implied one asks "how much movement is the options market paying for". Both matter to a trading desk; only the first is this session's subject.

This is close to a free lunch in market microstructure: the same OHLC bar most systems already store contains several times more information about today's volatility than its closing price alone — §5.5.4 measures exactly how much, and exactly where that stops being true.

5.5.3 The estimators, in formulas

Words first, then three formulas — each one fixes a specific blind spot in the one before it.

Write HH, LL, OO, CC for a single bar's high, low, open and close, all in log price, and nn for the number of bars averaged. The Parkinson estimator is the simplest: the wider the day's range, the higher the variance, so squaring the log high-low range and rescaling it recovers volatility directly, without ever looking at the close:

σˆP2=14nln⁡2∑i=1n(ln⁡HiLi)2\hat\sigma^2_P = \frac{1}{4n\ln 2}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ln\frac{H_i}{L_i}\right)^2

The constant 4ln⁡2≈2.774\ln 2 \approx 2.77 makes the estimator exactly unbiased for a driftless, continuously observed geometric Brownian motion (Session 3.5 — Simulation & Monte Carlo's exact simulation scheme). Real markets — and this session's own simulation — only sample a finite number of times a day, which under-samples the true high and low by the same small, well-understood amount as Session 3.5 — Simulation & Monte Carlo's discrete-monitoring correction for a barrier probability; §5.5.4 measures and corrects for it once, so it never contaminates the more interesting result later in that section.

The Garman-Klass estimator keeps Parkinson's range term and adds the day's own open-to-close move, capturing a little more of what happened without needing any extra data:

σˆGK2=1n∑i=1n[12(ln⁡HiLi)2−(2ln⁡2−1)(ln⁡CiOi)2]\hat\sigma^2_{GK} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\frac12\left(\ln\frac{H_i}{L_i}\right)^2 - (2\ln 2-1)\left(\ln\frac{C_i}{O_i}\right)^2\right]

Both formulas assume trading never stops — that today's open equals yesterday's close exactly. Real markets gap overnight, and neither formula can see a jump that happened before the bar it is looking at even opens. The Yang-Zhang estimator fixes this by adding an explicit overnight term — the variance of ln⁡(Ot/Ct−1)\ln(O_t / C_{t-1}) across the window — to a weighted blend of the plain open-to-close variance and the Rogers & Satchell (1991) estimator, a range formula that, unlike Parkinson and Garman-Klass, does not assume zero drift:

σˆYZ2=σˆo2+kσˆc2+(1−k)σˆRS2,k=0.341.34+n+1n−1\hat\sigma^2_{YZ} = \hat\sigma^2_o + k\,\hat\sigma^2_c + (1-k)\,\hat\sigma^2_{RS}, \qquad k = \frac{0.34}{1.34 + \frac{n+1}{n-1}}

For the one-month (21-bar) windows this session uses, k≈k \approx 0.139 — small for any realistic window length, so Yang-Zhang leans mainly on the overnight term and the Rogers-Satchell blend rather than the plain open-to-close variance. That is exactly why it stays accurate once trading stops overnight and the other two formulas do not (§5.5.4).

🧭 Margin note — k is not a free dial

k is fixed by the window length n, not chosen by hand: it is the weighting that minimises the estimator's own sampling variance for a random walk. You cannot tune it for a better-looking number without changing what the formula is estimating — the same discipline as the Yang-Zhang paper's own derivation. A practitioner who instead hand-picks weights to match a preferred answer has quietly left the realm of estimation and entered the realm of curve-fitting a single number.

5.5.4 In code: efficiency, and what a gap breaks

Simulate an OHLC bar with a known true volatility, and every claim above becomes checkable.

To grade an estimator you need to know the truth, so the companion lab simulates a month of one-day bars with a chosen true annualised volatility of 20%, using Session 3.5 — Simulation & Monte Carlo's exact log-space geometric Brownian motion at 200 steps per day, and keeps the running maximum and minimum within each day — exactly how a real exchange's OHLC bar is built, just from ticks instead of steps:

# the whole Parkinson formula, on arrays of log(open), log(high), log(low), log(close)
def parkinson_var(o, h, l, c):
    return ((h - l) ** 2).mean(axis=1) / (4 * np.log(2)) / DT_DAY

Repeating this 20,000 times gives a distribution of estimates for every formula, which answers two questions a single backtest never could. First: with no overnight gap, how much less noisy is each estimator than close-to-close? Relative efficiency is the ratio of sampling variances — an estimator that is 5× more efficient has a fifth of the estimation error for the same number of days, or needs a fifth as many days for the same precision. This simulation finds 5.6× for Parkinson, 8.9× for Garman-Klass and 8.1× for Yang-Zhang (fig 5.5.3) — the same regime as, though not identical to, the papers' continuous-time figures (Parkinson up to ~5.2×, Garman-Klass up to ~7.4×, Yang-Zhang up to ~14×), because a finite number of trials and a 200-step daily discretisation are not quite their continuous-time limit. That same discretisation also very slightly under-samples the true high and low, biasing every range-based estimate down by a small, known amount — between 110% and 114% relative — which is corrected once here and carried through the rest of this section.

Second, and more consequentially: what happens once trading actually stops overnight? Re-running the same simulation with 30% of each day's true variance moved into an overnight gap shows exactly the blind spot fig 5.5.3 hides. Parkinson and Garman-Klass only ever look at one day's own bar, so neither can see a jump that happened before that day's open: both land at 16.7% and 16.7% — matching the intraday-only truth of 16.7%, not the 20.0% total — a 3.3-percentage-point understatement that sits 549 standard errors from noise. Close-to-close and Yang-Zhang, which both look across the gap, land on 20.0% and 20.0% — correct, and, for Yang-Zhang, still far less noisy than close-to-close (fig 5.5.4). Put in terms a live risk system cares about: an estimator several times more efficient reaches a given precision in a corresponding fraction of the trading days — the difference between a volatility estimate that reacts within a week and one that needs a calendar quarter to say anything with confidence.

5.5.5 EWMA: one parameter, a real trade-off

RiskMetrics reduced the volatility forecast problem to a single number — and a trade-off you cannot dial away.

The simplest volatility forecast that actually reacts to news is J.P. Morgan's RiskMetrics EWMA volatility: an exponentially weighted moving average of squared returns, updated with one decay parameter λ:

σˆt2=λσˆt−12+(1−λ)rt−12\hat\sigma^2_t = \lambda\,\hat\sigma^2_{t-1} + (1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}^2

λ is a multiplier, not a probability, but it behaves like one: it is the weight carried over from yesterday's estimate, so 1−λ1-\lambda is the weight given to yesterday's single squared return. RiskMetrics' original convention — still the default in most risk systems descended from it — is λ = 0.94 for daily data and λ = 0.97 for monthly data (RiskMetrics, 1996).

fig 5.5.5 makes the trade-off literal: a small λ gives yesterday's return a big vote, so the estimate reacts fast to a real regime change but also jumps around on ordinary noise; a large λ barely moves from one day to the next, so it is smooth but slow to admit the world has changed. There is no λ that is both. The same shrinkage converts to a half-life — the number of days for a shock's weight to halve, ln⁡(0.5)/ln⁡(λ)\ln(0.5)/\ln(\lambda) — of 7 days at λ = 0.90, 11 days at λ = 0.94, and 23 days at λ = 0.97 (Table 2).

Measured against a simulated process whose true conditional volatility is known exactly (the same GARCH(1,1) process §5.5.6 fits), no fixed λ wins by construction: λ = 0.90 tracks it best here, with a root-mean-square error of 1.49 percentage points of annualised volatility, against 1.55 for the RiskMetrics default and 2.68 for λ = 0.97. That is not a general result — a different true process would favour a different λ — which is exactly EWMA's limitation: the decay parameter is fixed once, by convention, and never adapts to how persistent the data actually turn out to be. §5.5.6 fits that persistence directly instead of assuming it.

5.5.6 GARCH and GJR-GARCH: a fitted alternative

GARCH replaces EWMA's fixed decay with numbers estimated from the data — and knows where volatility is heading, not just where it is.

A GARCH(1,1) model (Bollerslev, 1986, generalising Engle's 1982 ARCH) writes tomorrow's variance as three pieces: a constant, a reaction to yesterday's squared shock, and memory of yesterday's own variance:

σt2=ω+αεt−12+βσt−12\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2
🧭 Margin note — reusing α, β, γ

GARCH's own literature calls its coefficients α, β and (below) γ — so does the arch package's own output — which collides with this course's reserved α (a return not explained by factors) and β (factor exposure) elsewhere in the course, and with γ as risk aversion in Unit 3. In this section and the lab, α, β and γ always mean GARCH's own reaction, memory and asymmetry coefficients, never a factor loading or a risk-aversion parameter.

EWMA is the special case ω = 0, α = 1−λ, β = λ — an "integrated" GARCH whose reaction and memory sum to exactly 1, which is why its forecast never reverts anywhere (§5.5.5). A genuine GARCH(1,1) instead reverts to a long-run variance ω/(1−α−β)\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta), so it needs α and β estimated precisely, not assumed. We fit one with the arch package's maximum likelihood rather than hand-rolling the optimisation:

# arch's own advice: scale returns to roughly unit variance before fitting
from arch import arch_model
res = arch_model(returns * 100, mean="Zero", vol="GARCH", p=1, q=1, dist="t").fit()

On 2,500 simulated days from a known GARCH(1,1) (true α = 0.08, β = 0.90), the fit recovers α = 0.102 and β = 0.879 — both within the model's own reported standard errors of the truth (Table 3) — and tracks the true conditional volatility with a root-mean-square error of 0.61 percentage points, beating every fixed λ from §5.5.5, including the best one at 1.49 (fig 5.5.6).

The gap is starkest in a genuine forecast. GARCH's own multi-step forecast decays geometrically toward its long-run level as the horizon grows; EWMA's, because reaction and memory sum to exactly 1, is flat at today's estimate for every horizon — it has no long-run level to revert to. From the same day, GARCH's 60-trading-day-ahead forecast moves from 27.2% toward the true long-run volatility of 15.9%, reaching 21.1% — 5.2 percentage points still to go — while EWMA's forecast sits frozen at 29.1%, 13.2 points away (fig 5.5.7). Persistence this high means even a correctly specified GARCH model reverts slowly — but it moves in a direction EWMA structurally cannot. This is not a subtle difference in practice: a risk desk that reports a flat 30-day volatility forecast every single day, whatever just happened, is not forecasting — it is relabelling today's estimate and hoping nobody asks what "30-day" was supposed to mean.

Both models still treat a positive and a negative shock of the same size identically. Session 5.1 — Stylized Facts of Returns calls the real-world asymmetry — bad news raising volatility by more than equally-sized good news — the leverage effect; fig 5.5.8 is the decision it implies.

The GJR-GARCH model (Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle, 1993) answers it with one extra parameter γ, switched on only when yesterday's shock was negative — let dt−1=1d_{t-1}=1 when εt−1<0\varepsilon_{t-1}\lt0, else 0:

σt2=ω+(α+γdt−1)εt−12+βσt−12\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,d_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2

Simulating a process with true γ = 0.09 — so a bad-news shock moves next-day variance by α+γ = 0.12 for every good-news shock's α = 0.03 — and fitting GJR-GARCH the same way recovers γ = 0.077, 5.7 standard errors from zero: detected reliably, not a coin flip. Fit the plain, symmetric GARCH(1,1) to the very same asymmetric data instead, and its single α lands at 0.065 — close to the theoretical average reaction α+γ/2 = 0.075 — a compromise number that hides the asymmetry rather than measuring it (fig 5.5.9, Table 3).

5.5.7 Where people go wrong

Four mistakes this session's numbers were built to catch.

Reading only the close when the bar already has a high and a low. Every venue already timestamps open, high and low for free; ignoring them and computing a plain close-to-close standard deviation throws away the efficiency gain fig 5.5.3 measures, for no saving in data or effort (fig 5.5.10). The only honest reason to prefer close-to-close is genuinely having closes but no intraday range — end-of-day-only data — not habit.

Getting the annualisation exponent wrong. Session 2.3 — Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage fixed the rule: variance scales with the number of periods, so volatility — its square root — scales with the square root of the number of periods (the √n rule, distinct from Session 14.3's market-impact "square-root law"). A GARCH or EWMA output like this session's is a daily figure; multiplying a daily σ by 252 instead of √252 overstates annual volatility roughly sixteen-fold — an error invisible until it is checked against an actual annual number.

Quoting IGARCH's flat forecast as if it knew something about a specific future date. §5.5.6's forecast comparison (fig 5.5.7) is the whole point: an EWMA-based "60-day-ahead volatility forecast" is mathematically identical to today's estimate at every horizon — that is not evidence the desk expects volatility to stay exactly where it is, only that the model cannot say otherwise.

Confusing GARCH's α, β, γ with a factor model's alpha and beta. A course that reuses Greek letters across units makes this an easy slip; §5.5.6's margin note flags it once. The fix is context: a GARCH table's α, β, γ always describe how variance updates, never a factor loading or a return in excess of a benchmark — if a sentence mixes "alpha" and "volatility" in the same breath, stop and check which α is meant before trusting the conclusion.

⚠️ Pitfall — "more data means a better volatility estimate"

None of the four mistakes above are about sample size. A Parkinson estimate computed from a hundred days is still measuring the wrong quantity if the market gapped overnight on every one of them (§5.5.4); more data sharpens a biased estimator's precision without ever fixing its bias. If a volatility number looks suspiciously stable across a market you know gaps often, that stability is itself a symptom, not a reassurance.

5.5.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

realized volatility — a volatility estimate built directly from prices actually observed over a period (a day's own open, high, low and close), rather than one implied by a fitted model.
Parkinson estimator — a realized-volatility formula using only a period's high and low, rescaled by 1/(4 ln 2) to be unbiased for a driftless, continuously observed price.
Garman-Klass estimator — a realized-volatility formula that adds a period's open-to-close move to the Parkinson range term, for a further gain in precision.
Yang-Zhang estimator — a realized-volatility formula that adds an explicit overnight (close-to-open) variance term, so it stays accurate even when the market gaps between periods.
EWMA volatility — an exponentially weighted moving average of squared returns with one decay parameter λ; RiskMetrics' 1996 convention for a fast, simple volatility forecast.
GARCH model — a fitted model of conditional variance as a constant plus a reaction to the last shock and memory of the last variance, mean-reverting to a long-run level EWMA cannot express.
GJR-GARCH model — a GARCH model with one extra parameter that lets a negative shock raise next period's variance by more than an equally-sized positive one.
volatility forecast — a statement about a future period's volatility, as opposed to a measurement of a period that already happened; EWMA and GARCH each produce one in a structurally different way.

Check yourself

1. A desk stores full OHLC bars, but a junior analyst computes historical volatility from daily closes only. What is the analyst leaving on the table, and when would close-to-close actually be the right choice?
They are giving up several times the estimation precision for free (fig 5.5.3) — the closing price alone throws away the day's own high and low. Close-to-close is the right choice only when there genuinely is no intraday range to use, such as weekly or monthly bars built from period-end prices only.
2. Two numbers both get described as "today's annualised volatility is 18%": one is a Yang-Zhang estimate from the last 21 trading days, the other is a GARCH(1,1) forecast for tomorrow. Are these the same statement?
No. The first measures a period that already happened (backward-looking); the second forecasts one that hasn't (forward-looking). Treating a realized estimate as if it were a forecast, or vice versa, is §5.5.1's key idea and §5.5.7's first cousin of the main pitfall.
3. Why can EWMA's 60-day-ahead volatility forecast never differ from its 1-day-ahead forecast, no matter which λ you pick?
Because EWMA is GARCH with reaction and memory (α+β) pinned to exactly 1 — an integrated GARCH with no long-run level to revert to. Its forecast is flat at today's estimate at every horizon, by construction, not by a modelling choice (§5.5.6, fig 5.5.7).
4. A fitted GARCH(1,1) reports α = 0.10. A separate factor-model regression on the same asset also reports a coefficient of 0.10. Are these measuring the same thing?
No. GARCH's α is how much next-day variance reacts to yesterday's squared shock; a factor model's coefficient is a return sensitivity to a benchmark or a return unexplained by one. The shared Greek letter is a standard, flagged collision (§5.5.6's margin note), not a shared meaning.
5. Parkinson and Garman-Klass both assume the market never stops trading. A stock you are modelling gaps by 2% at the open most days. Which estimator should you reach for, and why?
Yang-Zhang: it adds the overnight close-to-open variance explicitly, so it is not blind to a jump that happens before the bar even opens (§5.5.3–§5.5.4). Parkinson and Garman-Klass would both systematically understate the true volatility.
6. You fit a plain GARCH(1,1) to an equity index known for a strong leverage effect. Its single α comes out close to the average of the true up-shock and down-shock reactions. What test tells you whether you actually need GJR-GARCH's extra parameter?
Fit GJR-GARCH and check how many standard errors γ's own estimate sits from zero (§5.5.6 found about 5.7). If γ is not reliably different from zero, the extra parameter is not earning its added complexity.
