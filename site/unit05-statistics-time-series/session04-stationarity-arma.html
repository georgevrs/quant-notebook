Stationarity & ARMA Models

A random walk and a mean-reverting series can look identical on a chart, yet they call for completely different models and completely different trust in a forecast. This session gives you the machinery to tell them apart — the ADF and KPSS unit-root tests, the AR/MA/ARMA family that models what is left once a series is stationary, and the ACF/PACF plots that tell you which one to fit. The core move: test for stationarity before you model anything, because the standard tests have opposite null hypotheses and, near the boundary, will disagree.

5.4.1 What "stationary" means (and why prices aren't)

Every test, every model and every confidence interval in this session rests on one question: does the series' behaviour change depending on when you look at it?

Statisticians call this property stationarity: a series is (weakly) stationary when its mean, its variance and the way it correlates with its own past do not depend on the date. Ten years of a stationary series look statistically like any other ten years: same average, same spread, same tendency to snap back after a shock. Nothing about being stationary says the series is smooth or boring; a stationary series can be jagged and unpredictable period to period. It just cannot drift.

Prices do drift. A stock's price today is close to yesterday's price plus an unpredictable shock, over and over, so its plausible range widens the longer you watch: a price series with this property has a unit root, meaning today's value carries the full weight of every past shock forward, undiminished. Formally, a price path is well approximated by a random walk — the purest example of a unit-root process — and a random walk is the textbook case of a non-stationary series: its variance grows in proportion to how long you have been watching it, and a shock from ten years ago is still sitting in today's level, exactly as large as the day it hit. Not every non-stationary series is a unit root, though. A series can instead be trend-stationary: stationary once you subtract a deterministic trend, rather than once you difference it. Differencing a trend-stationary series still makes it stationary, but it is the wrong fix — it throws away information and can manufacture short-term correlation that was never really there. Telling the two apart is exactly what the choice of ADF specification in §5.4.2's pitfall callout is for.

Log returns are a different animal. Each day's log return is close to independent of the last, its average and spread don't depend on which decade you sample, and a bad day does not "carry over" — tomorrow's return does not remember yesterday's. That is Session 5.1 — Stylized Facts of Returns's near-zero autocorrelation restated as a stationarity claim: returns are close to stationary; the price level built by accumulating them is not.

The engineering analogy is exact: a random walk is what you get by integrating white noise. Each individual increment is unpredictable and mean-zero, exactly like the noise feeding it, but the running sum of unpredictable increments is not — its standard deviation grows with the square root of elapsed time, the same n\sqrt{n} rule that governs a sum of independent shocks anywhere else in this course. A ten-day-ahead forecast interval for a stationary series is barely wider than a one-day-ahead one, because the series keeps snapping back to its mean; a ten-year-ahead forecast interval for a price level is enormously wider than a one-year-ahead one, because nothing pulls it back. That difference is not a modelling inconvenience — it is the entire reason "the price will be roughly here in ten years" is a much harder claim to defend than "returns will average roughly this much."

The practical stakes go beyond forecasting. Regress one unit-root series on another — two unrelated price levels, say — and ordinary least squares will often report a high R2R^2 and a strongly significant slope even when there is no real relationship at all: two series that are each independently drifting will drift together often enough, over any one sample, to look related. This is the classic "spurious regression" trap, and it is precisely why Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie's OLS machinery assumes stationary inputs. Session 5.6 — Cointegration & Mean Reversion comes back to this from the opposite direction: two unit-root series occasionally really do move together in a way that survives the test properly done — that relationship is called cointegration, and testing for stationarity first is exactly what separates a genuine pairs-trading candidate from a coincidence.

📌 Key idea

Stationarity is not a property of the numbers looking calm — it is the property that makes a hypothesis test, a confidence interval, or a forecast horizon mean the same thing regardless of which window of history you happened to sample. Fit a model that assumes stationarity to a series that has a unit root, and every standard error you compute is quietly wrong.

5.4.2 Detecting a unit root: ADF and KPSS

Two tests, built the opposite way round, are the standard first check — and disagreeing with itself is exactly what makes the pair useful.

The ADF test (Augmented Dickey-Fuller) asks whether a series' own last value helps predict its next change. Write the series as an AR(1), rt=c+ϕrt−1+εtr_t = c + \phi r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, and re-arrange it as Δrt=c+(ϕ−1)rt−1+εt\Delta r_t = c + (\phi - 1) r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t — augmented, in practice, with enough lagged Δrt−i\Delta r_{t-i} terms to soak up any leftover autocorrelation in the residuals. The null hypothesis is H0:ϕ=1H_0: \phi = 1, a unit root; the alternative is ϕ<1\phi \lt 1, stationary. A large negative test statistic rejects the unit root. Critically, ADF's null is "non-stationary" — failing to reject it is not proof of a unit root, only a failure to rule one out, exactly the p-value logic from Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie.

The KPSS test (Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin) is built the other way around: it splits the series into a deterministic part, a random-walk component and a stationary error, then tests whether the random walk's variance is exactly zero. Its null hypothesis is H0H_0: the series is stationary; rejecting it is evidence of a unit root. Run side by side, ADF and KPSS are testing opposite default assumptions — one presumes non-stationarity until proven otherwise, the other presumes stationarity until proven otherwise. That is not redundancy; it is the entire point of using both.

ADF verdictKPSS verdictWhat to conclude
rejects unit rootfails to reject stationarityagreement — treat the series as stationary
fails to reject unit rootrejects stationarityagreement — treat it as a unit root; difference it
rejects unit rootalso rejects stationaritycontradiction — often a trend the ADF specification missed; check the plot and try a trend term
fails to reject unit rootfails to reject stationaritycontradiction — usually just an underpowered sample; gather more data, do not force a verdict

The word "augmented" in ADF's name is doing real work: a plain Dickey-Fuller regression with no extra lagged-difference terms only removes serial correlation if there happens to be none left to remove, and any that remains inflates the test's residual variance and biases the test toward failing to reject. In practice you let the routine choose the augmentation length itself — statsmodels's adfuller(..., autolag="AIC") tries a range of lag counts and keeps whichever minimises AIC, the same criterion §5.4.5 uses for ARMA order — rather than hard-coding a fixed number of lags that might be wrong for the series in front of you. This is also why the two tests genuinely disagree at moderate persistence rather than only in a pathological edge case: series like short-term interest rates or dividend yields are economically mean-reverting but do so slowly, with an AR coefficient that can sit anywhere from 0.90 to 0.99 — precisely the range where a few hundred observations rarely settle the question either way.

The lab makes both halves of this concrete. Simulate 200 GBM price paths, log them, and split each into its level and its return series. ADF rejects the unit root on the level only 2.5% of the time — almost exactly its nominal 5% size, because the null (a unit root) is actually true there — but rejects it on returns 100% of the time, full power against a truly stationary alternative. KPSS shows the mirror image: it rejects stationarity on the level 95% of the time and on returns only 5% of the time. Same two tests, same 5% threshold — the verdicts flip completely depending on which object you feed them, exactly as the session's opening claim predicts.

Now sweep an AR(1) coefficient ϕ\phi from comfortably stationary toward an exact unit root and run both tests on 150 fresh replications at each value. Far from the boundary the tests agree almost every time — at ϕ=0.50\phi = 0.50 they contradict each other in only 6% of replications. But around ϕ=\phi = 0.90 — a series whose shocks take roughly 7 periods to decay halfway back to its mean, the kind of persistence real short-rate and dividend-yield series show — ADF's power to detect stationarity has fallen sharply while KPSS's tendency to (correctly, but for a genuinely persistent series, unhelpfully) flag it as non-stationary has risen, and the two flatly contradict each other in 20% of replications: nearly one run in five gives you one test screaming "stationary" and the other screaming "unit root" from the very same data. Session 5.6 — Cointegration & Mean Reversion turns exactly this decay rate into a tradable half-life of mean reversion — the slower the decay, the longer you have to wait for a bet on reversion to pay off.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the test told me the answer"

ADF and KPSS both come in three flavours — no constant, a constant, and a constant plus a linear trend — and the choice matters as much as the test itself. Always start with a constant, add a trend term only if the plot plainly shows one, and never let a script silently pick the specification for you: a trend-stationary series tested with the wrong variant of ADF will look exactly like a unit root.

5.4.3 AR, MA and ARMA: the building blocks

Once a series is stationary (or has been differenced until it is), the next question is what shape its own memory takes.

An autoregressive model — AR(p) for short — says today's value is a weighted sum of its own last p values plus fresh noise:

rt=c+ϕ1rt−1+ϕ2rt−2+⋯+ϕprt−p+εtr_t = c + \phi_1 r_{t-1} + \phi_2 r_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p r_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t

An AR(1) is stationary exactly when |ϕ1|<1|\phi_1| \lt 1 — the same condition, restated, that made ADF's null hypothesis ϕ=1\phi = 1 the dividing line in §5.4.2. For a general AR(p), the condition is that the roots of its characteristic polynomial all lie outside the unit circle; |ϕ|<1|\phi| \lt 1 is just the p=1 special case.

A moving-average model — MA(q) for short — looks completely different: today's value is a weighted sum of the last q unpredictable shocks, not of past levels at all:

rt=c+εt+θ1εt−1+⋯+θqεt−qr_t = c + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}

An MA(q) is automatically stationary for any θ\theta — it is a finite sum of noise terms — but it needs an analogous condition of its own, called invertibility, before it can be rewritten as an (infinite) AR process; statsmodels checks this for you when it fits one. Invertibility matters practically, not just theoretically: an invertible MA(q) can be expressed as an AR(∞), which is what lets you recover today's shock εt\varepsilon_t from the observed history and therefore produce a sensible one-step forecast at all. Fit a non-invertible MA by mistake and two completely different parameter values explain the data equally well — the likelihood cannot tell them apart — so the fitted θˆ\hat\theta becomes an arbitrary pick between look-alike models rather than an estimate of anything real.

AR and MA are also two views of the same idea: a stationary AR(1) unfolds into an MA(∞), and an invertible MA(1) unfolds into an AR(∞). ARMA, letting a small amount of each do the work, is usually the shortest honest description of a real series that would otherwise need a long pure AR or pure MA.

An ARMA model combines both pieces into a single equation:

rt=c+∑i=1pϕirt−i+εt+∑j=1qθjεt−jr_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i r_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^q \theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j}

and is written ARMA(p,q). The appeal is parsimony: a process that would need a long pure AR or pure MA to describe well often needs only one or two terms of each once you let it mix. The running example for the rest of this session is an ARMA(1,1) with ϕ=0.6\phi = 0.6 and θ=−0.4\theta = -0.4 — small enough to write out, rich enough that neither a pure AR(1) nor a pure MA(1) fully captures it, which is exactly what makes it a good test of the identification and selection tools in §5.4.4 and §5.4.5. If §5.4.2's tests had found a unit root instead, the fix is mechanical: difference the series once (twice, on the rare occasion one difference still leaves a unit root) and fit the AR/MA structure to what remains, rather than to the original level. statsmodels' ARIMA class takes this differencing order as its middle argument — order (p, d, q) — so an ARMA(1,1) fit directly, as in §5.4.6, is simply the special case d=0.

ModelDepends onTypical use
AR(p)its own last p valuesmomentum or mean-reversion in the series' own history
MA(q)the last q shocks (surprises)a shock whose effect takes a few periods to fully arrive
ARMA(p,q)both at oncefits real data more parsimoniously than a large pure AR or pure MA

5.4.4 Reading the ACF and PACF

Before fitting anything, look at two plots — they tell you which family of model to even try.

The ACF (autocorrelation function) at lag k is the correlation between rtr_t and rt−kr_{t-k}, for every k. It picks up both a direct effect and every indirect one relayed through the lags in between — lag 2's correlation partly reflects lag 1's, transmitted forward. The PACF (partial autocorrelation function) strips that relaying out: PACF at lag k is the correlation between rtr_t and rt−kr_{t-k} after netting out everything already explained by the lags in between, the same idea as a partial regression coefficient from Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie applied one lag at a time. Under the null of no autocorrelation at a given lag, both are approximately normal with standard deviation 1/T1/\sqrt{T}, so a bar beyond roughly ±1.96/T\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T} is conventionally called significant.

The two plots' shapes identify the model. A pure AR(p) has an ACF that tails off gradually (a damped sinusoid or exponential decay) but a PACF that cuts off sharply to zero right after lag p — the direct effect of anything further back really is zero once you control for the p lags in between. A pure MA(q) is the mirror image: its ACF cuts off sharply after lag q — by construction, a shock more than q periods old has already fully left the equation — while its PACF tails off. An ARMA(p,q) with both terms present shows neither a clean cutoff: both the ACF and the PACF decay gradually, which is itself diagnostic — it tells you a pure AR or pure MA won't be enough.

ProcessACF shapePACF shape
AR(p)tails off (gradual decay)cuts off sharply after lag p
MA(q)cuts off sharply after lag qtails off (gradual decay)
ARMA(p,q)tails offtails off — neither cuts off

On 2,000 simulated points from the running ARMA(1,1) example, the lag-1 ACF and PACF are, as they must always be for any process, identical: 0.26. From lag 2 on they diverge — PACF drops to 0.08 and keeps shrinking, while the ACF decays more slowly, tailing off the way the table predicts for a process with a genuine AR component. With a sample this size the approximate 95% significance band sits at about 4.4% in either direction — narrow enough that the bars beyond lag 4 or so are indistinguishable from noise, which is exactly the point: real ARMA signatures are usually only visible in the first handful of lags.

A practical rule of thumb for how far out to even plot: look at roughly min⁡(10log10⁡T,T/4)\min(10 \log_{10} T,\, T/4) lags rather than an arbitrary round number like 20 or 50 regardless of sample size. Plot too few lags and you can miss a slow AR decay; plot too many relative to T and you are mostly looking at noise, since the significance band ±1.96/T\pm 1.96/\sqrt T only shrinks as the square root of the sample grows — quadrupling your data halves the band, not eliminates it. Under the hood, the PACF at each lag is computed by fitting successively longer AR regressions and reading off the coefficient on the newest lag each time (the Yule-Walker equations, or an equivalent recursion) — which is exactly why it isolates the direct effect: everything the shorter lags already explain is absorbed into their own coefficients before the new lag gets a look in.

Worth keeping in mind while you look at fig 5.4.5: this ARMA(1,1) example was chosen to have visible structure worth reading. Run the same exercise on real daily log returns and, true to §5.4.1's opening claim, almost every bar in both plots sits inside the significance band — the near-zero Session 5.1 — Stylized Facts of Returns autocorrelation showing up again, this time as a diagnosis rather than a headline number.

🧭 Margin note — reading a real ACF/PACF plot

Do not chase bars two or three lags past where the pattern clearly ends — with finitely many observations, roughly one bar in twenty will poke past the significance band by chance alone even under pure noise, the same multiple-comparisons logic Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie covers for regression. Look for a clear structural break, not the single largest bar.

5.4.5 Choosing the order: AIC, BIC, and when they disagree

ACF and PACF narrow down the candidates; an information criterion picks among them — but "pick the smallest number" hides a real choice about what you are optimising for.

Fitting a model by maximum likelihood always improves (or at worst does not worsen) as you add parameters, so raw likelihood cannot select an order — it would always vote for the biggest model on offer. An information criterion penalises complexity to compensate. The Akaike information criterion is

AIC=−2ln⁡Lˆ+2k\mathrm{AIC} = -2 \ln \hat L + 2k

where Lˆ\hat L is the maximised likelihood and k the number of parameters; the Bayesian information criterion swaps the flat penalty for one that grows with the sample:

BIC=−2ln⁡Lˆ+kln⁡T\mathrm{BIC} = -2 \ln \hat L + k \ln T

with T the number of periods in the sample. Since ln⁡T>2\ln T > 2 for any T>7T > 7, BIC penalises an extra parameter more harshly than AIC does at any realistic sample size — and the gap widens as T grows. The two criteria are not just two estimates of the same thing: AIC is built to minimise out-of-sample prediction error and is not guaranteed to land on the true order even with infinite data, while BIC is built to be consistent — as T→∞T \to \infty it selects the true order with probability approaching one — at the cost of favouring smaller models than AIC in any finite sample.

The lab makes that gap concrete rather than asymptotic. Refit the same five candidate orders — MA(1), AR(1), the true ARMA(1,1), ARMA(2,1) and ARMA(1,2) — on 200 fresh 300-point draws of the running example. AIC recovers the true ARMA(1,1) order in 45% of replications; BIC manages only 20%. The flip side is just as stark: BIC settles for the too-simple AR(1) in 74% of replications, against AIC's 36%. Both criteria are looking at exactly the same fitted likelihoods on exactly the same data — the disagreement is entirely the penalty term, log(300) ≈ 5.7 against AIC's flat 2, doing exactly what the formulas say it should.

SituationLean onWhy
You mainly care about forecast accuracyAICtuned to minimise out-of-sample prediction error, even at the cost of an extra parameter
You want the most defensible, parsimonious model to reportBICconsistent — converges on the true order as data grows, and resists overfitting in the meantime
The two disagreefit both, check residualsa Ljung-Box test on the smaller model's residuals (§5.4.6) tells you whether the extra term is really needed
💡 Tip

Never compare AIC or BIC values computed on different sample sizes, different differencing, or different software defaults for the constant term — the additive constants in the likelihood do not cancel unless everything else about the fit is identical.

Neither criterion is "wrong" — they answer different questions. In quant research this is an everyday trade-off, not an academic footnote: a young ETF or a strategy that only makes sense post-2015 might give you a few hundred usable observations, nowhere near the asymptotic regime either criterion assumes. Many practitioners default to BIC precisely because its stronger penalty resists mistaking noise for structure when data is scarce, accepting some underfitting as the price of not fitting noise; others prefer AIC once there is enough history that its sensitivity to real structure earns its keep. Either default is defensible — picking one after seeing which model it selects on your data is not.

5.4.6 Fitting ARMA in code

Three calls to statsmodels — a unit-root test, a stationarity test, and a maximum-likelihood fit — cover everything above.

# statsmodels does the heavy lifting: ADF, KPSS and ARMA maximum likelihood
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, kpss
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import acorr_ljungbox

adf = adfuller(series, autolag="AIC", result_object=True)   # H0: unit root
kp = kpss(series, regression="c", nlags="auto", result_object=True)  # H0: stationary

fit = ARIMA(series, order=(1, 0, 1), trend="n").fit()          # ARMA(1,1), no separate constant
resid_ok = acorr_ljungbox(fit.resid, lags=[10], return_df=True)

On one 300-point draw of the running ARMA(1,1) example, the fit recovers ϕˆ=\hat\phi = 0.59 and θˆ=\hat\theta = −0.43 against true values of 0.6 and −0.4, with standard errors of 0.21 and 0.24 — both true values sit comfortably inside two standard errors of their estimate. The last line is the check you should never skip: the Ljung-Box test asks whether a batch of residual autocorrelations, taken together, is distinguishable from zero. Here it returns a p-value of 0.62 — comfortably above 0.05, so the residuals show no leftover structure the model missed. A small Ljung-Box p-value after fitting is a direct signal to revisit §5.4.4's ACF/PACF and try a different order.

🧭 Margin note

trend="n" tells ARIMA not to fit a separate mean/intercept on top of the AR and MA terms — appropriate here because the simulated series is already mean-zero. Real return series usually need trend="c" instead, since even a small non-zero average return matters over many periods.

One naming quirk worth knowing: older tutorials often import a standalone ARMA class from statsmodels.tsa.arima_model, a module removed after deprecation. The current, single entry point is statsmodels.tsa.arima.model.ARIMA, fitting AR, MA, ARMA and differenced models alike through one state-space maximum-likelihood routine. Fitting is iterative, so it is worth checking fit.mle_retvals["converged"] before trusting a model.

5.4.7 Where people go wrong

Every mistake here comes from skipping a check, not from getting the algebra wrong.

The single most common error is treating "my ADF test did not reject the unit root" as proof that a unit root exists. It is not — a failure to reject is, at best, a failure to rule the null out, and ADF has famously low power exactly where it matters most: a slowly mean-reverting series with ϕ=0.90\phi = 0.90 or 0.950.95 will often fail to reject a unit root in a few hundred observations even though it is genuinely, if slowly, stationary. §5.4.2's contradiction-rate experiment is this exact failure mode, engineered on purpose so you see it happen. The fix is procedural, not statistical: run both tests, read the table's four cells rather than one test's verdict, and when they disagree, say so rather than picking whichever answer you expected.

The second error is fitting an ARMA model directly to a price level without testing it first. Because a near-unit-root series is highly persistent, an AR(1) fit to raw prices will typically return ϕˆ\hat\phi extremely close to 1 regardless of whether the truth is 1.00 or 0.97 — the estimate cannot tell the difference well, but the forecasts it produces absolutely can, extrapolating a trend that is really just one accumulated random walk. Difference first, or model returns instead of levels, and this problem disappears on its own. Picture a backtest that fits exactly this AR(1)-on-price-levels model every month and trades the model's implied "reversion" back to a forecast mean: in sample, ϕˆ≈0.995\hat\phi \approx 0.995 fits beautifully — the model tracks the price almost perfectly one step ahead, because a near-unit-root series is, tautologically, well predicted by its own last value. Out of sample, the tiny gap between 0.995 and a true unit root is exactly where the strategy's edge was hiding, and it evaporates the moment the drift that happened to hold during the fit window stops holding.

The third error is treating any single check in isolation as sufficient. A model can pass ADF and KPSS, look clean on the ACF/PACF, and still fail Ljung-Box if the true order needed one more term than the plots suggested. Each tool narrows the search; none of them, alone, proves a model is right — which is why the workflow in fig 5.4.2 ends in a validation step, not a selection step.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the model with the best AIC is definitely the right one"

An information criterion ranks candidates against each other on one sample; it does not certify that the winner is correctly specified in any absolute sense. Always follow order selection with a residual check (Ljung-Box, §5.4.6) — a model that "wins" on AIC but leaves visible autocorrelation in its residuals is still missing something the criterion could not see.

5.4.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

stationarity — the property that a series' mean, variance and autocorrelation do not depend on the date you measure them from.
unit root — a series whose current value carries forward the full, undiminished weight of every past shock, so its variance grows without bound; the defining feature of a random walk.
ADF test — the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test; null hypothesis: the series has a unit root.
KPSS test — the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin test; null hypothesis: the series is stationary.
autoregressive model — AR(p): a series modelled as a weighted sum of its own last p values plus noise.
moving-average model — MA(q): a series modelled as a weighted sum of the last q unpredictable shocks.
ARMA model — a model combining autoregressive and moving-average terms in one equation.
ACF — the autocorrelation function: the correlation between a series and its own lag k, for every k.
PACF — the partial autocorrelation function: the correlation at lag k after netting out the effect of every shorter lag.

Check yourself

1. You plot a fund's cumulative P&L and its daily returns side by side. One wanders further from its starting point the longer you watch; the other stays inside a roughly fixed band. Which one is closer to stationary, and why does it matter before you fit a model to either?
Returns are the (roughly) stationary series — bounded mean and variance regardless of the window. Cumulative P&L behaves like a unit root: its variance grows with the horizon, so an AR(1) fit directly to its level would return ϕˆ\hat\phi close to 1 and extrapolate what is really just an accumulated random walk as if it were a trend. Test first (§5.4.2); model returns, not levels.
2. On a 200-observation interest-rate series, ADF fails to reject the unit-root null (p=0.34) and KPSS also fails to reject stationarity (p=0.09). What should you conclude, and what should you do next?
Neither "definitely a unit root" nor "definitely stationary" — this is the fourth row of the contingency table: both tests are simply inconclusive at this sample size, a common outcome for a slowly mean-reverting series. Failing to reject a null is never evidence for it (the same logic Session 5.2 attaches to p-values). Gather more data, or widen the sample, rather than forcing a verdict.
3. A residual series' ACF cuts off sharply to zero after lag 2, while its PACF decays smoothly, roughly halving every lag. Does this point to AR(2) or MA(2), and why?
MA(2). An MA(q) process is defined by an ACF that cuts off sharply after lag q and a PACF that tails off — the mirror image of AR's signature (PACF cuts off, ACF tails off). The pattern described is exactly MA's, not AR's.
4. Fitting five candidate orders to the same 300-point series, AIC prefers ARMA(2,1) while BIC prefers the simpler AR(1). Which one do you report as "the model," and what do you check before deciding?
Neither, automatically. Fit both and run a Ljung-Box test on each one's residuals: if AR(1)'s residuals already look like white noise, take it for parsimony (BIC's case); if they still show structure that ARMA(2,1) removes, the extra term is real and AIC's pick is doing useful work. The information criteria narrow the search — they do not replace the residual check.
