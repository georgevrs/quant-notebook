Unit 5 · Statistics & Time Series of Returns — Session 01 of 07

Stylized Facts of Returns

You already know real returns are noisy and fat-tailed (Session 3.2); this session asks what that noise looks like over time. Almost no memory sits in the returns themselves, yet strong, slow-fading memory sits in their size: volatility clusters, bad news moves it more than good news of the same size, and only summing returns over months makes the bell curve honest again. Every model this course builds from here — GARCH, VaR, option pricing — exists to reproduce these four regularities, not explain them away.

5.1.1 The four stylized facts

Fit any model you like to returns — a random walk, a factor model, a neural net — and these four regularities will still be sitting in the residuals, waiting to misprice risk.

Stylized facts are the empirical regularities that show up in return data across almost every asset, market and decade — robust enough that Cont (2001) could survey them across equities, indices, exchange rates and commodities and find the same short list every time. Session 3.2 — Distributions & Fat Tails already gave you two of them: returns have fat tails far beyond a normal distribution's, with an excess kurtosis and a tail index this session will not re-derive. This session gives you four more, all about how returns behave over TIME rather than what any single day looks like: near-zero autocorrelation in the returns themselves, volatility clustering in their size, an asymmetric leverage effect where bad days move risk more than good ones, and aggregational Gaussianity — the way summing returns over longer horizons makes them look steadily more normal.

Cont's survey lists more than a dozen such facts in total; this session picks the four that matter most for the volatility and risk models the rest of the course builds, and leaves aggregate volume/volatility co-movement and cross-sectional correlation patterns for later units — and for you to find in this session's homework.

Think of it the way you'd think about load on a production service: request latency isn't independent from one second to the next — a slow database call this second makes a slow call next second more likely, even though the AVERAGE latency hasn't moved. A monitoring system that only tracks the mean misses exactly the bursts that page you at 3 a.m. Returns behave the same way, and the four facts above are the return-side equivalent of "know your tail latency, not just your mean." The rest of this unit turns each fact into a testable number: Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie formalises the statistical machinery, Session 5.4 — Stationarity & ARMA Models the time-series models, and Session 5.5 — Modeling Volatility the volatility forecasts — all built to survive the four facts you are about to measure.

📌 Key idea

A model that reproduces the mean and the variance of returns has matched almost nothing. These four facts are about the SHAPE of dependence over time, and a model that ignores them will look fine on average and fail exactly when it matters — in a cluster of bad days.

5.1.2 Testing for autocorrelation

The most basic question you can ask of a return series: does today tell you anything about tomorrow? For raw returns, almost never — but "almost never" needs a number and a test, not a glance at a chart.

Autocorrelation at lag kk is the correlation between a series and its own value kk periods earlier, ρk=Corr⁡(rt,rt−k)\rho_k = \Corr(r_t, r_{t-k}) — informally here; Session 5.4 — Stationarity & ARMA Models builds the full ACF/PACF toolkit for identifying AR and MA models. For a market built from shocks that are independent of the past, the theory is clean: tomorrow's shock does not depend on today's, so the POPULATION autocorrelation of raw returns is exactly zero at every lag, however volatile the market has recently been.

Step 3 asks whether one lag's correlation is bigger than sampling noise alone would produce: under the null of no autocorrelation, a sample autocorrelation has a standard deviation of roughly 1/n1/\sqrt{n}, so the band narrows as more data arrives — the √n rule from Session 3.5 — Simulation & Monte Carlo, run in reverse. Step 4 pools many lags into one number, the Ljung-Box Q-statistic, a weighted sum of squared sample autocorrelations that behaves like a chi-squared variable under the null — useful because checking twenty lags one at a time at 5% each would flag a false positive on more than half of all series by chance alone, a smaller cousin of the multiple-testing problem Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie treats in full.

The lab runs exactly this on 200 independent 25-year synthetic markets — the same GARCH(1,1) shape Session 3.2 used for fat tails, with Student-t shocks at ν=\nu = 6. Averaged across all 200 markets, the lag-1 autocorrelation of raw returns is 0.001 with a standard error of 0.002 — indistinguishable from zero, and well inside the textbook Bartlett band of about ± 0.013 for a single 6,300-day sample.

🧭 Margin note — a test built for iid noise, run on GARCH noise

Ljung-Box assumes the series is independent and identically distributed under its null, not merely uncorrelated. GARCH returns are uncorrelated but not independent (§5.1.3), and that gap matters: fat tails and clustering inflate the true sampling variance of a sample autocorrelation well past the iid formula the test assumes. Running the same lag-10 test on 200 markets built with a genuinely zero autocorrelation, it rejects at its nominal 5% level on 61.5% of them (Monte Carlo SE 3.4%) — not because anything is predictable, but because the test's own assumptions do not hold for this kind of data. Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie builds the heteroskedasticity-robust correction.

5.1.3 Volatility clustering

Turn the same lens on the SIZE of returns instead of their sign, and the story flips completely.

Square the returns (or take absolute values) and repeat §5.1.2's test: volatility clustering is the finding that squared or absolute returns are strongly and persistently autocorrelated, even though the raw returns are not. The chart below shows why in one picture: this session's market's volatility comes in visible bursts — calm stretches near its long-run 1.00% a day, and stormy stretches several times as large — even though no single day's return predicts the next day's sign.

The mechanism is a conditional variance that depends on its own recent past — today's variance is a base level, plus a jolt from yesterday's surprise, plus inertia from yesterday's variance:

σt2=ω+αεt−12+βσt−12\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2

Read it as an update rule, the same shape as an exponentially-weighted moving average with a floor: today's variance never falls below a base level ω/(1−α−β)\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta), gets a jolt in proportion α\alpha to how big yesterday's surprise was, and carries over a fraction β\beta of yesterday's variance regardless. That carry-over is where the memory lives — a single big shock keeps echoing through σt2\sigma_t^2 for as long as β\beta (and α+β\alpha+\beta) says it should, which is the entire mechanical explanation for clustering: no persistence in the LEVEL of returns, plenty of persistence in the SCALE of the noise generating them.

Session 5.5 — Modeling Volatility formalises this as the GARCH model (Engle 1982; Bollerslev 1986); here it is only the mechanism that produces the fact. Because variance depends on its OWN past, a shock's influence does not vanish after one day: it decays roughly geometrically at rate α+β\alpha + \beta per lag (the ARMA(1,1) representation of a GARCH(1,1)'s squared residuals, Bollerslev 1986). With α=\alpha = 0.08 and β=\beta = 0.90 here, persistence is α+β=\alpha+\beta = 0.98 — a half-life of 34 trading days, about seven weeks for one day's shock to lose half its extra pull on variance.

🧭 Margin note — four letters, two meanings

GARCH notation overloads four symbols this course uses elsewhere: here α\alpha is the ARCH (shock) coefficient, β\beta the GARCH (persistence) coefficient, γ\gamma an asymmetry term (§5.1.4) and ω\omega the variance intercept — standard notation in Engle (1982), Bollerslev (1986) and the arch package's own parameter names, not the portfolio beta, alpha, Kelly fraction or Lagrange multiplier those letters mean in other units. Session 5.5 keeps the same convention.

Fitting a GARCH(1,1) by maximum likelihood with the arch package — never re-derive the optimiser by hand — recovers this persistence cleanly. Averaged over 40 independent 25-year markets, the fitted αˆ+βˆ\hat\alpha+\hat\beta is 0.980 (SE 0.0006), matching the 0.98 built into the simulation to within noise. The chart below puts the two autocorrelation functions side by side: raw returns hug zero at every lag; squared returns stay clearly positive even 30 trading days out.

Persistence this close to 1 is also a practical warning sign: at α+β=0.98\alpha+\beta=0.98, a single day's shock takes seven weeks to fade by half, so a risk model calibrated in a calm year can be badly stale within a quarter of a regime change. Real equity indices are often fitted even closer to the α+β=1\alpha+\beta=1 boundary — the "IGARCH" case, where a shock never fully fades — which is one reason Session 5.4 — Stationarity & ARMA Models's stationarity tests matter for volatility, not only for prices.

Session 5.4 — Stationarity & ARMA Models gives the ACF/PACF machinery formal footing; Session 5.5 — Modeling Volatility forecasts variance itself with GARCH and EWMA; Session 9.7 — VaR, ES, EVT & VaR Backtests's VaR backtests lean on exactly this clustering — it is why a VaR breach yesterday raises the odds of another one today.

5.1.4 The leverage effect

Clustering says a big move, in either direction, raises tomorrow's risk. The leverage effect says direction matters too: a fall raises it more than a rise of the same size.

Fischer Black named this asymmetry the leverage effect in 1976, from a candidate mechanism: a falling share price raises a levered firm's debt-to-equity ratio, making the remaining equity riskier. Christie (1982) then found the effect far larger, across hundreds of stocks, than financial leverage alone can explain — so the honest modern reading treats "leverage effect" as the NAME of the asymmetry, not a settled account of its cause (volatility feedback and a time-varying risk premium are the leading competing stories). Whatever the mechanism, the asymmetry itself is one of the most reproduced findings in the stylized-facts literature.

The competing story, volatility feedback, reverses the arrow: if volatility itself carries a risk premium, then news that volatility is about to rise should immediately lower the current price — investors demand compensation for the extra risk right away — producing the same negative return/future-variance relationship without any change in leverage at all. Both mechanisms predict the same sign, so the two are hard to separate from a return series alone; one reason quants increasingly reach for options data, where a market's own priced-in skew reveals which story it leans on, rather than trying to resolve the debate from a time series like this lab's.

To model it, extend the variance equation with one more term that only switches on after a down day — the GJR-GARCH(1,1) of Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993):

σt2=ω+(α+γ𝟙εt−1<0)εt−12+βσt−12\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \big(\alpha + \gamma\,\mathbb{1}_{\varepsilon_{t-1} \lt 0}\big)\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2

Set γ=0\gamma=0 and it is §5.1.3's plain GARCH(1,1); γ>0\gamma \gt 0 makes a negative shock raise variance by an extra γε2\gamma\varepsilon^2 that a positive shock of the same size does not add. Session 5.5 — Modeling Volatility covers fitting and forecasting the GJR-GARCH model in full; this session only needs it to state the leverage effect as a NUMBER rather than a picture: for a symmetric shock distribution, Cov⁡(rt,σt+12)\Cov(r_t, \sigma_{t+1}^2) is exactly zero when γ=0\gamma=0 (a shock's cube averages to zero whichever way it points) and strictly negative whenever γ>0\gamma \gt 0.

The lab builds two markets with the SAME total variance persistence (0.98) and the same unconditional volatility (1% a day) — one symmetric (α=\alpha = 0.08, γ=0\gamma=0), one asymmetric (α=\alpha = 0.03, γ=\gamma = 0.10) — so the only difference between them is whether sign matters. In the symmetric market, the correlation of today's return with tomorrow's variance averages −0.004 (SE 0.003) across 200 markets: indistinguishable from zero. In the asymmetric market it averages −0.105 (SE 0.003): reliably, decisively negative.

Fitting a GJR-GARCH(1,1) with arch_model(..., vol="GARCH", p=1, o=1, q=1) on one market from each recovers exactly this pattern: the asymmetry term comes back significant (p=p= 6.6e-15) on the asymmetric market and not significant (p=p= 0.30) on the symmetric one — no false positive on a market with no real asymmetry.

📌 Key idea — same persistence, different shape

Two markets can share IDENTICAL total variance persistence and unconditional volatility and still have completely different risk profiles after a selloff. Persistence alone doesn't tell you whether tomorrow's risk depends on today's sign — you have to check the asymmetry directly, with a correlation like this session's or a fitted γ\gamma.

The practical stakes are immediate. A risk system that sizes tomorrow's value at risk off a symmetric volatility model understates risk precisely on the day it matters most — right after a selloff, when the asymmetric market's variance is already elevated and the symmetric one still thinks nothing has changed. Session 9.7 — VaR, ES, EVT & VaR Backtests's VaR backtests catch this the hard way, as a cluster of breaches immediately following large down days rather than a scattering of isolated ones. Sizing positions, setting margin and choosing option hedges all inherit this asymmetry, whether or not the model in use admits it.

5.1.5 Aggregational Gaussianity

Zoom out far enough and even famously fat-tailed daily returns start to behave.

Log returns add across time (Session 2.3 — Returns, P&L, Compounding & Leverage), so a month's return is just the sum of that month's daily log returns. Aggregational Gaussianity is the finding that as you sum over longer and longer horizons, the resulting distribution looks steadily more like a normal — the fat tails visible daily fade at longer horizons, even though no single day became any less fat-tailed. The mechanism is elementary once stated in cumulants rather than raw moments: for a sum of hh weakly dependent shocks, the excess kurtosis of the total falls off roughly like 1/h1/h, because the fourth cumulant of a sum scales with hh while the square of its variance scales with h2h^2.

The lab sums this session's returns over a day, a week, a month and a quarter and re-measures excess kurtosis each time (Session 3.2 — Distributions & Fat Tails owns the definition). Because kurtosis is itself an unstable statistic this close to a GARCH's moment boundary — this exact market's own eighth moment does not exist, as Session 3.2 found — the lab reports the MEDIAN across 200 independent markets rather than the mean, with a bootstrap standard error rather than the naive one. Median excess kurtosis falls from 6.0 at one day to 3.4 at one week, 2.2 at one month and 1.3 at one quarter — a decisive decline, many bootstrapped standard errors wide, though still far from the normal's zero even after a quarter.

As a benchmark: pure 1/h1/h scaling from the one-day figure predicts roughly 6.0/5 ≈ 1.2 at a one-week horizon; the lab measures 3.4, distinctly higher — precisely because the shocks behind these returns are not independent. §5.1.3's volatility clustering keeps extra kurtosis alive well past what independent summation alone would predict, a preview of why Ding, Granger & Engle found real markets decline slower still.

The practical corollary cuts both ways. A desk that assumes normality for a one-day horizon is on thin ice — Session 3.2 — Distributions & Fat Tails already showed how badly the normal misprices a one-day tail. One that assumes it for a one-quarter or one-year holding period is on comparatively safer ground: the same aggregational Gaussianity that makes short-horizon normal models dangerous makes long-horizon ones far more defensible.

Real markets decline more slowly than this simple model predicts. Ding, Granger & Engle (1993) found that the autocorrelation of absolute returns in real equity data decays far more slowly than a plain GARCH(1,1) implies — closer to a "long memory" process than the geometric decay of §5.1.3 — which is exactly why GARCH persistence estimates on real data often sit uncomfortably close to 1, and why §5.5's model menu grows beyond a single GARCH(1,1).

🧭 Margin note — "closer to normal" is not "normal"

Aggregation shrinks excess kurtosis roughly like 1/h1/h, but a quarter is only 63 trading days — nowhere near the regime the central limit theorem describes asymptotically. Session 3.2 — Distributions & Fat Tails already showed how badly a normal misprices tail probabilities; the same caution applies here. Don't swap in a normal model for monthly or quarterly returns just because the kurtosis number looks smaller.

5.1.6 Where people go wrong

Two mistakes account for most of the bad conclusions drawn from an autocorrelation number.

SituationChooseWhy
Unexpected tick-level autocorrelationCheck for bid-ask bounce firstRoll (1984): quotes bouncing between bid and ask alone create negative lag-1 autocorrelation with zero information content
Statistically significant weekly/monthly index autocorrelationDon't assume it's tradableLo & MacKinlay (1988) found small but real index autocorrelation, largely explained by non-trading and microstructure, not a free lunch
"Returns are uncorrelated" in a write-upAsk which momentUncorrelated is a first-moment statement only; §5.1.3's squared returns show the second moment is very much predictable
⚠️ Pitfall — "uncorrelated returns are independent returns"

This is the single most consequential confusion in this session. A market can have EXACTLY zero return autocorrelation at every lag and still be far from random: GARCH itself is the counterexample, built by construction to have zero linear memory in returns and strong memory in their size. Independence is a much stronger statement than zero correlation, and volatility clustering, the leverage effect and the whole GARCH literature live entirely inside the gap between them.

None of this means autocorrelation is never tradable — short-horizon reversal and momentum strategies are real, published, and still traded by someone. It means the bar is higher than "I saw a number": a defensible claim needs a standard error that accounts for heteroskedasticity (§5.1.2's margin note), a look at whether the effect survives realistic costs, and — before either — a look at whether it survives out of the sample it was found in (Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle).

5.1.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Stylized facts — the empirical regularities in return data that are robust across almost every market and period (Cont 2001).
Autocorrelation — the correlation between a series and its own value kk periods earlier.
Volatility clustering — large moves, of either sign, tend to be followed by more large moves; calm and stormy periods persist.
Leverage effect — a negative return raises future volatility more than a positive return of the same size.
Aggregational Gaussianity — summing returns over longer horizons makes their distribution look steadily more normal.

Check yourself

1. A colleague reports "daily ACF(1) = 0.15 on this instrument, so returns aren't really random — I can trade this." What's the more likely reading?
On this session's own market, tested at a nominal 5% level, Ljung-Box actually rejects a GENUINELY zero autocorrelation on 61.5% of markets, because heteroskedasticity inflates its true size. An isolated significant ACF number is cheap even when nothing is predictable; it needs a robust standard error (§5.2) and a look at trading costs before it's a trade.
2. Why can a series have exactly zero autocorrelation at every lag and still not be independent?
GARCH returns are the counterexample: 𝔼[εt|past]=0\E[\varepsilon_t \mid \text{past}] = 0 makes linear correlation zero at every lag, but variance depends on the past, so squared and absolute returns are strongly autocorrelated. Uncorrelated is a weaker statement than independent — that gap is where volatility clustering lives.
3. Two candidate volatility models share the same total persistence, α+β(+γ/2)=0.98\alpha+\beta(+\gamma/2) = 0.98. One is symmetric, one asymmetric. Which fits an equity index better, and how would you tell without a chart?
Equity indices almost always show a reliably negative correlation between today's return and tomorrow's variance — the asymmetric model fits better. You'd tell by computing that correlation directly, or by fitting a GJR term and checking the sign and significance of γ\gamma; persistence alone can't distinguish the two models.
4. A trader claims 5-minute FX returns show a strong negative lag-1 autocorrelation, exploitable as an edge. What should you check first?
Bid-ask bounce (Roll 1984): quotes bouncing between the bid and the ask alone produce negative lag-1 autocorrelation with zero informational content. Check the size of the effect net of the spread and trading costs before believing it's a real edge.
5. If daily excess kurtosis is about 6 and daily shocks were independent, what excess kurtosis would you expect at a one-month (21-day) horizon — and why does the real decline tend to be slower than that?
Roughly 6/21≈0.36/21 \approx 0.3 under the 1/h1/h cumulant-scaling rule. The decline is slower in practice because returns aren't independent across days: Ding, Granger & Engle (1993) found the memory in absolute returns decays far more slowly than a plain GARCH(1,1) implies.
