Transaction Costs & Capacity

Every backtest reports a Sharpe ratio computed on the fill it assumed, not the fill it would actually get — and the gap between the two grows with the money behind the trade. This session turns commission, spread, market impact and borrow cost into a single P&L line, then asks the question that decides whether a strategy is a business or a curiosity: how much capital can it actually run? The core move: cost is not a fixed haircut — it grows with trade size, so a strategy's edge and its capacity are two different numbers.

6.5.1 The anatomy of a transaction cost

A backtest's Sharpe ratio is only as honest as its cost model — and "cost" is not one number. It is four different things a trade pays for, and only one of them shows up on a bill.

Every time a strategy trades, it pays a transaction cost: the gap between the price a backtest assumes it trades at and the price the strategy would actually realize, summed over everything a trade gives up beyond the quoted mid. Skip this line and every backtest is fiction, as Session 2.2 already showed for a single round trip: a strategy trading weekly lost roughly 4.5% a year to costs in a liquid stock, before it had earned anything. This session gives that line a name for every piece of it, then asks what happens as the money behind the trade grows.

The easiest piece to see is commission: the flat, explicit fee a broker or exchange charges per share or per dollar traded, independent of what the trade does to the price. Session 2.2 itemized a real bill for it — clearing fees, exchange fees, the SEC's Section 31 fee, FINRA's Trading Activity Fee — and found it was a small share of the total, about 1.4 of 9.1 basis points even in a liquid name. Commission does not depend on how much capital is behind the trade; a $1,000 order and a $100 million order pay the same rate per share.

The rest of the bill is invisible on any statement. Slippage is the gap between the price you decided to trade at and the price you actually got. Part of it is the fee for immediacy — the bid-ask spread that Session 2.1 introduced, which a market order pays half of just to trade now rather than wait. That session's book-walk already measured this rising with order size — about 2.0 basis points for a small order, 9.1 for a large one — and called it a first estimate. This session names the rest of that gap: market impact, the part of slippage you cause yourself, because your own order moves the price against you and other participants see you coming. It is the part of the bill that grows with the trade's size relative to the market's, and it is the piece that eventually caps how much money a strategy can run — the subject of most of this session. Session 14.3 derives the model properly and reviews the evidence for it; here you get the functional form and put it to work.

The fourth piece is different in kind: borrow cost, the fee paid to hold a trade rather than to make it. Shorting a stock means borrowing it first, and Session 2.2 already named the annual rate κ\kappa a lender charges for that loan, accrued daily on the position's value. A leveraged long position pays a cousin of the same thing — financing at roughly the risk-free rate for every dollar borrowed, which Session 2.3 built into its leverage arithmetic. Both are rate-based: they scale with how long and how large a position is held, not with how often it trades. Fig 6.5.1 lays out all four pieces side by side.

📌 Key idea — cost is not a haircut, it's a function

Treating cost as "a flat X basis points" is the single most common shortcut in a backtest, and it is wrong in a specific, predictable way: commission and spread are roughly flat per share, but market impact is not — it grows with the size of the trade relative to the market. A cost model that ignores that difference will look fine at the size you tested it at and quietly lie at every other size.

This is why costs belong in the same unit as the rest of backtesting discipline, not as an afterthought bolted on once a signal looks promising. A backtest with the wrong cost model is not slightly wrong the way a rounding error is wrong; it is wrong in the specific direction that makes a strategy look better the bigger it gets pitched, which is exactly backwards. The remaining sections build the model, then use it for the one question a naive backtest cannot answer honestly: how big can this actually get.

6.5.2 Building a cost-aware backtester

Charging a cost is a small addition to a backtester that already has positions and turnover — the discipline is charging it on the right line, at the right time, without peeking.

Session 6.2 owns the vectorized backtester itself — signal to position to return, with the shift discipline that keeps tomorrow's information out of today's trade. A cost model bolts onto the end of that pipeline at exactly one point: after positions are known, before returns are booked. Fig 6.5.2 shows where.

6.5.3 The math: cost, break-even cost and capacity

One formula for what a trade costs, one for the cost level that erases a strategy's edge outright, and one for the capital at which the first formula reaches the second.

Write the cost of trading one dollar, in basis points, as a flat part plus a part that grows with size. Let KK be the assumed capital, θday\theta_{\text{day}} the fraction of KK traded on a typical day, and ADV\text{ADV} the market's average daily dollar volume in the names traded. The flat part c0c_0 is commission plus half-spread; the size-sensitive part is a square-root-style impact term with a daily volatility σ\sigma and a dimensionless coefficient η\eta (a real desk calibrates η\eta from its own fills, per Session 14.3):

costbps(K)=c0+ησθdayKADV\text{cost}_{bps}(K) = c_0 + \eta\,\sigma\sqrt{\dfrac{\theta_{\text{day}}\,K}{\text{ADV}}}

Annualize by multiplying by how often the book turns over, θyr\theta_{\text{yr}} (in multiples of net asset value per year), and add the borrow cost on the fraction ss of capital held short at rate κ\kappa. That gives the annual cost drag and the net Sharpe ratio at capital KK, against the strategy's gross annualized excess return μgross\mu_{\text{gross}} and volatility σgross\sigma_{\text{gross}}:

drag(K)=θyrcostbps(K)×10−4+sκ,SRnet(K)=μgross−drag(K)σgross\text{drag}(K) = \theta_{\text{yr}}\,\text{cost}_{bps}(K)\times 10^{-4} + s\,\kappa, \qquad \SR_{\text{net}}(K) = \dfrac{\mu_{\text{gross}} - \text{drag}(K)}{\sigma_{\text{gross}}}

Two numbers follow immediately, and they answer two different questions. The break-even cost is the cost per dollar traded at which the strategy's edge is entirely consumed, at its actual turnover — a size-independent property of the strategy itself, useful for deciding whether it is worth building at any scale:

cost⋆=104(μgross−sκ)θyr\text{cost}^\star = \dfrac{10^4\left(\mu_{\text{gross}} - s\,\kappa\right)}{\theta_{\text{yr}}}

The size-dependent question is different: given the actual, size-sensitive cost model above, how much capital does it take to reach that break-even cost? Because drag(K)\text{drag}(K) is affine in K\sqrt{K} — write it as D0+D1KD_0 + D_1\sqrt{K}, with D0D_0 the flat fees plus borrow and D1D_1 collecting the impact model's sensitivity to size — the break-even capital has a closed form. That capital is this session's practical definition of capacity: the assumed capital at which a strategy's own square-root cost curve meets its break-even cost, and net Sharpe crosses zero.

drag(K)=D0+D1K,K⋆=(μgross−D0D1)2\text{drag}(K) = D_0 + D_1\sqrt{K}, \qquad K^\star = \left(\dfrac{\mu_{\text{gross}} - D_0}{D_1}\right)^{2}

Plugging in this session's strategy: the flat part is 0.0030 a year (commission, half-spread and borrow cost, none of which move with KK), and the impact sensitivity is D1=D_1 = 6.03e-07 per $\sqrt{\$} — a small number because it multiplies the square root of a number in the billions. Section 6.5.5 works this formula for a grid of capital levels and finds where it crosses zero; Section 6.5.7's exercise asks you to move ADV\text{ADV} and θyr\theta_{\text{yr}} yourself and predict which one moves K⋆K^\star more.

🧭 Margin note — bps of what, exactly

Every cost number in this session is basis points of the dollar amount traded, not of capital. To turn it into a drag on capital you always multiply by turnover — a cost that looks tiny per trade can still be a large annual drag if the book turns over often, and a cost that looks large can be harmless if the book barely trades. Section 6.5.5 puts real numbers on that trade-off.

6.5.4 In code

Three small functions carry the whole model: a cost per dollar traded, an annual drag, and the net Sharpe ratio they imply.

# cost per dollar traded, in bps: flat fees + a square-root impact term
def cost_bps(capital, turnover_annual=TURNOVER_ANNUAL):
    turnover_daily = turnover_annual / PERIODS_PER_YEAR
    participation = turnover_daily * capital / ADV_TOTAL
    impact = IMPACT_COEF * IMPACT_DAILY_VOL * 1e4 * np.sqrt(participation)
    return COMMISSION_BPS + HALF_SPREAD_BPS + impact

# annualised drag on capital, then the net Sharpe ratio it implies
def annual_drag(capital, turnover_annual=TURNOVER_ANNUAL):
    return turnover_annual * cost_bps(capital, turnover_annual) * 1e-4 + SHORT_FRAC * KAPPA

def net_sr(capital, turnover_annual=TURNOVER_ANNUAL):
    return (MU_GROSS_ANNUAL - annual_drag(capital, turnover_annual)) / SIGMA_ANNUAL

The strategy behind these numbers is a 150-name synthetic long-short book (20% true winners, 20% true losers, the rest no edge — planted and known, per synthetic data's whole point). Rebalanced daily back to fixed target weights, it measures a gross Sharpe ratio of 1.03 on 7.84% of annual excess return and 7.59% of volatility, turning its book over about 4.0 times a year — the turnover comes entirely from one day's price drift pulling the book away from its fixed targets, exactly as it would for a real constant-mix strategy. At $1,000,000,000 of assumed capital, the model prices each dollar traded at 50.5 basis points — 0.02% a year in commission, 0.08% in half-spread, 1.91% in market impact and 0.20% in borrow cost — for a total annual drag of 2.21% and a net Sharpe ratio of 0.74, down from the gross 1.03.

Two constants here are assumptions, not measurements, and it matters which is which. COMMISSION_BPS and ADV_TOTAL could both come from a real bill and a real market-data feed — a live desk would fit them from its own venue statements and volume history. IMPACT_COEF cannot: no fill has "market impact" printed on it, so it can only be inferred, by comparing executed prices to where the market would have gone without the order. That inference — and the honest range of values it actually produces across names and venues — is what Session 14.3 spends a full session on; this session's η=0.6\eta = 0.6 is a stylized placeholder chosen so the numbers above tell a clean story, not a fitted parameter.

6.5.5 Capacity: why Sharpe decays with capital

The strategy does not change as more money trades it. Only the cost does — and because it grows with the square root of size, Sharpe decays smoothly, then crosses zero.

Hold everything about the strategy fixed — same signal, same 20% winners and losers, same 4.0x annual turnover — and sweep only the assumed capital. Commission and half-spread barely move the number; market impact does all the work, because it alone grows with KK. Table 6.5.2 and fig 6.5.3 show the same sweep two ways.

Assumed capitalCost per dollar tradedNet Sharpe ratio
$100 million17.7 bp0.91
$1 billion50.5 bp0.74
$10 billion154.2 bp0.20
$30 billion265.2 bp−0.38

The break-even cost for this strategy is 192 basis points per dollar traded — the level at which its 7.84% gross edge, spread over 4.0 turns of the book, is entirely gone. The square-root impact model reaches that cost — and net Sharpe crosses zero — at a capacity of $15.6 billion. Beyond it, the strategy is not marginal, it is a loser: at $30 billion the model prices the same book at −0.38. Fig 6.5.4 breaks the $1 billion example back into its four lines, so the size-independent pieces (commission, spread, borrow) are visibly dwarfed by the one that grows.

This is a stylized number from a synthetic strategy, not a universal constant — a strategy's capacity depends on its turnover as much as on its edge. A low-turnover book like this one can run into the tens of billions before impact catches up; a high-turnover strategy trading the same edge would hit the same wall at a small fraction of the capital, because it pays the size-sensitive part of the bill many more times a year. That is exactly the shape of the real evidence: Korajczyk and Sadka (2004) put the break-even fund size for liquidity-weighted cross-sectional momentum — a strategy that trades far more often, and concentrates in less liquid recent movers — at around $5 billion, and Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) find that low-turnover styles such as size, value and profitability carry the most capacity of all the anomalies they test. Turnover, not just the size of the edge, sets the ceiling. Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz (2018), measuring costs from $1.7 trillion of a real institution's live trades, find realized impact an order of magnitude below what academic models like this section's predict — one reason is netting: a book running several signals at once lets offsetting buys and sells cancel internally before either ever reaches the market, so the effective turnover a single-strategy model like this one assumes overstates what a diversified desk actually pays.

The Monte Carlo check behind this section runs 4,000 independent 10-year paths of the strategy's daily excess return, charges each the same deterministic cost drag, and compares the average realized Sharpe ratio to the formula above. At a comfortably sub-capacity $500 million, theory predicts 0.815 and the simulated average comes in at 0.807 — 1.51 standard errors apart, comfortably inside the 4-SE bar this course holds every Monte Carlo claim to. Exactly at the break-even capital, the simulated average is −0.008, essentially zero as the formula predicts — but individual 10-year paths spread over a standard deviation of 0.32, and only 49% of them show a positive realized Sharpe ratio at all. Fig 6.5.5 shows that spread directly: 4,000 realized 10-year Sharpe ratios, centred almost exactly on zero.

🧭 Margin note — right at the ceiling, it's a coin flip

Near its own capacity, whether a decade of live trading "looks like" a winning strategy is mostly noise, not evidence one way or the other. A single good decade at $15 billion tells you almost nothing about whether the true edge survives at that size — you would need many more years of track record than any fund actually has to tell a lucky path from a real one this close to break-even.

6.5.6 The flat-cost mistake

The most common way a backtest lies about capacity is also the most forgivable: it calibrates cost once, at a small size, and never revisits the assumption.

A strategy first trades a modest pilot allocation — say $20 million — and its live fills show a cost of 9.3 basis points per dollar traded. That number goes into the backtest as a constant, because at $20 million it is a constant: market impact is a small share of the bill at that size. The mistake is assuming it stays constant as the pitch grows to billions.

SituationDo thisWhy
Reporting a backtest's Sharpe ratioSay what capital it assumes, alwaysa Sharpe ratio without a capital figure is not comparable to anything
Comparing two strategies' raw edgeCompare break-even cost, not Sharpeit strips out the capital-dependent part of the story
Pitching a strategy at scaleShow a Sharpe-versus-capital curve, not one numbercapacity is a curve; a point estimate hides where it bends

Applying the pilot's flat 9.3 basis points at $15 billion gives a net Sharpe ratio of 0.96 — barely different from the gross number, because a flat rate never charges more as size grows. The size-aware model, at the same $15 billion, gives 0.02: essentially the strategy's entire capacity, spent. Nothing about the strategy changed between those two numbers. Only the honesty of the cost model did. Novy-Marx and Velikov's single most effective fix costs nothing to model: require a stronger signal to open a new position than to keep one already held (a "buy/hold spread"). It does not change the impact formula at all — it simply stops paying it on trades that were never worth making.

⚠️ Pitfall — "our backtest's Sharpe is 1.0, so it scales"

A Sharpe ratio measured (or backtested) at one size says nothing about a different size, because cost is not part of the strategy — it is a function of how much of the market's volume the strategy has to eat. A pitch deck that quotes one Sharpe number for "however much capital you want to give us" has, whether it knows it or not, quietly assumed a flat-cost model. Ask what capital the number assumes, and ask to see the curve.

6.5.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Transaction cost — everything a trade gives up beyond the quoted mid price: explicit fees plus every implicit cost of getting the trade done.
Commission — the flat, explicit fee a broker or exchange charges per share or dollar traded, independent of the trade's effect on price.
Slippage — the gap between the price a trade was decided at and the price it actually achieved, from both the cost of immediacy and the trade's own market impact.
Market impact — the part of slippage caused by the trade itself moving the price; here modelled as growing with the square root of trade size relative to market volume.
Borrow cost — the fee paid to borrow shares for a short sale, charged daily on the position's value for as long as it is held.
Capacity — the assumed capital at which a strategy's size-sensitive costs consume its entire edge; the practical ceiling on how much money it can run.
Break-even cost — the cost per dollar traded at which a strategy's edge is entirely consumed, independent of how much capital trades it.

Check yourself

1. A fund pays zero commission on every trade but still underperforms its own paper portfolio by several percentage points a year. Where did the cost go?
Into the invisible lines: half the spread on every fill, plus market impact from the fund's own order flow. Commission is often the smallest line on the bill (§6.5.1); "zero commission" says nothing about the other three.
2. Two strategies trade the same signal and the same average trade size, but one turns its book over 2 times a year and the other 20 times. Which one's Sharpe ratio is more sensitive to switching from a flat cost assumption to a size-aware one, and why?
The 20x strategy. It pays the size-sensitive part of the bill ten times as often per year, so any error in modelling how that part scales with size is multiplied by ten before it hits annual drag — and reaching a high daily participation rate (and so a high impact cost) takes far less capital when the book turns over that fast.
3. A strategy has a gross annual excess return of 9%, turns its book over 6 times a year, and holds no shorts. Roughly what cost per dollar traded would erase its edge entirely?
Using cost* = 10⁴·μ_gross / θ_yr from §6.5.3: 10,000 × 0.09 / 6 ≈ 150 basis points. Above that average round-trip cost, the strategy is not marginal at some size — it loses money at every size.
4. An investor wants to allocate $2 billion to a strategy whose only published track record is a $50 million backtest with a Sharpe ratio of 1.2. Should they expect the same Sharpe at $2 billion?
Not without a capacity analysis. Market impact grows with the square root of capital, and $50 million may sit well below where impact matters at all — the honest answer is a Sharpe-versus-capital curve (§6.5.5), not an extrapolation of one number forty times the size it was measured at.
5. Your backtest charges a flat 5 basis points on every trade, calibrated from a $10 million pilot. You're asked how the strategy performs at $5 billion. What's wrong with just running the same backtest with more capital?
The flat rate was measured where market impact was negligible; it silently omits the size-sensitive cost entirely. The backtest will report an unchanged Sharpe ratio at any size (§6.5.6) — precisely the naive mistake this session names — when the true cost, and therefore the true Sharpe, both move with capital.

2. Pick a real, published strategy description (a fund's marketing deck, a paper's abstract) and estimate its rough turnover. Using this session's break-even cost formula and a guessed gross Sharpe, sanity-check whether the strategy could plausibly run at the AUM the source claims.
