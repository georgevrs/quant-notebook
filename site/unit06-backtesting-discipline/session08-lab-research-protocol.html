Lab: The Research Protocol

Sessions 6.1–6.7 gave you a backtester, a bias hall of fame and the statistics to price in how many things you tried. This lab wires them into one small, real pipeline: pre-register a hypothesis, sweep it with vectorbt, log every trial to MLflow, score the log with PBO and DSR, and write the memo that says go or kill it. The core move: let a rule fixed in advance, not your own hopeful reading of the winner, make the call.

6.8.1 The mental model: three durable artefacts

A research protocol is not a mindset — it is three physical documents that outlive the researcher's memory of what happened.

Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle already gave you the discipline this lab industrialises: write the hypothesis and the kill criterion before touching the data. That works for one experiment, checked by one honest person. It stops working the moment a second person joins, six months pass, or the sweep has 45 variants instead of one — nobody's memory of "what we tried" survives that. A research protocol replaces memory with three artefacts that anyone, including you in six months, can re-open and check: the pre-registration (what you committed to), the trial log (what you actually ran), and the go/no-go memo (what you decided, and why). This lab builds all three, for real, twice — once on a synthetic sleeve with a genuine edge, once on pure noise — and lets the identical pipeline decide both.

Without this, a research desk drifts toward exactly the failure mode Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame catalogued: try things informally, remember the ones that worked, forget the rest, and call the survivor a discovery. With it, the count of things tried is a number in a database, not a guess — and that number is precisely what Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO's statistics need to price in how impressed you should be.

The engineering analogy is close enough to lean on directly: a research protocol is continuous integration for a hypothesis. The pre-registration is the spec, written before a single test result exists, so "passing" is defined before anyone has a number to feel good or bad about. The trial log is the build log — every run kept in the same place with the same fields, whether it looked promising or not, exactly the way a CI system keeps every job's output rather than only the ones that went green. The go/no-go memo is the release note: a short document that says what shipped, what didn't, and why, filed either way so the next person doesn't have to re-run the whole pipeline just to find out what happened last time. None of the three pieces is new engineering. What's new — for most research desks — is refusing to skip any of them because "it's just a backtest."

📌 Key idea — remove judgement from the parts that don't need it

A protocol works by deciding, in advance, exactly what gets logged and exactly what rule scores it — so that the only judgement call left on decision day is one you already made before you knew the answer.

6.8.2 Pre-registering the hypothesis and the kill criterion

The pre-registration fixes four things before the sweep runs: the hypothesis, how many variants you're allowed, the primary metric, and the exact rule that kills the idea.

This lab's pre-registration (R-014, continuing the numbering Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle started) commits to a small sleeve of 40 synthetic instruments, a single parameter sweep of 45 moving-average crossovers, and — this is the part worth reading twice — 20 years of in-sample history before any decision is made, plus 8 frozen holdout years touched exactly once. That is a long commitment for what looks like a simple crossover rule, and §6.8.2's own numbers below explain why it has to be.

FieldWhat this run committed to
Hypothesisthe sleeve's daily returns carry positive short-lag autocorrelation a crossover can harvest
Universe40 synthetic instruments, equal-weighted
Data20y in-sample + 8y frozen holdout
Primary metricannualised Sharpe ratio, risk-free rate 0
Variants allowed45 (fast, slow) pairs, one grid, no re-fitting
Kill criteriongo only if every one of three gates passes (below)

The kill criterion itself is a conjunction, not a single number to eyeball: go only if the deflated Sharpe ratio clears 0.95, the probability of backtest overfitting stays under 0.50, and a holdout Sharpe ratio — touched exactly once — comes back positive.

GO⟺DSR≥0.95 and PBO<0.50 and SRholdout>0\text{GO} \iff \mathrm{DSR} \ge 0.95 \ \text{and}\ \mathrm{PBO} \lt 0.50 \ \text{and}\ \mathrm{SR}_{\text{holdout}} \gt 0

The three gates are not redundant — each one catches a failure the other two would miss. DSR catches optimism from trying many parameter combinations at once, the multiple-testing problem a single Sharpe ratio can't see past. PBO catches something subtler: mechanical overfitting built into the shape of the search, visible only by re-splitting the same data many ways and asking how often the in-sample winner is merely average out of sample. Neither of those touches the one thing only a frozen holdout can show — whether the relationship the whole grid was tuned to find is still there on years the sweep never got to see. A rule that dropped any one gate would be blind to exactly the failure mode the other two were never built to catch.

Why 20 years for what is, mechanically, a simple crossover? Because a modest, entirely real edge needs a long track record before Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO's deflated statistic will believe it — the same arithmetic behind that session's minimum track record length, checked here directly by simulation rather than the closed-form formula. Running the exact three-gate rule above 400 times on a synthetic sleeve with a genuine annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.9 and 45 trials in the grid, the rule says go only 31% of the time with 3 years of history, but 91% of the time with 8 and 90% with 20 (fig 6.8.4). Three years is not stingy — it is simply not enough data for an honest rule to trust a real edge.

💡 Looking ahead

Under pure noise, the same three-gate check calls a go only 0.2% of the time across 400 repeats (§6.8.7's Try-it lets you push the thresholds and watch both rates move) — a conservative rule, by design, because Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO already showed you how expensive a false go is.

6.8.3 The trial log: what MLflow keeps that memory doesn't

A trial log is a database row per attempt — not a notebook cell you happened to keep, and not your recollection of "the good one."

Experiment tracking is the practice this session owns: recording every trial's exact parameters, the metrics that resulted, and enough provenance to reproduce it, so that nobody ever has to reconstruct "what did we try" from memory or a scattered folder of notebooks. This lab uses MLflow (already installed) for it, logging one run per (fast, slow) pair before any result is inspected. A trial log is the artefact that results in: a table, one row per trial, that answers "what happened" without asking a person.

In code, logging one trial is three lines — a parameter block, a metric block, both inside one run:

# one MLflow run per (fast, slow) pair, before the winner is even known
with mlflow.start_run(run_name=f"momentum_f{fast}_s{slow}"):
    mlflow.log_params({"project": project, "fast": fast, "slow": slow})
    mlflow.log_metrics({"sharpe_in_sample": sr_is, "n_trades_sleeve": n_trades})

Read back with mlflow.search_runs(...), the log for the momentum sleeve returns exactly 45 rows — matching 45 pre-registered, not 44 or 46 — and its best in-sample row is the (fast, slow) pair 5/8, trading 18,236 times across the sleeve over the full 28 years. Every one of those three numbers came from the log itself, not from a variable this notebook happened to still have in memory.

This is also why a trial log outlives the sweep that created it. Once a strategy moves past a go decision, the same log-then-query habit continues under a different name: a model registry tracks which model version is live, when it was promoted, and what it scored before promotion — Session 15.5 — Operations & MLOps owns that discipline in production. A research trial log and a live model registry are the same idea at two points in a strategy's life: nothing ships, or stays shipped, on the strength of someone's memory of how it performed.

🧭 Margin note — the honour-system spreadsheet

The alternative most researchers actually use is a spreadsheet someone updates when they remember to. It works until a trial goes unlogged because it "obviously" wasn't going to win — which is exactly the trial that turns out to matter when Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO's statistics ask how many you really tried.

6.8.4 The parameter sweep, with vectorbt

A parameter sweep runs one rule many ways at once; vectorbt is built to do that across a whole grid in a single call instead of a Python loop.

A parameter sweep is trying every combination in a fixed grid of one strategy's settings — here, all 45 pairs from a 10-window ladder (C(10, 2)), long/flat on a close-vs-moving-average crossover. Sessions Session 6.2 — Build a Vectorized Backtester and Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO hand-rolled this kind of loop in plain pandas; vectorbt instead broadcasts price, signal and parameter grid into one array job that a single call runs for every instrument and every (fast, slow) pair together:

# every (fast, slow) pair from the window ladder, on all 40 instruments, one call
fast_ma, slow_ma = vbt.MA.run_combs(price, window=WINDOWS, r=2, short_names=["fast", "slow"])
entries = fast_ma.ma_crossed_above(slow_ma)
exits = fast_ma.ma_crossed_below(slow_ma)
pf = vbt.Portfolio.from_signals(price, entries, exits, fees=0.0005, freq="D")

That speed is why the sweep needs a trial log at all: a hand-timed loop makes you feel every trial go by, but a vectorised sweep returns 45 answers before you have looked at one, which is exactly when it is easiest to only remember the winner. The flat 0.0005 above is a placeholder cost so turnover isn't free — Session 6.5 — Transaction Costs & Capacity owns the full transaction-cost model this lab doesn't attempt. (vectorbt is fast and free to use, but its Commons Clause licence forbids selling a product built on it — read it before you ship anything on top.)

Both projects below go through this identical call. Momentum pilot is 40 synthetic instruments built with a known, exaggerated daily autocorrelation of 0.25 — measured back out of the panel at 0.251 (standard error 0.0019, so within four standard errors of the truth we built in, the same "know the truth first" method Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame uses for every exhibit). Noise null is the identical construction with the autocorrelation set to exactly zero — a fair game, same as Session 6.1 — What a Backtest Is (and Isn't)'s baseline. Sweeping both and comparing each project's best in-sample Sharpe ratio against its own frozen holdout makes the honesty gap visible before any formal score is computed:

Forty instruments, not one, for a reason worth being explicit about: averaging the crossover's return across an equal-weighted sleeve removes each individual instrument's idiosyncratic luck from the number the sweep reports, the same reason Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame tests survivorship on 5,000 names rather than one. It does not, and cannot, remove the other kind of uncertainty — how much a Sharpe ratio estimated over a fixed number of calendar years can vary from the truth — which is exactly why §6.8.2's calibration turns on years, not on how many instruments are in the sleeve. Diversifying the universe and lengthening the history fix two different problems; a protocol that only does the first can still be fooled.

vectorbt's speed here is not free of trade-offs. It assumes signals translate into fills without the order-book mechanics Session 6.3 — Build an Event-Driven Backtester's event loop models explicitly — a fine trade for a first pass at 45 variants, a bad one for anything that will trade close to its own capacity limit.

Momentum pilot's winner keeps almost all of it: an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.63 against a holdout Sharpe ratio of 1.63. Noise null's winner does not: 0.42 in-sample collapses to 0.05 on data it never touched.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the best in-sample Sharpe ratio is a real number"

It is a real number — of the single best-looking cell in a grid you swept 45 ways. Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame and Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle already showed what a naive best-of-N figure does to your confidence; a sweep is that exact mechanism, run once per parameter pair instead of once per random seed. The fix is never to stop sweeping — it is to never report the winner's number without first scoring the whole log, which is §6.8.5's job.

6.8.5 Scoring honestly: PBO and DSR, read off the log

The deflated Sharpe ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting are Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO's statistics; this section's only new move is reading their inputs straight off the trial log instead of trusting a remembered trial count.

Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO derives the probabilistic Sharpe ratio, the deflated Sharpe ratio and CSCV's probability of backtest overfitting from first principles — this lab does not repeat that derivation, only uses it. The one detail worth making explicit: DSR's trial count N is not typed in by hand. It is len(mlflow.search_runs(...)) — the exact row count of the log built in §6.8.3, which is why an unlogged trial is not a shortcut, it is a wrong answer to a question DSR asks with certainty.

ProjectTrials loggedDSRPBOHoldout SharpeDecision
Momentum pilot451.000.001.63GO
Noise null450.400.240.05NO-GO

Notice what noise null's row shows: its holdout Sharpe ratio of 0.05 is barely positive — a naive rater who only checked the holdout might have shipped it. DSR does not agree: at 0.40, it is nowhere near the 0.95 bar, because it correctly prices in that this was the best of 45 attempts, not the only one made. This is exactly why the kill criterion is a conjunction of three gates rather than a vote: a single flattering signal — even a real one, like a positive holdout — never overrides the other two.

📌 Key idea — PBO and DSR are read off the same log, not a separate analysis

Nothing about scoring the sweep needs information the trial log doesn't already have: PBO's Session 6.6 — Validation: Walk-Forward, Purged K-Fold & CPCV-adjacent block splits and DSR's trial count both come out of the identical rows §6.8.3 logged. Scoring honestly is a query, not a new experiment.

6.8.6 The go/no-go memo

A go/no-go memo is the pre-registration, the trial-log summary and the decision, assembled without adding anything after the fact.

The go/no-go memo is this session's last artefact: one document, filed whether the answer is go or kill, that a reader who wasn't in the room can audit line by line — every field traces back to either the pre-registration or the trial log, never to a fresh judgement made once the numbers were in. It is a commit message, not a hallway conversation: a hallway "yeah, this one looked good, let's run it" carries no trial count, no bar, no holdout figure, and disappears the moment everyone involved changes teams. A memo survives that. Momentum pilot's memo, filled in from this run:

Noise null goes through the identical template and comes out the other way: 45 trials logged, winning variant fast=130/slow=180, DSR 0.40 against a 0.95 bar — NO-GO. The memo still gets filed and archived, not deleted: it is the record that stops anyone re-running the identical idea next quarter with a new seed, hoping for a kinder verdict, which is Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame's data-snooping pitfall arriving one calendar quarter at a time instead of one seed at a time.

A go is not a finish line. Once live, the same strategy is protected by an operational kill switch (Session 15.4 — Pre-Trade Risk & Kill Switches — a different, faster circuit-breaker than this session's statistical kill criterion) and is retired by the same kind of pre-committed discipline in Session 9.9 — Performance, Attribution & When to Switch Off, which owns strategy retirement in full.

6.8.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Trial log — a durable, queryable record of every attempt in a sweep: its parameters, its metrics and enough provenance to reproduce it, kept so nobody has to reconstruct "what did we try" from memory.
Parameter sweep — running one strategy specification across every combination in a fixed grid of its settings, mechanically, without re-fitting the grid itself partway through.
Experiment tracking — the practice (here, via MLflow) of logging every trial's parameters, metrics and provenance automatically, so the trial log is a byproduct of running the sweep, not a separate chore.
Go/no-go memo — a one-document decision record, filed whether the answer is go or kill, whose every field traces back to the pre-registration or the trial log rather than a post-hoc judgement call.

Check yourself

1. Your sweep's best in-sample Sharpe ratio is 1.6 and the holdout Sharpe ratio is also a strong 1.6 — surely that is a real edge? Then you notice the whole sweep only used 2 years of history. Should you trust it?
Not on that basis alone. §6.8.2's own calibration shows that even a genuine annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.9 clears the pre-registered bar only a minority of the time with just a few years of data and 45 trials in the grid — a good-looking holdout number from a short history is exactly the kind of point-luck the DSR gate exists to catch, not a substitute for it.
2. A colleague wants to add a 46th (fast, slow) pair to the grid after seeing which of the original 45 looked best. What's wrong with that, specifically?
It silently changes N, the trial count DSR deflates by, without changing what the trial log says N is — unless the new trial is logged too, the log now undercounts how many things were actually tried. It is 6.4's data-snooping pitfall, just arriving one grid cell at a time instead of one random seed at a time.
3. Six months from now, a teammate asks exactly how many parameter combinations this project tried. What does the protocol answer with, and why does the precise number matter?
The row count of mlflow.search_runs(...) for that project's experiment — an exact count, not a recollection of "a bunch." DSR's expected-best-of-N correction is a direct function of that count, so an approximate answer produces a wrong deflation, not just an imprecise one.
4. Noise null's winning variant showed a holdout Sharpe ratio of 0.05 — technically positive. Why did the memo still say NO-GO?
Because the kill criterion is a conjunction of three gates, and DSR (0.40, against a 0.95 bar) failed on its own: a single positive signal, even a real one, never overrides a statistic that has already priced in how many variants were tried. One weakly favourable number out of three required is still a kill.
5. Why does the noise null memo get filed and kept, rather than just deleted since the idea failed?
A kept NO-GO memo is the record that stops the same idea being re-run next quarter with a new random seed in the hope of a kinder verdict — the research-protocol equivalent of refusing to seed-hunt. Deleting it would let exactly that happen invisibly.
