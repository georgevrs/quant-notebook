Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO

A backtest's Sharpe ratio is a lucky guess until you price in how many you tried, how long you watched, and how it was chosen. This session builds four numbers that do exactly that pricing — the probabilistic Sharpe ratio, the minimum track record length, the deflated Sharpe ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting — and proves each one from its published formula. The core move: a Sharpe ratio without its trial count is not a number, it is a claim.

6.7.1 A Sharpe ratio is an estimate, not a fact

Every reported Sharpe ratio is one noisy draw. This section turns that draw into a probability, using the shape of the returns themselves — not just their average.

Session 5.2 — Regression & Inference That Doesn't Lie already gave you the standard error of the Sharpe ratio: under Lo's (2002) assumptions (returns close to normal, independent across time), an annualised Sharpe ratio estimated from 5 years of daily data carries real sampling noise. The probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR) generalises that one step further: instead of assuming normal returns, it asks the question directly — given exactly what this track record's returns look like, what is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio clears a benchmark SR∗\SR^* (often 0)? All of this session's returns are synthetic excess returns; the risk-free rate is taken as 0 throughout.

Bailey & López de Prado (2012) give the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratio estimator as a function of the sample's own skewness and kurtosis — raw kurtosis, in Session 3.2's convention, so a normal distribution scores 3, not 0. Writing SRˆ\widehat{\SR} for the estimated per-period Sharpe ratio and TT for the number of periods:

PSRˆ(SR∗)=Φ((SRˆ−SR∗)T1−skew(R)SRˆ+kurt(R)−14SRˆ2)\widehat{PSR}(\SR^*) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{(\widehat{\SR}-\SR^*)\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{1 - \text{skew}(R)\,\widehat{\SR} + \frac{\text{kurt}(R)-1}{4}\widehat{\SR}^2}}\right)

where Φ\Phi is the standard normal CDF. Set skew(R)=0\text{skew}(R)=0 and kurt(R)=3\text{kurt}(R)=3 (a normal distribution) and the denominator becomes (1+SRˆ2/2)/T\sqrt{(1+\widehat{\SR}^2/2)/T} — exactly Session 5.2's Lo (2002) formula. The lab checks this line by line: Lo's formula gives a per-period standard error of 0.0257, and PSR's own formula at skew=0, kurt=3\text{skew}=0,\ \text{kurt}=3 gives 0.0257 — identical, because it is the same formula wearing a more general coat. Probabilistic Sharpe ratio is the name for that coat: a Z-score run through the normal CDF, using the sample's own skewness and kurtosis instead of assuming them away.

PSR reads like a Bayesian statement — "the probability the true Sharpe ratio exceeds zero" — but the calculation behind it is entirely frequentist: it treats the estimator SRˆ\widehat{\SR} as approximately normal around the unknown true Sharpe ratio, with the standard deviation the formula above computes, and asks how much of that normal curve sits above SR∗\SR^*. It is the same plug-in logic as any Z-test; the honest reading is "a track record this good would arise this often under the null," dressed in more useful language. PSR is not a substitute for asking whether the strategy's story makes sense in the first place — Session 1.3's three questions (who pays you, why, and why does it persist) come first, and no amount of statistical confidence repairs a strategy with no answer to them.

One honestly-reported track record makes this concrete: 5 years of daily returns from quantnb.synth.jump_diffusion_returns (Session 3.2's compensated-crash generator — rare down-jumps, drift adjusted so the expected growth rate is unaffected), calibrated to a true annual Sharpe of 0.6. The sample shows an annualised Sharpe of 0.12 (five years is not a lot of data — §6.7.2 below quantifies exactly how little), skewness −0.85 and kurtosis 6.09 — visibly negative and visibly fat, the shape a strategy with occasional bad days actually has. Its own PSR(0) is 60.29%; assuming (wrongly) that its returns were normal would have reported 60.32% instead — barely different, because at daily frequency SRˆ\widehat{\SR} (the per-period figure) is tiny, and both the skewness and the SRˆ2\widehat{\SR}^2 term it multiplies are small next to the "1" in the square root.

Reporting frequencyPeriods (T)PSR, this shapePSR, if normalGap
Daily1,26098.5%98.7%0.2%

That table holds this strategy's measured shape fixed and asks only how the correction's bite changes if the same 5-year span were instead rated from monthly or annual observations (fewer, coarser periods, same shape) — an illustration of the formula's own sensitivity, not a claim about how real returns actually aggregate. At daily frequency the correction rounds to nothing; at annual frequency, confidence drops from a comfortable 97% under the (wrong) normal assumption to a shakier 90% once the real shape counts. This is exactly why Bailey & López de Prado recommend rating a track record at the highest frequency the data allows: it makes the normal approximation nearly exact.

This creates a perverse incentive, because coarser reporting is also the industry norm — chosen for readability, not statistics. A fund that only ever shows monthly or annual numbers needs a much larger true edge before the same PSR is honest, precisely because it has denied itself the frequency that would make the normal approximation safe. When you can choose your own reporting frequency, use the highest one your data supports; when you can't — an annual letter is all a manager will show you — treat the confidence on offer as optimistic until you see the number recomputed at a finer grain.

📌 Key idea — PSR is a probability, not a p-value trick

PSR(0) is literally "P(true Sharpe ratio > 0), given this track record." It is not a hypothesis-test p-value in disguise, and it is not corrected for anything you tried beside this one track record — that correction is §6.7.3's job. PSR alone answers "is this one number believable"; it cannot answer "how many numbers did you look at before choosing this one."

6.7.2 How it works: pricing in the checks — and how long is long enough

Four statistics, four different questions. Before the formulas, the map of which one you reach for and why.

StatisticNeedsAnswers
PSRone track record (T, skewness, kurtosis)is the true Sharpe ratio above a benchmark?
MinTRLa target Sharpe ratio and a confidence levelhow long a track record would it take to prove it?
DSRPSR's inputs, plus N independent trials…once you price in how many things you tried?
PBO (via CSCV)many parameterisations of one rule on one datasetwould the in-sample winner have looked average out of sample?

Read that table against Session 1.3's research lifecycle. PSR and MinTRL are checks you can run on a single, pre-registered idea before it has competed with anything else — they belong at the validation gate even for N=1. DSR and PBO only make sense once a search has actually happened, which in practice is nearly always: a lookback window tuned, a threshold nudged, a universe narrowed. "Which one do I compute" usually reduces to "was there a search, and can I count it (DSR) or only resample it (PBO)?"

Start with minimum track record length (MinTRL): the length of history, in periods, that a claimed Sharpe ratio SRˆ\widehat{\SR} would need before PSRˆ(SR∗)\widehat{PSR}(\SR^*) reaches a chosen confidence 1−α1-\alpha. It is PSR's own formula, solved for TT instead of for a probability:

MinTRLˆ(SR∗)=(1−skew(R)SRˆ+kurt(R)−14SRˆ2)(z1−αSRˆ−SR∗)2\widehat{MinTRL}(\SR^*) = \left(1 - \text{skew}(R)\,\widehat{\SR} + \frac{\text{kurt}(R)-1}{4}\widehat{\SR}^2\right)\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\widehat{\SR}-\SR^*}\right)^2

At skew=0, kurt=3\text{skew}=0,\ \text{kurt}=3 this is exactly Session 5.2's years_needed — the lab checks it to eleven decimal places (10.8275 years against 10.8275 years). Fed this session's measured negative skew and fat tails instead, the same target Sharpe needs visibly more patience:

MinTRL is a critical value, not a promise: it is built so a track record of exactly that length turns PSRˆ(0)=1−α\widehat{PSR}(0) = 1-\alpha if the observed Sharpe ratio lands right on the target. Equivalently, a skill-less manager (true Sharpe 0) measured for MinTRL(SR∗)\text{MinTRL}(\SR^*) periods should show an estimate at or above SR∗\SR^* only α\alpha of the time — the lab's Monte Carlo check confirms it: 5.0% of 20,000 skill-less five-year-plus tracks cleared the bar, against a target of 5%. This is the same logic as the size of any hypothesis test: MinTRL is chosen so a manager with no real skill is wrongly declared skilled no more than α\alpha of the time, whatever their true (unknown) Sharpe ratio actually is.

💡 Tip — use MinTRL before you start, not after

Run MinTRL at the design stage: "if this strategy really has a Sharpe of 0.5, I need about 11 years to know it." If you do not have that much history and cannot get it (a new exchange, a new instrument), you have learned something important before spending months on the idea — this is the same discipline as Session 1.3's pre-registration, applied to sample-size planning.

6.7.3 Deflating for the number of trials

PSR trusts the one track record in front of it. DSR asks the harder question: what if this is the best of many?

Session 1.3 proved this by simulation: try enough skill-less strategies and keep the best, and the winner's in-sample Sharpe ratio grows with N even though nothing was ever there. The exact result behind that demo is the False Strategy Theorem (López de Prado & Bailey, 2021): the expected value of the best Sharpe ratio among NN skill-less trials, each estimated with per-trial standard deviation σˆN\hat\sigma_N, is approximately

SR0=σˆN[(1−γ)Φ−1(1−1N)+γΦ−1(1−1Ne)]\SR_0 \;=\; \hat\sigma_N\left[(1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1-\tfrac1N\Big) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1-\tfrac1{Ne}\Big)\right]

with γ≈0.577\gamma \approx 0.577 the Euler–Mascheroni constant (unrelated to skewness or kurtosis — a coincidence of Greek letters, not a shared meaning). SR0\SR_0 is not a guess about any one trial; it is what the best of N honestly skill-less trials would show, on average. Intuitively, SR0\SR_0 grows the way the maximum of N draws from any roughly bell-shaped distribution grows: slowly, on the order of 2ln⁡N\sqrt{2\ln N}, because pushing the sample maximum further into the tail gets exponentially harder the further out it already is. That slow growth does real work in the table below: going from 1 trial to 10 moves the deflation benchmark by nearly half of the entire range between 1 and 1,000 trials, while quadrupling 50 to 200 barely moves it at all. The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) is simply PSR evaluated at that benchmark instead of at SR∗=0\SR^*=0:

DSRˆ=PSRˆ(SR0)\widehat{DSR} \;=\; \widehat{PSR}(\SR_0)

Bailey & López de Prado (2014) built DSR exactly to answer the question a raw Sharpe ratio cannot: is this the best of N because it is skilled, or because N was large? The one number DSR needs beyond a normal PSR calculation is the number of trials — how many genuinely independent things were tried before this one was reported. A grid search over 50 lookback windows for one signal is not 50 independent trials in DSR's sense: consecutive lookbacks produce highly correlated Sharpe ratios, so the effective count could be closer to 5 or 10. This is Session 3.3's effective number of independent bets, applied to trials instead of assets — correlation shrinks how much independent evidence N numbers actually carry, and undercounting it in the optimistic direction is the easiest way to make DSR claim more confidence than the search earned. Estimating that "effective N" honestly, and logging it as you go, is Session 6.8 — Lab: The Research Protocol's job.

The lab reruns Session 1.3's experiment — N skill-less strategies, 5 years, 200 repeats, keep the best in-sample Sharpe — but this time computes both statistics on the winner. A rater who only sees the raw number, or even PSR alone, is fooled exactly as N grows; DSR is not:

Trials (N)Best in-sample SharpeNaive PSRDeflation benchmark SR0Deflated Sharpe ratio

Naive PSR climbs from an unremarkable 52% at N=1 to a seemingly airtight 100% at N=1,000. DSR barely moves — it sits within a couple of points of 52% at every N in the table, because SR0\SR_0 grows to absorb exactly the inflation that a larger search manufactures. A best-of-1,000 Sharpe ratio of 1.46 is, once deflated, statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip.

DSR is not a blanket penalty on ambition, though — it is a penalty on unreported trials. The same measured track record can score very differently depending on what actually happened before it:

A strategy with a real annual Sharpe ratio of 0.9, tested honestly among 5 pre-declared variants, keeps a DSR of 70% on average. Report the identical track record as the best of 1,000 trials — a lie of omission, not a different strategy — and the same numbers deflate to 19%. Nothing about the strategy changed. Only the honesty of the reported search did.

Notice what did not change between the honest and dishonest reports: the same 1,260 days of returns, the same skewness, the same kurtosis, the same measured Sharpe ratio. The only input that differed was N — a number that lives entirely in the researcher's notebook, not in the data. That is exactly why a trial log is not paperwork: it is the one piece of evidence that makes DSR computable at all, and the one thing a raw Sharpe ratio can never disclose on its own.

🧭 Margin note — DSR needs a number N; PBO does not

DSR's SR0\SR_0 comes from extreme-value theory applied to N independent Sharpe ratio estimates. A grid of moving-average lookbacks fit to one price series is not that: the same days are reused by every parameterisation, so there is no clean N to plug in. §6.7.4 covers exactly that case.

6.7.4 When you don't know N: PBO and CSCV

A parameter sweep on one dataset is the most common research pattern of all — and DSR cannot score it.

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2016) built combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) for exactly this case: many parameterisations of one rule, fit and judged on the same finite sample. Split the sample into SS contiguous blocks (even SS), and form every way of splitting those blocks into two equal halves — (SS/2)\binom{S}{S/2} combinations in all. In each combination, one half is in-sample, the other out-of-sample.

Why not just hold out one block, the way Session 6.6's walk-forward analysis does? Because with a single split, which half happened to be called "out-of-sample" is itself an arbitrary choice, and a rule that looks lucky on one split can look unlucky on another purely by chance. CSCV's combinatorial symmetry removes that arbitrariness: every block plays both roles across the full set of combinations, so PBO reflects the selection procedure itself rather than one lucky or unlucky slice of the calendar.

For each combination cc, the rule with the best in-sample Sharpe ratio is found, then its rank among all NN rules' out-of-sample Sharpe ratios is converted to a relative rank and a logit:

ωc=rankcN+1,λc=ln⁡ωc1−ωc\omega_c = \frac{\text{rank}_c}{N+1}, \qquad \lambda_c = \ln\!\frac{\omega_c}{1-\omega_c}

λc≤0\lambda_c \le 0 means the in-sample winner did no better than the out-of-sample median — as bad an outcome as picking a rule at random. The probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) is the fraction of combinations where that happens:

PBOˆ=1|C|∑c∈C𝟙[λc≤0]\widehat{PBO} = \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{c \in C} \mathbb{1}[\lambda_c \le 0]

The lab runs this on a genuine parameter sweep: 25 moving-average crossover rules (five fast lookbacks × five slow lookbacks), all trading the same simulated zero-drift price series — no true edge exists anywhere in this universe. Split into 10 blocks gives 252 combinations. Choosing SS trades two errors against each other: too few blocks and each in-sample or out-of-sample half is too short to estimate a Sharpe ratio reliably; too many and the number of combinations explodes for no statistical benefit — ten blocks of roughly a year each is a standard middle ground, not a magic number. The in-sample winner turned out to be no better than an out-of-sample coin flip in 82% of them — a high PBO, and the right answer: with a real mechanism (a crossover rule that can fit noise-specific wiggles in a specific block) tuned against the same finite sample over and over, most of the apparent skill is exactly that fitting, not signal.

🧭 Margin note — CSCV is not Session 6.6's CPCV

The names collide but the jobs don't. Session 6.6's combinatorial purged cross-validation (CPCV) builds many realistic, leakage-free backtest paths from one dataset, respecting purging and embargo, so you get an honest distribution of a single strategy's performance. CSCV asks a narrower, different question: given that you picked a winner in-sample, would it have looked like a winner out-of-sample? One builds paths; the other scores a selection decision. The parameter sweep above ignored transaction costs entirely, to keep the statistics visible — Session 6.5 is what you add before trusting any of these numbers on a live book.

6.7.5 Where people go wrong

Two failure modes account for nearly every misused deflated Sharpe ratio: silence about N, and a formula fed the wrong number.

⚠️ Pitfall — "kurtosis is kurtosis, whichever function computes it"

PSR's formula wants raw kurtosis (3 for a normal, per Session 3.2's convention), but scipy.stats.kurtosis and pandas' .kurt() return excess kurtosis (0 for a normal) by default. Feed the wrong one in and the (kurt(R)−1)/4(\text{kurt}(R)-1)/4 term is off by a factor that scales with how fat the tails really are — quietly overstating every PSR and DSR the code reports, in exactly the direction that makes a bad backtest look better. This is not a hypothetical mix-up: production trading-strategy code, including at least one widely used open-source backtesting library, has shipped this exact bug. Whichever library you use, check its convention before you trust its number — this lab's own psr() takes raw kurtosis and says so in its docstring.

A newer instrument makes the opposite mistake harder to excuse. The MinervaScore (Santoni, Jouanne & Scullin, 2026) bundles DSR, PBO, a superior-predictive-ability test and MinTRL into one 0–100 robustness grade — and its own authors report that on real markets it barely predicts anything forward-looking (a Spearman correlation of just 0.013 with future performance), despite separating genuine skill from noise almost perfectly on synthetic data where the truth is known (AUROC 0.989). That gap is not a flaw unique to their score; it is this whole session's warning, restated: these four statistics tell you whether a backtest's Sharpe ratio earned its confidence. None of them, alone or combined, tell you the strategy will keep working. Session 6.6's validation splits and Session 6.8 — Lab: The Research Protocol's paper-trading gate are what carry that further burden.

Treat every statistic in this session as a filter that removes bad ideas, never as a certificate that promotes a good one. A high DSR or a low PBO is necessary before risking capital on a backtest; neither is sufficient, and no combination of them replaces an untouched holdout or time spent trading on paper.

6.7.7 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Probabilistic Sharpe ratio — the probability that a strategy's true Sharpe ratio exceeds a benchmark, computed from the track record's own length, skewness and kurtosis (Bailey & López de Prado, 2012).
Minimum track record length — the number of periods a claimed Sharpe ratio needs before its PSR against a benchmark reaches a chosen confidence level.
Deflated Sharpe ratio — PSR evaluated against the expected best Sharpe ratio of N independent skill-less trials, instead of against zero; a Sharpe ratio corrected for how many things were tried (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014).
False strategy theorem — the result that the expected best Sharpe ratio among N skill-less trials grows with N and shrinks with more data, following an extreme-value-theory formula (López de Prado & Bailey, 2021).
Number of trials — how many genuinely independent strategies, parameters or variants were tried before the one being reported; near-duplicate trials count as less than one independent trial each.
Probability of backtest overfitting — the fraction of in-sample/out-of-sample splits (via CSCV) in which the in-sample winner performs at or below the out-of-sample median (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, 2016).
CSCV — combinatorially symmetric cross-validation: splitting a sample into S blocks and evaluating every equal-sized in-sample/out-of-sample combination of them, to score a selection decision rather than a single backtest.

Check yourself

1. Two managers report the identical annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.2 from five years of monthly returns. Manager A's returns are close to normal; Manager B's show negative skew and heavy tails. Whose PSR is higher, and why?
Manager A's. In the PSR formula, negative skewness and kurtosis above 3 both increase the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratio estimator (for a positive Sharpe ratio), which lowers the resulting probability. The gap widens exactly as sampling frequency coarsens — see the frequency table in §6.7.1.
2. A vendor pitches a strategy with a 3-year track record and a reported annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.0. Reassuring?
Barely. This session's own MinTRL table shows that even under the generous assumption of normal returns, a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 needs about 2.7 years to clear a zero benchmark at 95% confidence — 3 years clears it, but only just, and under any real fat-tailed shape the requirement is longer. Before being reassured, ask how many other variants were tried; that is a DSR question, not a MinTRL one.
3. A colleague swept 1,000 parameter combinations and reports the best Sharpe ratio, 1.46, without correction. What does this session's evidence say?
That number is close to meaningless on its own. This session's own N=1,000 experiment shows the best of 1,000 skill-less strategies averages exactly that Sharpe ratio in-sample, while its deflated Sharpe ratio sits at about 50% — statistically indistinguishable from no skill at all. Ask for the deflated number, or the trial count needed to compute it.
4. Why can't you just run DSR on a 25-combination moving-average sweep over one price history instead of CSCV/PBO?
DSR's deflation benchmark SR0 is built from the expected maximum of N genuinely independent Sharpe ratio estimates. The 25 moving-average variants are not independent: every one of them trades the same days, so their Sharpe ratios are highly correlated and there is no clean N to plug into the False Strategy Theorem's formula. CSCV sidesteps the question of N entirely by directly testing whether an in-sample selection decision would have held up out of sample, resampled many ways from the one dataset you have.
5. A junior researcher's DSR implementation passes scipy.stats.kurtosis(returns) (excess kurtosis, default) straight into a textbook PSR formula written for raw kurtosis. Which direction does the error push every reported DSR, and why does that make it dangerous?
It silently overstates DSR (and PSR): the formula computes (kurt - 1)/4 * SR^2 expecting kurt≈3 for near-normal returns, but receives kurt≈0 instead, understating the true standard deviation of the Sharpe ratio estimator and so overstating the resulting probability. It is dangerous precisely because it fails silently, in the direction that makes a mediocre backtest look better — the same code will pass unremarked-on until someone checks the convention.
