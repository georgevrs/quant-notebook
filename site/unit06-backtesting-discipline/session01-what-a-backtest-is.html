What a Backtest Is (and Isn't)

A backtest is not a crystal ball and not a lab result you can rerun on reality: it is a historical simulation of one fully specified rule, honest only as far as its stated assumptions reach. This session gives Unit 6 its shared vocabulary — strategy specification, historical simulation, fill assumption, in-sample — and shows, with one toy trend rule and nothing else changed, how much of a backtest's headline number can come from an assumption nobody wrote down. The core move: write down every assumption a simulation makes, then ask which of them you could actually keep once real money is on the line.

6.1.1 What a backtest actually is

Every session in this unit assumes you already believe one plain claim — and that claim hides three separate ideas that are easy to blur together.

As Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle put it, a backtest is a simulation of how a trading rule would have performed on historical data. That sentence is correct and, on its own, dangerously incomplete. It doesn't say the rule has to be written down precisely. It doesn't say what price a trade is assumed to fill at. It doesn't say the history has to be data the rule never touched while it was being built. This unit spends eight sessions on those three gaps; this session names them. In López de Prado's blunt framing, backtesting is "one of the most essential, yet least understood, techniques in quantitative finance" precisely because people treat it as a research tool — a place to go looking for a strategy — when in its narrowest, honest definition a backtest is a hypothetical, not an experiment: it tells you what would have happened if a fully specified rule had been followed, once, on a stretch of history it did not influence.

Say the "if" out loud and a historical simulation stops being mysterious: it is the mechanical replay, one period at a time, of a rule that was completely nailed down in advance, against price history it played no part in shaping. Nothing in that definition promises the rule was any good. It only promises the replay was faithful to what was written down. Whether the rule was good — whether it captured something real, rather than noise, luck, or a broken assumption — is a separate question, and it is the question the rest of Unit 6 is built to answer.

This course keeps one analogy for exactly this session: a backtest is a unit test that can pass by luck. A unit test in software fails deterministically — the assertion either holds or it doesn't, and rerunning it changes nothing. A backtest can satisfy every assertion you write, run without a single error, and still be describing a coincidence in a decade of noise rather than a real, repeatable mechanism. The code being correct and the strategy being real are two different claims, and a clean run only ever establishes the first one. The reason this bites harder in finance than in most engineering is that markets provide no oracle: a web service either returns the right answer today or it doesn't, but a trading rule's "correctness" is a statistical property that years of history barely constrain. Session 1.3's lab already showed the scale of the problem — a rule with zero real skill, tried a thousand times, produces a best backtested Sharpe ratio around 1.4 purely from trying enough variations — so the same bug that a unit test catches in ten seconds can hide in a trading rule for years, if anything ever catches it at all. Everything from here to Session 6.8 — Lab: The Research Protocol is about closing that gap in two stages: making the simulation itself trustworthy, which is this session's job, and then pricing in how many chances you gave luck to fool you, which is the second half of the unit.

📌 Key idea — a backtest is a conditional claim

Every backtest secretly begins with an "if": if this exact rule, on this exact data, under this exact fill assumption, had been followed. Change any one of those three and you get a different, equally "true" historical claim about a past that never quite happened. This session's job is to make the "if" visible and inspectable, so you can see exactly which one changed when two backtests of "the same idea" disagree.

6.1.2 The anatomy of a strategy specification

A backtest is only as trustworthy as the rule it replays is exact — and "exact" means a stranger could reproduce your trades from the written spec alone.

A strategy specification is everything needed for two independent people, or the same person a year apart, to run identical trades from identical data. Not "buy when it looks strong" — a number, a threshold, a rule for ties. Not "size it reasonably" — a fraction of capital, stated. One row is doing more work than the rest. Universe, signal, sizing and costs describe the rule. The fill assumption describes something else: at what price, and after what delay, a decided trade is assumed to actually execute. It is a modelling choice, exactly as inspectable and exactly as falsifiable as the signal itself — and, as §6.1.5 shows with numbers, changing only that one choice can move a Sharpe ratio by more than a real trading idea ever will.

Look at what precision buys, row by row. "Universe: one synthetic asset" rules out an entire family of mistakes at once — a universe that quietly includes companies that have since been delisted, or an index membership list computed with today's constituents applied to yesterday's prices, is one of the more expensive point-in-time mistakes a real desk can make (look-ahead bias, in full in Session 4.2). "Signal: price vs. its 100-day moving average" rules out the far more common failure of a rule that only ever lived in someone's head — "the trend looked strong" is not reproducible by anyone, including the person who said it, a week later. "Position sizing: all-in or all-out" is a deliberately blunt choice: a real desk would size with something closer to the fractional-Kelly rules of Session 3.6 — Bet Sizing, Kelly & Ruin, but layering a sizing model on top of the fill-assumption experiment in §6.1.5 would make it harder to see which change caused which result. Precision is not the same thing as complexity — this lab holds every dimension but one fixed, on purpose, so that the one it varies is the only possible explanation for what changes.

The dashed loop is not decorative. It is the single most common way a backtest quietly stops meaning what you think it means: you see the verdict, tweak the window or the threshold, rerun, and repeat. Nothing about steps 1–5 was done wrong, but the history you are testing against has been looked at, so by Session 1.3's definition it is no longer available to prove anything out-of-sample — it is in-sample now, whatever you privately call it. §6.1.3 makes that precise; Session 6.6 — Validation: Walk-Forward, Purged K-Fold & CPCV onward builds the machinery (holdouts, purging, the deflated Sharpe) that lets you tune a rule without lying to yourself about what the result still proves.

6.1.3 Simulating history, honestly

The formula is almost embarrassingly short. The discipline is entirely in what it refuses to let a position depend on.

Write the wealth a strategy would have produced over TT periods, starting from W0W_0, as compounding one period at a time:

WˆT=W0∏t=1T(1+Pt−1Rt)\hat{W}_T = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} \big(1 + P_{t-1} R_t\big)

Here RtR_t is the asset's realised simple return over period tt, and Pt−1P_{t-1} is the position size — here 0 or 1 — decided using only information available through period t−1t-1. That subscript is the entire discipline of a historical simulation in one place: the position is dated before the return it earns. Let PtP_t multiply RtR_t instead, and the "simulation" is quietly assuming a trader can see period tt's return before period tt has happened — not a subtle statistical bias, an internally inconsistent replay of a past that could not have occurred that way. §6.1.5 measures exactly how much that one subscript is worth.

The product form matters too, and for a familiar reason: Session 2.3 already established that simple returns compound by multiplication across time, not addition, and summing them instead would silently overstate any strategy that ever recovers from a loss. Walk the formula for two days by hand to see the lag do its job. Say day 1 closes up 2% while the filter has already been on since before day 1, so P0=1P_0 = 1: an honest simulation credits that whole move, 1+1×0.02=1.021 + 1 \times 0.02 = 1.02. Now say day 1's move is exactly what flips the filter off — the close dips just under its moving average. The rule is flat starting day 2, so day 2 contributes exactly zero, 1.02×(1+0×R2)=1.021.02 \times (1 + 0 \times R_2) = 1.02, whatever day 2's return turns out to be. That zero is not a bug; it is the entire content of "flat." A one-line implementation error — using day 2's own close to decide day 2's own position, then applying that position to day 2's own return — would instead let day 2's realised move leak into the decision of whether to hold it. That is exactly the naive fill assumption §6.1.5 measures, not a hypothetical.

The other term this session owns follows from the same idea. A stretch of history is in-sample the moment it has influenced any decision about the rule — which parameter, which window length, or even the decision to test this rule instead of another. That has nothing to do with a calendar date and everything to do with what you have looked at. It is the mirror image of out-of-sample, which Session 1.3 already treats as a resource you spend once: what this session adds is that a specification is what turns a stretch of raw prices into "in-sample" data in the first place — no specification has been tested against it yet, or it has, and now the clock has started.

🧭 Margin note — periods, not years

TT above counts periods — trading days in this lab. When a chart needs continuous elapsed time instead, this course calls it τ\tau, in years, never a calendar date. Every simulated-history chart on this page is indexed that way (year 1, year 2, …) specifically so nobody mistakes a synthetic path for a real historical one.

6.1.4 A toy strategy, precisely specified

Here is §6.1.2's checklist as code — nothing about the rule is hidden in a cell above this one.

The whole rule is four lines. signal is a plain boolean-to-float cast of one comparison; honest shifts that decision forward one full row before multiplying it by the return it is allowed to earn; naive skips the shift. Nothing about the universe, position sizing or the risk-free rate needs to appear in this snippet, because §6.1.2 already fixed all three at simple, stated values — the only thing left for this code to get right is the timing.

# the whole specification: one filter, two fill assumptions, nothing else
ma = prices.rolling(window).mean()
signal = (prices > ma).astype(float)      # 1 = trend up, using info through THIS close
ret = prices.pct_change()

honest = signal.shift(1) * ret               # P_(t-1): filled at the NEXT close
naive  = signal * ret                        # P_t applied to R_t: the same-close bug

On one simulated 8-year history of a single synthetic asset (7% annual drift, 18% annual volatility, no real trend beyond that drift), the filter is long 47% of the time and changes position 101 times. Both fill assumptions replay the identical signal — only the timing differs:

Fill assumptionCAGRSharpeMax drawdown
Buy & hold (benchmark)−1.1%0.03−43.1%
Honest — next close, 1-day lag−0.4%0.03−26.5%
Naive — same close, 0-day lag19.2%1.49−7.4%

On this one simulated history, the naive fill turns a strategy with no real edge into what would look, in a pitch deck, like an exceptional live track record: a 1.49 Sharpe and a shallow −7.4% drawdown, against an honest 0.03 and a real −26.5%. But one path is one draw — exactly the kind of anecdote §6.1.1 warned against treating as proof of anything. §6.1.5 repeats this same experiment on 400 independent synthetic histories to see whether the gap is systematic or just this path's luck.

The drawdown column is the most seductive part of the naive row. A −7.4% maximum drawdown, next to an honest −26.5%, looks like a genuinely better-engineered strategy — smoother, calmer, easier to hold through a real drawdown. It isn't. The naive fill's position for a day is chosen using that same day's own close, so on days the price is about to fall relative to its average the rule disproportionately finds itself already flat, and on days it is about to rise, already long. It is not avoiding bad days by skill; the simulation is deciding after the fact and presenting it as before.

6.1.5 The fill assumption that quietly changes the answer

Change one clause of the specification — how many periods pass between deciding and trading — and the identical rule tells two different stories.

The asset in this lab is pure geometric Brownian motion: a drift plus noise, with no genuine trend for a moving-average filter to find. That makes it a clean instrument for isolating the fill assumption, because any reliable, repeatable gap between the two variants cannot be real momentum — there isn't any — so it can only be the fill assumption itself. Repeating §6.1.4's experiment on 400 independent 4-year synthetic histories confirms it is not one path's luck:

Averaged over all 400 worlds, the naive fill beats the honest fill by 1.42 Sharpe (1.73 vs. 0.31) — a gap 64 standard errors from zero, nowhere near a coincidence of sampling. This lab asserts that gap at 4 standard errors, never a property of a single lucky path, and 64 clears that bar by a wide margin. A one-line bug — forgetting to lag a signal by even one period — manufactures more apparent "edge" than most real trading ideas ever produce.

The honest-fill number carries its own lesson. Averaged the same way, it trails simply buying and holding the asset (0.41 vs. 0.31, 5.1 standard errors apart) — not because the simulation is broken, but because a filter with nothing real to filter for still pays for its whipsaw at every crossing while sometimes sitting out the market's drift. That matches real evidence, not just synthetic data: reconstructing a monthly 10-month moving-average filter on the S&P 500 back to 1900, Faber (2007) found it nearly matched buy-and-hold's return — 11.72% a year versus 11.66% — while cutting volatility and drawdown sharply, not beating the index outright. An honestly simulated rule with no real edge doesn't have to look bad to be honest; it just has to stop pretending to beat a coin flip it happened to tie.

The mechanism behind that 5.1-standard-error shortfall is ordinary, not mysterious. Every time the price crosses its moving average, the honest rule needs one full day after the cross to react, and on a pure random walk that crossing carries no information about what comes next — so the filter pays a small, real cost in whipsaw at every crossing (101 of them, in the single path of §6.1.4) while collecting none of the upside a genuine trend-following mechanism would need to exist in order to earn back. A specification can be perfectly honest and still lose to the simplest possible benchmark; that is a fact about the rule, not a flaw in the simulation.

None of the naive-fill result requires exotic code. A one-line expression like position * returns, with position built from the same price column as returns and no .shift() in sight, reproduces the naive case exactly — by default, the first time someone writes a backtest before learning to ask "filled when, at what price?" It requires no malice and no obviously broken code; it requires only that the fill assumption was never written down as its own parameter, the way §6.1.2 insists it must be.

⚠️ Pitfall — "it ran without errors, so the fill must be fine"

The naive version above also ran without a single error, warning, or type mismatch — it produced a spurious 1.73 Sharpe as cleanly as the honest version produced 0.31. A backtest engine has no way to know whether the price it just used to fill a trade was actually available when the trade was decided; only the person writing the fill assumption down, and checking it against what a real order could achieve, catches this. This is the mildest member of a much larger family — using a close that was later restated, an index membership you only know in hindsight, a price nobody could actually have traded at — that Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame catalogues in full.

6.1.7 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Strategy specification — everything needed for two independent runs of the same rule on the same data to produce identical trades: universe, signal, entry/exit, sizing, fill assumption, costs and the risk-free rate.
Historical simulation — the mechanical replay, one period at a time, of a fully specified rule against price history it played no part in shaping.
Fill assumption — the explicit choice of what price, and after what delay, a simulated trade is assumed to execute at.
In-sample — any stretch of history that has influenced a decision about a rule — its parameters, its form, or the choice to test it at all.

Check yourself

1. A colleague says "my backtest shows a 1.8 Sharpe, so the strategy works." What's wrong with that sentence?
It treats a historical simulation as proof of a live edge. Per §6.1.1, a backtest is a conditional claim about the past under stated assumptions — not a prediction, not proof of skill, not a live track record. Before being impressed, ask for the strategy specification (especially the fill assumption and costs) and whether the result is even out of sample.
2. In §6.1.5, the identical signal earns a Sharpe of about 1.73 under one fill assumption and about 0.31 under another, on data with no real trend at all. What single change caused that, and why does it matter for any backtest you're handed?
Removing the one-period lag, so the position for period t uses the same close that produces period t's own return (P_t applied to R_t instead of P_{t-1}). It matters because this is a one-line bug that runs cleanly — no crash, no warning — and manufactures more apparent edge than most real strategies ever show; you cannot tell it happened without checking the specification.
3. A researcher tunes the moving-average window from 100 days to 40 after seeing that 40 improves the honest-fill Sharpe, then reports the improved number as "the" result. What has happened to that stretch of history?
It is now in-sample: the choice of window was made using information from that period, so its Sharpe no longer measures performance on data the rule hasn't seen. Reporting it as if it were a fresh test is exactly the mistake a written kill criterion and a fresh holdout (Sessions 6.6–6.8) exist to prevent.
4. In the wealth formula of §6.1.3, why must the position at period t, P_(t-1), be decided using only information through period t-1 rather than period t?
Because letting the position depend on period t's own information while also earning period t's return (P_t · R_t) requires knowing that return before it occurs — something no live trader can do. It isn't a minor statistical bias; it silently turns an honest simulation into a replay of a past that couldn't have happened that way, exactly the naive fill assumption in §6.1.5.
5. Two people backtest "the same" moving-average rule and get different equity curves. Per §6.1.2, what's the first thing to check?
Whether their strategy specifications actually match — universe, signal, sizing, and above all the fill assumption and costs. If any of those differ, the two runs are honest answers to two different questions, not evidence that one person's code is better than the other's.
