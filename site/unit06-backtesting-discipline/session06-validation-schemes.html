Validation: Walk-Forward, Purged K-Fold & CPCV

A backtest's cross-validation score is only honest if the way you split the data respects time. Shuffle a labeled panel the way you would shuffle photographs of cats and dogs, and overlapping, serially correlated financial labels leak the future into the past — quietly, without an error, without a warning. The core move: never let a training observation share information with a test observation, and prove it with a number, not a hunch.

6.6.1 Why standard cross-validation lies to you

Ordinary k-fold cross-validation assumes every observation is independent of every other. Financial data almost never is, and the gap between the assumption and the reality is where a backtest's most convincing, least detectable lies live.

You already know the shape of the problem from Session 6.1 — What a Backtest Is (and Isn't) and Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame: a historical simulation can look excellent and still say nothing about live performance, because it quietly used information it would not have had in real time. Cross-validation is supposed to be the ML-flavoured defence against that: hold out a slice of data the model never trained on, and score it there. In a vision or NLP dataset, shuffling observations into folds is harmless, because one labeled photo of a cat tells you nothing about the label of a different, unrelated photo. Three properties of financial data break that assumption, and all three get worse the more realistic your labels become.

This session gives you the toolkit that survives all three. Walk-forward analysis is the oldest and simplest: never validate on anything that isn't strictly later in time than everything you trained on, and re-fit as you go. Purging and embargo are the surgical fix for a labeled, panel-style dataset: cut out exactly the training observations that share information with the test fold, and a little more besides. Purged k-fold cross-validation combines the two into a drop-in replacement for ordinary k-fold. Combinatorial purged cross-validation (CPCV) then reuses the same idea to generate many honest backtest paths instead of one. All four exist because of the same failure mode: leakage, information from the test set reaching the training set by any route at all — a shared label window, a slowly moving feature, or a careless shuffle.

📌 Key idea — the question every scheme in this session answers

"Does any training observation know something about the test observation that live trading would not have known yet?" If the answer is yes, your validation score is not an estimate of live performance — it is an estimate of how well your model memorises the past. Every method below is a different way of forcing the answer to be no.

Recall the engineering analogy this course keeps coming back to: a backtest is a unit test that can pass by luck, and look-ahead is train/test leakage under a different name. This session is that analogy taken literally into the validation harness itself. The stakes are ordinary, not exotic: a leaked cross-validation score doesn't announce itself with a crash or a stack trace, it just reports a Sharpe ratio or an accuracy figure that is a little too good, in the one direction that gets a mediocre idea greenlit for paper trading — or, worse, real capital — instead of sent back for more work. Nobody discovers the mistake until the live numbers quietly fail to match the backtest, months later, with real money already committed.

6.6.2 Walk-forward analysis

The oldest validation discipline in systematic trading is also the simplest to state: decide everything using only data strictly before the moment you are simulating, then move the clock forward and repeat.

Walk-forward analysis splits the sample into a sequence of train/test pairs that all point the same direction in time. You fit — or re-fit, or re-select a parameter — on a training window, apply the result, untouched, to the very next chunk of data, then roll the whole window forward and repeat. Two variants differ only in what happens to the training window as you roll: an anchored (expanding) walk-forward keeps every observation from the start of the sample, so the training window grows each fold; a rolling (sliding) walk-forward keeps the window's length fixed and drops the oldest data as it adds the newest, which adapts faster to a changing market at the cost of a shorter effective history each fold.

The lab runs both variants on a synthetic daily return series with a small, genuine, first-order autocorrelation of 6% — a real but modest momentum edge, the kind you can plausibly find and just as plausibly talk yourself out of trusting. The "strategy" is deliberately simple: pick a lookback window from a small grid and go with the sign of the trailing sum of returns. Picking that window by looking at the whole sample — including the days you are about to "test" on — and reporting the resulting Sharpe ratio is exactly the mistake Session 6.1 — What a Backtest Is (and Isn't) warns about: it is not out-of-sample, no matter how the code is organised. Over 2,016 simulated days split into 8 walk-forward folds, that whole-sample selection reports an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.54 using a lookback of 80 days. Walking forward honestly — choosing the lookback using only the training window, applying it strictly out-of-sample, then rolling — reports 0.31 (anchored) and 0.44 (rolling) on the very same underlying data.

One path proves nothing by itself — Session 6.4 — The Bias Hall of Fame is a whole session on how a single convincing backtest can be luck. So the lab redraws the entire synthetic panel from scratch 60 times and checks the gap. Averaged across redraws, whole-sample selection beats anchored walk-forward by 0.39 Sharpe points (standard error 0.046 — more than 8 standard errors from zero) and beats rolling walk-forward by 0.42 points (standard error 0.058). The gap is not a fluke of one seed; it is what happens, reliably, when a parameter is chosen with knowledge of the exact data it will be judged on.

Two practical choices drive every walk-forward setup: how long each test window is, and how the training window behaves as it rolls. A shorter test window rolls forward more often and produces a longer, more informative out-of-sample path, at the cost of re-fitting more often — expensive for a slow model, irrelevant for the sign-of-a-sum rule used here. Anchored windows suit slowly changing relationships and small total samples, because they never throw data away; rolling windows suit markets that drift or break, at the cost of a shorter effective history in every fold. Neither variant is free of judgement calls — window lengths, fold counts and re-fit frequency are themselves choices you could overfit by trying many of them and keeping the one that flatters your backtest, which is precisely the "number of trials" problem Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO puts a number on. Systematic trading shops that predate modern ML tooling by decades still run walk-forward as their default discipline for exactly this reason: it needs no labeled panel, no purge horizon, nothing but the willingness to never let the model see its own exam paper in advance.

💡 Looking ahead

Walk-forward answers "is this one honest?" one path at a time. 6.6.3 Purging and embargo, precisely

Walk-forward analysis is a way of splitting a single ordered SEQUENCE. A labeled machine-learning panel needs something more surgical, because its "test set" can sit anywhere in time and its labels reach forward past the test set's own edges.

Suppose a label is built from information over the horizon [t+1,t+H][t+1, t+H] — a fixed-horizon return, or the triple-barrier label that Session 12.2 — Labeling: Triple Barrier & Meta-Labeling builds on this same idea. Two labels at times ii and jj share at least one day of information whenever |i−j|<H|i-j| \lt H. Purging is the rule that follows directly from that fact: remove from the training set every observation whose horizon overlaps any test observation's horizon, on either side of the test block. Embargo is a second, one-directional rule for a subtler leak: even after purging removes every overlapping label, a training observation that starts just after the test block can still have FEATURES — a moving average, a rolling volatility estimate — that were computing over days that fall inside the test window, because features are serially correlated even once labels no longer overlap. Embargo removes a further block of observations immediately after the test set to close that gap. Neither correction runs backward past a test set that has already been embargoed forward from an earlier fold — time only flows one way, so the fix only needs to point one way too.

HH is not a free parameter you tune for a better score — it is a fact about how the label was built, and you should be able to point to the line of code that fixes it. A fixed-horizon label with a 20-day lookahead has H=20H=20; a triple-barrier label's vertical barrier (the maximum holding period before the position is closed regardless of the profit-taking or stop-loss barriers) plays exactly the same role, which is why Session 12.2 — Labeling: Triple Barrier & Meta-Labeling inherits this session's purging machinery rather than reinventing it. Embargo, by contrast, is closer to a judgement call: a common default sizes it to a small percentage of the total sample, or to the longest lookback window used anywhere in the feature set, whichever is easier to defend to a reviewer.

A small worked example, sized to fit on the page: 20 observations, a label horizon of H=H=4 days, one test fold covering indices 8–11, and an embargo of 6 observations. Purging alone clears indices 5 through 14 — everywhere a horizon of length HH could reach across the fold boundary. Embargo then reaches three observations further, through index 17. Of the original 20 observations, only 7 remain usable for training this fold — a blunt reminder that purging and embargo cost you data, which is exactly why the next section measures what you buy back with it.

🧭 Margin note — purge vs. embargo, in one line

Purge answers "did this label peek at the test set?" Embargo answers "could this FEATURE still smell of the test set even once the labels are clean?" You need a value for HH to purge; embargo just needs an honest guess at how long your features' memory runs.

6.6.4 The overlapping-label leak, in code

Here is the leak in numbers, on a labeled panel built the way a real one behaves: a feature you can observe today, and a label that depends on where a latent process ends up over the next H days.

The lab constructs 2,000 samples from a single autocorrelated latent process (an AR(1) "regime" with persistence 0.90). Each sample's feature is 5 noisy trailing readings of that process — a stand-in for the trailing indicators every technical feature set is built from — and its label is the sign of the process's summed path over the next 40 days, exactly the fixed-horizon label Session 12.2 — Labeling: Triple Barrier & Meta-Labeling will generalise into a full triple-barrier label. Consecutive samples' labels overlap almost entirely: sample 500 and sample 510 share 30 of their 40 label days. A simple 3-nearest-neighbour classifier is trained and scored four ways on the identical data:

# the from-scratch splitter this session contributes to quantnb
class PurgedKFold:
    def split(self, n):
        # contiguous folds, then purge + embargo around each one
        for start, stop in fold_bounds(n, self.n_splits):
            mask = purge_and_embargo(n, [(start, stop)], self.H, self.embargo)
            yield np.where(mask)[0], np.arange(start, stop)

Naive k-fold means scikit-learn's KFold(shuffle=True): five folds, randomly assigned, exactly the default a careless copy-paste reaches for. Purged k-fold uses the splitter above with the embargo set equal to HH. A genuine holdout trains on the first 70% and tests on the final 30%, with a gap of HH days between them — a second, independent honest estimate that never even sees the purging machinery. And because the data-generating process is a known, jointly normal one, the lab also computes the Bayes-optimal accuracy in closed form: the best any classifier could ever do from these features is ℙ(correct)=12+arcsin⁡rπ\Prob(\text{correct}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\arcsin r}{\pi}, the classical result for jointly normal variables, where rr is the correlation between the optimal linear combination of the features and the label (derived from the AR(1) covariance structure, and checked numerically in the lab).

Purged k-fold and the independent holdout agree to within half a point, and both sit comfortably below the theoretical ceiling — exactly what an honest, finite-sample estimate of real but modest predictive power should look like. Naive k-fold does not: it overstates accuracy by 1.8% on average, a gap with a Monte Carlo standard error of only 0.19% over 50 redrawn datasets — more than nine standard errors from zero, never once seed-hunted. The mechanism is exactly fig 6.6.1's "serial correlation" panel: because the features are smooth, a query point's nearest neighbour in feature space is usually its neighbour in time, and shuffled k-fold routinely puts that time-neighbour in the opposite fold, where its label — built from an overlapping window of the same underlying path — hands the classifier a preview of the answer.

Nothing about this mechanism is specific to a 3-nearest-neighbour classifier — it is the sharpest, easiest-to-see instance of a general problem. Any model flexible enough to exploit local structure in feature space — a random forest splitting on a narrow range of a trailing indicator, a gradient-boosted tree memorising a handful of similar rows, even a well-regularised linear model if the features are informative enough — can pick up the same spurious edge from a temporally close, overlapping-label neighbour that naive k-fold hands it. A less flexible model leaks less only because it is worse at exploiting the opportunity, not because the opportunity isn't there.

⚠️ Pitfall — "cross-validation is cross-validation, right?"

KFold(shuffle=True) is the scikit-learn default reflex for "get me an honest score," and it is precisely wrong for panel data with a time axis. It doesn't raise an exception, doesn't warn, and — as fig 6.6.5 shows — doesn't even look absurd. It just quietly reports a number a few points too high, in the direction that gets a mediocre model shipped.

6.6.5 Combinatorial purged cross-validation

Purged k-fold gives you one honest score. CPCV gives you a whole distribution of them, by testing on every combination of held-out groups instead of just one partition — and reconstructing several complete out-of-sample paths from the pieces.

Split the TT observations into NN contiguous groups instead of NN folds used one at a time. Combinatorial purged cross-validation tests on every combination of kk of those NN groups, purging and embargoing the training set with respect to all kk held-out groups at once, and fits and scores (Nk)\binom{N}{k} times in total. Each of the NN groups turns up as part of a test set in exactly (N−1k−1)\binom{N-1}{k-1} of those combinations — and that count is also the number of complete, non-overlapping out-of-sample paths you can stitch back together, one prediction per group, with every path built from a different, disjoint set of splits:

φ(N,k)=(N−1k−1)=kN(Nk)\varphi(N,k) = \binom{N-1}{k-1} = \frac{k}{N}\binom{N}{k}

López de Prado's own illustration uses N=6N=6 groups, testing k=2k=2 at a time: (62)=15\binom{6}{2}=15 splits, reconstructing φ(6,2)=\varphi(6,2)=5 paths. Scaled up to N=15N=15, k=11k=11 — his own worked example — the same formula gives φ(15,11)=\varphi(15,11)=1,001 paths from one historical dataset. The lab reruns the §6.6.4 dataset with N=N=10 groups and k=k=2: 45 purged-and-embargoed splits, reconstructing 9 complete paths — and, because k=2k=2 turns the reconstruction into exactly the classic round-robin tournament-scheduling problem (every pair of groups meets exactly once; every path is one "round" in which every group plays exactly once), the lab builds those 9 paths with the textbook circle-method algorithm, then cross-checks both counts against skfolio's independent CombinatorialPurgedCV implementation.

The nine reconstructed paths on the §6.6.4 dataset average 54.5% accuracy, with a standard deviation across paths of 0.79% and a range from 52.9% to 55.4%. That spread is the entire point: a single purged k-fold run reports one number and hides how much it would have moved had history run slightly differently. CPCV reports the distribution directly.

Choosing NN and kk is a real trade-off, not a default to leave alone. A larger NN means finer-grained groups, more splits to fit and score, and — per the table above — many more reconstructed paths; a larger kk means each split trains on less data, because more of it is held out and purged away at once. López de Prado's own (N,k)=(15,11)(N,k)=(15,11) example trades a demanding 1,365 model fits for 1,001 usable paths; the (N,k)=(10,2)(N,k)=(10,2) version in this lab is a fraction of the compute for a ninth of the paths — plenty to see the spread in fig 6.6.8, far cheaper to run inside a parameter sweep. Start small, and grow NN only once you have measured how long one split costs to fit.

🧭 Margin note — CPCV is not CSCV

Don't confuse this session's CPCV (many purged train/test paths from ONE labeled dataset) with the CSCV method Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO uses to compute the probability of backtest overfitting: CSCV resamples a matrix of many STRATEGIES' P&L to ask "how many of the paths I tried were actually skill?" CPCV instead answers "how would my ONE strategy's validated score have moved if history had shuffled its groups differently?" — and the paths it produces are exactly the raw material Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO and Session 12.4 — Gradient Boosting for Cross-Sectional Alpha build on next.

6.6.6 Choosing a scheme — and where people go wrong

All four schemes in this session answer the same question — "would this look this good live?" — for different shapes of problem. Picking the wrong one is the single most common validation mistake this course will show you.

Your problem looks like…UseWhy
One strategy, re-optimised periodically over a single historyWalk-forward analysismatches how you would actually trade it live
A labeled panel (features → label) with overlapping label windowsPurged k-foldreuses more of the data than a single holdout, without leaking
You need the VARIANCE of an honest score, not just its meanCPCVreconstructs many non-overlapping honest paths from one dataset
Any of the above, shuffled with scikit-learn's KFold defaultNeverfig 6.6.5 measured the cost: about two points of accuracy, silently

The naive-vs-disciplined contrast is worth stating plainly, because the naive version is not a strawman — it is the first thing that works, compiles, and returns a plausible-looking number.

💡 Tip — you rarely have to choose only one

A common production pattern: use purged k-fold (or CPCV) during feature and model selection, when you need to reuse the data many times without leaking; reserve one final walk-forward pass, or a true untouched holdout, as the last check before anything gets a paper-trading allocation.

None of the four schemes in this session makes a mediocre idea good. What they do is cheaper and more important: they stop a mediocre idea from looking good by accident. Every number this course reports from here on — a Sharpe ratio, an accuracy, an information coefficient — is only as trustworthy as the split that produced it, which is exactly why Session 6.7 — Overfitting Statistics: PSR, DSR, MinTRL & PBO takes these validated numbers as its starting point rather than raw backtest output, and why Session 12.4 — Gradient Boosting for Cross-Sectional Alpha will call the purged splitter built in this lab directly rather than reach for scikit-learn's default.

6.6.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Walk-forward analysis — validating (and re-fitting) a strategy on a sequence of train/test windows that all point forward in time, never backward.
Purging — removing every training observation whose label horizon overlaps a test observation's label horizon, on either side of the test set.
Embargo — removing a further block of training observations immediately after a test set, to protect against serially correlated features that purging alone does not catch.
Purged k-fold cross-validation — ordinary k-fold cross-validation with purging and embargo applied around every fold, so a panel's labels can be reused for training and testing without leaking.
Combinatorial purged cross-validation — testing on every combination of k held-out groups out of N (CPCV), purged and embargoed, then reconstructing φ(N,k) = C(N−1, k−1) complete out-of-sample paths from the results.
Leakage — any route by which information from a test observation reaches the training set, inflating a validation score above what live performance would show.

Check yourself

1. Your walk-forward loop selects a lookback window by checking which one has the best Sharpe ratio on the ENTIRE dataset, then applies it fold by fold. Is this walk-forward analysis?
No. Walk-forward means every decision uses only data strictly before the window it is applied to. Selecting the parameter on the whole sample first and then "walking forward" with it is whole-sample optimisation wearing a walk-forward costume — exactly the §6.6.2 comparison, where it overstated the honest Sharpe ratio by roughly 0.4.
2. A colleague purges training observations that overlap the test set's labels, but sets the embargo to zero because "purging already removes the overlap." When would that go wrong?
Whenever the FEATURES have their own memory beyond the label horizon H — a 60-day moving average feature, say, used with a 20-day label. Purging clears the label overlap correctly, but a training observation just after the test set can still have a feature value that was computed using days inside the test window. Embargo is the separate fix for that, sized to the features' memory, not the label horizon.
3. Why does naive k-fold cross-validation OVERSTATE accuracy here, rather than just adding noise in a random direction?
Because the leak is one-directional: a shuffled fold can hand the training set a temporally close neighbour of a test point whose label overlaps it, which can only help the classifier guess correctly, never hurt it. Removing that neighbour (purging) can only lower the score or leave it unchanged — so the naive score is a biased overestimate, not a noisier one.
4. With N=8 groups and k=3 held out per split, how many splits does CPCV run, and how many complete out-of-sample paths does it reconstruct?
C(8,3) = 56 splits. Paths: φ(8,3) = C(7,2) = 21. Check it the other way too: (k/N)·C(N,k) = (3/8)·56 = 21 — the same answer, because k·C(N,k)/N is always an integer equal to C(N−1,k−1).
5. You are validating a single systematic trend-following strategy that will be re-optimised every quarter in production, with no cross-sectional panel of labels involved. Which scheme fits best, and which would be overkill?
Walk-forward analysis, anchored or rolling to match how the re-optimisation will actually run in production. Purged k-fold and CPCV are built for labeled panels reused many times across features or models; with one strategy and one re-optimisation cadence, they add purge/embargo machinery you do not need — walk-forward already mimics live deployment directly.
