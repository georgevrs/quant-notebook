Corporate Actions, Security Master & Continuous Futures

A dividend, a split and a futures roll look unrelated. To a data pipeline they are the same problem: a known, dated event that moves the quoted price without moving your wealth, and a security master's whole job is to fold that event into history as one clean number. The core move: an adjustment factor is not a fact about the world, it is a number you multiply in — and every convention for chaining those numbers trades away something.

4.3.1 The security master: one identity, many labels

Before you can adjust a price, you have to know which company you are talking about — through a name change, a ticker reused by a stranger, and a merger that ends one history and starts another.

Every vendor, exchange and regulator names a security differently. A security identifier is any of those codes: a ticker, a CUSIP (nine characters, North America), an ISIN (twelve characters, the global standard fixed by ISO 6166 — a two-letter ISO country code, a nine-character national body that is often just a CUSIP or local code padded out, and one check digit computed by the Luhn algorithm), or a FIGI (twelve alphanumeric characters, open and free, issued through Bloomberg's Object Management Group allocation process). Only some of these are safe to build a history on. A ticker is a mailbox: exchanges reuse them, sometimes within a year of a delisting. A CUSIP can be reassigned after some corporate reorganisations, and an ISIN inherits whatever its national numbering agency does to the code it wraps. A FIGI is the outlier by design: once assigned to a given instrument it is never reused, even after that instrument stops trading — it is simply retired and stays queryable (OpenFIGI's own specification is explicit about this). That permanence is about the instrument, not the company: a bond exchange or a share-class change still mints a new FIGI, so chaining one company's identifiers through time is still a security master's job even once you have picked the sturdiest identifier available.

A security master is the reference database that solves this: it assigns one permanent internal key to each company, maps every external identifier and every date range it was valid for onto that key, and stores the timeline of corporate actions against it. One level up from the security sits the issuer: GLEIF's Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) system answers "who owns whom" for the company itself — its direct and ultimate parent — which a security master usually carries as one more attribute of the permanent key, since one issuer can stand behind many securities (common stock, preferred stock, several bond series). Get the key wrong and every later step — adjustment, index membership, point-in-time joins (Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases) — inherits the mistake.

IdentifierScopeWho assigns itReuse risk

A worked example makes the failure concrete. Say a permanent identifier — call it PERM-00185 — traded under the ticker "QNTX" from $42.00 to $95.00 before being acquired: a true return of +126.2%. Years later an unrelated company, PERM-00931, is assigned the now-free ticker "QNTX" and trades from $18.00 down to $11.50, a true return of −36.1%. A pipeline that joins history on the ticker alone splices the two into one "QNTX" track record running end to end at −72.6% — a number that is neither company's return, built on a fabricated one-day jump of −81.1% the moment the ticker changes hands. Nothing in the raw data is wrong; the key is.

📌 Key idea — the ticker is a label, not a key

Key every table you build on the permanent identifier the security master hands you, keep the ticker (and CUSIP, and name) as a date-ranged attribute of that key, and never join two datasets on a ticker alone. Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases covers the twin failure — a company that vanishes from a naive universe (survivorship bias) rather than one that is silently merged with a stranger.

4.3.2 From corporate action to adjusted price

Corporate action economics — why a dividend or a split moves the quoted price — is Session 2.2 — Equities, Indices & ETFs's territory (§2.2.2-§2.2.3). This session owns what a pipeline does with that fact: turn each event into a number, and chain the numbers correctly.

Call the number one event contributes an adjustment factor ϕ\phi. A cash dividend paid on the ex-date, worth a fraction qq of the price, contributes ϕ=1/(1+q)\phi = 1/(1+q) — this is exactly CRSP's own "factor to adjust price" (CFACPR) convention for its US stock database. An N-for-1 split contributes ϕ=1/N\phi = 1/N. Both shrink history: a euro of dividend or a share split in the past bought less of today's company than it looks like on the raw tape. Multiplying every ϕ\phi still ahead of a given day gives the cumulative adjustment factor that day carries; multiplying that into the raw quote gives the adjusted price — anchored so that today's value never gets restated, only the past moves.

A security master usually keeps a second column for exactly the case this simple picture glosses over: the factor that adjusts a price is not always the factor that adjusts a share count. CRSP's own US stock database carries a cumulative factor to adjust price and a separate cumulative factor to adjust shares, and its own documentation is explicit that the two usually agree but not always — a partial buyback or an odd-lot tender can move one without moving the other by the same amount. A pipeline that multiplies one factor into both prices and share counts will occasionally get a market capitalisation or an index weight wrong even when the adjusted price series itself is fine.

ActionPer-event factor ϕ\phiEffect on earlier prices
Cash dividend, yield qq1/(1+q)1/(1+q)shrinks by about qq
Split, N-for-11/N1/Nshrinks by a factor of N
Reverse split, 1-for-NNNgrows by a factor of N
Two or more actions on one daythe product of each ϕ\phimultiplication commutes — order never matters

The lab builds one stock over 4 years carrying 4 quarterly dividends of 0.5% of the price each and a 3-for-1 split at year 2.0, so the cumulative factor carried by the very first day is 0.327 — the four dividend factors of 0.9950 each and the one split factor of 0.3333, multiplied together. The adjusted price this produces is, to nine decimal places, exactly proportional to the true reinvested value the stock actually earned: its CAGR is −5.98%, matching the true CAGR of −5.98% to the assert's tolerance, while the raw, un-adjusted quote implies a CAGR of −28.92% — a stock that actually lost value slowly looks, on the raw tape alone, like it lost value five times faster. A total-return index is nothing more than this same adjusted price rebased to a round number at the start (100 here, ending at 78.13) — the way S&P DJI and MSCI publish theirs: same shape, different base.

🧭 Margin note — adjustment on adjustment

A security master rarely learns about a dividend before its ex-date. Until the fourth dividend in the lab's example is entered, every day before it carries a cumulative factor missing one ϕ\phi; once it posts, every historical adjusted price before that date — 900 of 4-years'-worth of rows, 89% of the table — must be rescaled by 1.0050, not just the new row appended. A pipeline that only ever appends will quietly drift out of sync with itself.

4.3.3 Continuous futures: splicing contracts that die

Session 2.4 — Futures: Carry, Basis, Roll & Margin prices a curve from cost of carry and shows what a naive front-month chart hides (§2.4.4-§2.4.5); it promised this session would build the fix. Here it is, from real per-contract data.

A futures contract expires on a fixed date. A trend-following system, a risk model or a backtest wants one number line covering years, not thirty-two overlapping ones each alive for a quarter — a continuous futures series. Building one needs two decisions: a roll schedule — the rule that decides, for every day, which contract the series is "in" — and an adjustment convention to remove the jump the switch creates. Session 2.4's own pitfall callout already showed the cost of skipping both: a spliced front-month chart reads off contango exactly like backwardation, because the jump at every roll hides the very thing that separates them.

Real vendors use two families of roll schedule. A calendar rule switches a fixed number of trading days before expiry — simple, and independent of what anyone is trading. A liquidity rule switches when the next contract's volume or open interest overtakes the expiring one's; CME Group's own Active Contract series is built this way, choosing the roll date from "the liquidity profile of each asset" rather than a fixed date, alongside a separate Front Contract series that always rolls two business days before expiry. The two rules need not agree.

Roll ruleDecides onIn the lab (contract 1 → 2)
Calendara fixed offset before expiryrolls at year 0.21, 10 trading days out
Liquidity (volume crossover)the next contract's volume overtaking the front'srolls at year 0.22, 8 trading days out

The two rules land 2 trading days apart on this contract — a small gap, but every gap moves the exact day a gain or loss is booked, which matters for anyone reconciling a fund's return to a benchmark that rolls on a different rule. The liquidity rule exists because the calendar rule can strand a real position in a contract that has already gone quiet: a fixed offset picked in advance has no way to notice that, this particular quarter, traders moved to the next contract early. Rolling into wherever the volume already is keeps a systematic strategy trading the depth the market is actually offering, at the cost of a roll date that moves around from one expiry to the next instead of landing on the same weekday every time.

4.3.4 Back-adjustment vs ratio adjustment: the math

Two conventions turn the same sequence of contracts into one smooth line. Both are exact about what they preserve — and exact about what they give up.

Write Fi(t)F_i(t) for contract ii's cost-of-carry price at time tt (2.4 §2.4.2's F=SecτF = Se^{c\tau}), and say the position rolls from contract ii into i+1i+1 at time τi\tau_i. The naive spliced series is just whichever contract is currently held — the chart 2.4 already warned you about. Both adjustment conventions instead shift the naive series by everything that happens at every roll still ahead of a given day, so that today (the most recent contract) is never restated:

back-adjusted(t)=naive(t)+∑τi>t[Fi+1(τi)−Fi(τi)],ratio-adjusted(t)=naive(t)⋅∏τi>tFi+1(τi)Fi(τi)\text{back-adjusted}(t) = \text{naive}(t) + \!\!\sum_{\tau_i > t}\!\! \bigl[F_{i+1}(\tau_i) - F_i(\tau_i)\bigr], \qquad \text{ratio-adjusted}(t) = \text{naive}(t) \cdot \!\!\prod_{\tau_i > t}\!\! \frac{F_{i+1}(\tau_i)}{F_i(\tau_i)}

Back-adjustment adds the dollar gap at every future roll; ratio adjustment multiplies by the price ratio at every future roll. Both are engineered to keep one promise exactly: back-adjustment reproduces the true dollar profit and loss of holding the position, day by day, roll days included; ratio adjustment reproduces the true percentage return the same way. The lab checks both as an identity, not an estimate — comparing every day's change against the previously-held contract marked one day forward, at machine-precision tolerance, the same standard 2.4 used for its own carry identity.

Both formulas anchor to today, echoing §4.3.2's adjusted price: the most recent contract's real, tradeable quote is never restated, and everything else moves to match it. A pipeline could instead anchor to the first day of the history and adjust forward — leaving the earliest contract's price exactly as it traded and inflating or shrinking every later one instead. Neither choice changes a single return; it only decides which end of the series matches a number you could actually have seen print on a screen. This course, like most vendors building a live series, anchors to today, because that is the number a trading system has to agree with the exchange on right now.

Back-adjustmentRatio adjustment
Exact fordollar P&L, day by daypercentage return, day by day
Distant historycan go negativealways positive
Best forfixed-size positions, $-stops, P&L accountingSharpe, volatility, correlation, ML features

The lab makes the trade-off unavoidable rather than theoretical: an illustrative, deliberately extreme −25%-a-year backwardation (steeper than 2.4's own "tight" crude example), rolled through 32 quarterly contracts over 8 years, drives the back-adjusted series down to −$45.76 at year 0.01 — a negative futures price, the textbook defect of the additive method. The ratio-adjusted series over the same contracts never dips below $9.18; both still end, by construction, at today's real quote of $34.64.

The distortion this buys is not cosmetic. A rule such as "buy when the price crosses its 200-day moving average," evaluated on the ratio-adjusted series, triggers on a level that has been rescaled by the cumulative product of every future roll ratio — a number with no trading meaning of its own, however faithfully it tracks percentage moves. The same rule on the back-adjusted series triggers on a level that differs from the real, tradeable quote by a known dollar amount, because that is exactly what an additive shift preserves. Neither series is wrong; a rule written in price levels only means what its author intended on the convention it was built for.

🧭 Margin note — the jump is not noise

A naive series' jump at a roll is spot(roll) × a fixed constant, so its size is predictable, not random. Checked across 4,000 simulated paths, the mean jump at the first roll is −$4.220 against a theoretical −$4.226 — within $0.008 standard errors of each other, comfortably inside the course's four-standard-error bar for a Monte Carlo claim.

4.3.5 Building it: a roll schedule, in code

The same idea in code — copied from the companion lab so every number on this page matches a line you can run.

The lab keeps per-contract volume in a long-format table, writes it to Parquet, and finds the liquidity-based roll date with one DuckDB query rather than a Python loop — exactly the shape Session 4.5 generalises into a full storage stack:

# the day the NEXT contract's synthetic volume first overtakes the front's
SELECT roll, min(day) AS roll_day
FROM read_parquet(?)
WHERE next_volume >= front_volume
GROUP BY roll ORDER BY roll

The two adjusted series themselves are two lines once the roll dates are known — a running sum of dollar gaps for back-adjustment, a running product of price ratios for ratio adjustment, each restricted to rolls still ahead of the day being priced:

# n_rolls_after[t] counts the rolls strictly after day t
back_adjusted = naive + cum_gap_suffix[n_rolls_after]
ratio_adjusted = naive * gap_ratio_const ** n_rolls_after

Because the calendar rule always rolls the same number of days before and after expiry, the ratio at every roll collapses to one constant, 0.9394 here — a fact the lab asserts rather than assumes, and a useful sanity check on any implementation: if your ratios are not identical under a pure calendar rule, something upstream is wrong — the contract calendar has a gap, the carry is not actually constant, or the roll dates were computed off the wrong contract. The same assertion, left running in a production pipeline rather than a one-off lab, is a cheap alarm for exactly that class of bug. The naive series' first jump, −$5.09, is 4.6 times the $1.10 an ordinary trading day moves at this contract's volatility — big enough that no naive-series backtest would treat it as noise, which is exactly the problem.

Over the full 8 years the ratio-adjusted series compounds at +17.60% against spot's own −8.42% — a gap of 26.0 percentage points a year that is, to nine decimal places, exactly the curve's own −25% net carry (Session 2.4's roll-yield identity, reproduced here from real per-contract data and a real roll schedule instead of one idealised curve).

4.3.6 Where people go wrong

Three mistakes share one shape: treating a convenient label as if it were the underlying truth.

TaskUseWhy
Sharpe ratio, volatility, correlations, ML featuresratio-adjusted (or true per-contract %)reproduces the real percentage moves exactly
Fixed-size position P&L, a dollar stop-lossback-adjustedreproduces the real dollar moves exactly
Market capitalisation, P/E, index membership on a past dateraw price × shares outstanding that dayan adjusted price is not a historical price — it is today's price, restated

A fourth mistake is subtler: a roll rule defined as "switch into whichever contract has the most volume over the next ten days" cannot be backtested honestly, because on any given day you do not yet know the next ten days' volume. It is Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases's look-ahead bias wearing a futures costume — a roll schedule has to be computable from information available strictly as of that day, exactly like every other point-in-time rule in this unit.

A fifth mistake is treating the security master's mapping itself as fixed once written. §4.3.1 called CUSIP and ISIN reassignment "not zero," and it isn't: a bankruptcy reorganisation can leave a company's old CUSIP retired and a new one issued for the reorganised equity, with the same underlying business on both sides of the split. A security master has to version its own mappings — which permanent key a given ticker or CUSIP pointed to, and when that stopped being true — rather than storing one static lookup table and trusting it forever.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the ticker is close enough"

It rarely is. §4.3.1's ticker-reuse example is not a contrived edge case: exchanges recycle three- and four-letter tickers routinely, and a delisting followed within a year or two by a fresh listing under the same letters is common enough that any pipeline joining on ticker alone will eventually splice two unrelated companies together — silently, with no error and no warning.

4.3.8 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Adjustment factor — the number, computed from one corporate action or futures roll, that a pipeline multiplies into every price before that event's date.
Adjusted price — a raw quoted price rescaled by the cumulative adjustment factor, anchored so today's value matches today's actual quote.
Total-return index — an adjusted price series rebased to a fixed starting value, the way an index provider publishes dividend-reinvested performance.
Security master — the reference database mapping every external identifier and corporate-action history for a security onto one permanent internal key.
Security identifier — any code (ticker, CUSIP, ISIN, FIGI) used to name a security; only some are stable and never reused.
Continuous futures — a single price series built by splicing a sequence of expiring futures contracts under a roll schedule.
Back-adjustment — splicing continuous futures by adding the dollar gap between contracts at each roll; exact for dollar P&L, but distant history can go negative.
Ratio adjustment — splicing continuous futures by multiplying by the price ratio between contracts at each roll; exact for percentage returns and always positive, at the cost of a distorted absolute level.
Roll schedule — the rule, calendar-based or liquidity-based, that decides which contract a continuous series holds on each day.

Check yourself

1. A vendor's history for "AAPL" shows a huge one-day move you can't tie to any news or filing. What do you check first?
Whether the ticker was reused — whether the row before and the row after that day actually share the same permanent identifier in the security master, not just the same ticker string. §4.3.1's PERM-00185/PERM-00931 example produces exactly this signature: a fabricated jump with no underlying event.
2. A stock pays a 0.5%-of-price dividend, then later does a 3-for-1 split. What two numbers get multiplied into the cumulative adjustment factor for a date before both events, and does the order you apply them in matter?
The dividend factor 1/(1+q) ≈ 0.995 and the split factor 1/3 ≈ 0.333, multiplied together to about 0.331; order never matters because multiplication commutes — the lab's four dividends and one split compound to a cumulative factor of 0.327 by day 0 regardless of the sequence you apply them in.
3. You are building a moving-average crossover system sized in a fixed number of contracts, and separately computing the 20-year Sharpe ratio of a commodity strategy. Which continuous-futures adjustment fits each job?
The crossover system: back-adjusted, because its dollar-based rules (a fixed-point stop, a fixed position size) need the series' day-to-day dollar changes to be real. The Sharpe ratio: ratio-adjusted (or the true per-contract percentage returns directly), because Sharpe is computed on percentage returns and ratio adjustment reproduces those exactly, however extreme the curve.
4. A roll rule is defined as "switch into whichever contract has the highest volume over the next ten trading days." Why can't you backtest this rule as stated?
It looks ahead: on any day in the backtest, the next ten days' volume is not yet known. A roll schedule must be computable from information available strictly as of that day — the same point-in-time discipline Session 4.2 applies to every other rule in this unit, applied here to the decision of which contract you are even in.
