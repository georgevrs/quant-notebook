Time, Calendars, As-of Joins & Bars

Every backtest quietly trusts two things about time: that a timestamp means what you think it means, and that a join never lets tomorrow's information leak into today's decision. Both break more often than engineers expect — across time zones, across exchange holidays, and across the fundamentals-reporting lag that turns an innocent-looking join into a look-ahead bug. This session builds the machinery that gets time right, then uses it to sample a tick stream into bars that respect how much actually happened, not just how many minutes passed. The core move: never join or sample on a clock that a trader living in the past could not yet have read.

4.4.1 One instant, several clocks

A timestamp with no time zone is a bare number: it cannot be compared, joined or converted until you know which clock produced it.

A time zone is a named rule (maintained, since 2011, by IANA's tz database) for converting between a fixed reference clock — Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) — and a local wall clock that some region's law changes twice a year. America/New_York sits at UTC−5 in January and UTC−4 in July: 09:30 on the exchange floor is 14:30Z in winter and 13:30Z in summer. Every system in a trading stack — the feed handler, the database, the risk engine, the chart you eyeball — must agree on which of these it is holding, or two systems will silently disagree by exactly one hour for eight months of the year.

Precision matters as much as offset. Write timestamps in ISO 8601 (2026-03-08T14:30:00Z) rather than a locale-dependent string like 3/8/26 2:30 PM: the format is unambiguous about year-month-day order, carries an explicit zone, and — written this way — sorts correctly even as plain text. pandas 3 can store a Timestamp at a resolution as fine as nanoseconds; a feed that timestamps two prints a microsecond apart needs that resolution to keep them in the right order, while a daily close needs none of it. Pick the coarsest resolution that is still unambiguous for the data at hand, store it consistently, and never let two resolutions share a column — that is a quieter version of the same bug as the naive offset above.

Daylight saving is not a rounding error — it is two calendar days a year on which the mapping from local time to UTC is not even a function. In 2026 the United States springs forward on 2026-03-08 (the local hour 02:00-03:00 never happens: tz_localize must be told to shift it) and falls back on 2026-11-01 (the local hour 01:00-02:00 happens twice, an ambiguous time that pandas will refuse to guess about). The lab localises 2026-03-08 02:30 and gets 03:00 EDT back — the missing half hour is simply swallowed — and shows that the two readings of 2026-11-01 01:30 are 1 hour apart in UTC.

📌 Key idea — store UTC, display local

Every timestamp that crosses a system boundary should already be UTC (or carry an explicit offset); conversion to a human's local clock happens only at the edge, for display. A naive datetime with no time zone attached is not a fact about the world — it is a guess waiting to be wrong twice a year.

🧭 Margin note — DST is jurisdiction-specific too

The European Union's clock changes (EU Directive 2000/84/EC) fall on the last Sunday of March and October — in 2026, 29 March and 25 October — not the United States' second-Sunday-of-March / first-Sunday-of-November rule. For three weeks every spring and one week every autumn, New York and Frankfurt are not both on daylight time at once, and the UTC gap between them is briefly one hour different from the rest of the year. A global system that hard-codes "New York is 6 hours behind Frankfurt" is wrong on four weeks of most years.

4.4.2 A (toy) exchange calendar

A time zone converts a reading. An exchange calendar answers the harder question: which readings sit inside a trading session at all?

Weekends are the easy five-sevenths of the problem. What is left is exchange-specific and changes slowly: roughly nine full closures a year, a couple of early closes, and — every so often — a change to the hours themselves. On 5 November 2024 the Tokyo Stock Exchange moved its close from 15:00 to 15:30 JST, its first change to trading hours in seventy years; treat a calendar as a fact you look up for a dated jurisdiction, never as a constant you hard-code once.

The lab implements exactly this stack for a simplified NYSE-style calendar: fixed holidays (New Year's Day, Independence Day, Juneteenth, Christmas), rules that move with the calendar (Martin Luther King Jr. Day is the third Monday of January; Good Friday is fixed by Easter, computed with the Gregorian algorithm since it moves by up to a month), a weekend-observance shift, and two early closes at 1:00pm ET. In 2026-11-26's year there are 10 full closures, 2 early closes, and 251 trading days in total. Independence Day falls on a Saturday in 2026, so the exchange actually closes on 2026-07-03 instead — the kind of one-line rule that silently breaks a calendar built by hand.

Two more edge cases are worth knowing before you trust a calendar. Financial systems deliberately ignore leap seconds — the occasional extra second inserted into UTC to track Earth's slowing rotation — because a calendar built on a minute that occasionally has 61 seconds is more disruptive than letting exchange clocks drift a fraction of a second and resynchronise instead. And an instrument that trades on more than one market — a dual-listed stock, an ETF holding foreign equities — is genuinely open only when both calendars agree: the correct trading calendar for it is the intersection of the two, never either one alone.

💡 Looking ahead

A production system does not hand-roll this: it calls a maintained calendar package (pandas_market_calendars, or its upstream exchange_calendars) with fifty-plus exchanges kept current by other people. What you build here is small on purpose — enough to see every moving part once. The bars this calendar feeds, and the corporate actions that also change what a "price" means, are Session 4.3 — Corporate Actions, Security Master & Continuous Futures's job; the columnar store everything eventually lands in is Session 4.5 — The Research Storage Stack.

4.4.3 As-of joins that never peek

An as-of join attaches to each row of one table the most recent matching row of another — as of that row's timestamp, never a later one.

Point-in-time research (Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases's subject) depends on exactly one discipline at the join: the key you join on must be the moment a fact became knowable, not the moment it happened. A quarter's earnings are true as of the last day of the quarter, but nobody could trade on them until the filing actually posted — typically weeks later. Join daily prices to fundamentals on the filing date and you have a correct, if unglamorous, point-in-time table; join on the quarter's end date instead and every price row silently "knows" a number before it existed.

The lab builds sixteen quarters of synthetic fundamentals across four years, each with a reporting lag jittered around 44 days, and joins them to simulated daily prices with pandas.merge_asof(..., direction="backward") on the filing timestamp. A second, deliberately buggy version joins on the quarter's end date instead: it knows the future on 46% of trading days, by as much as 53 days right after a quarter closes and 22 days on average across the whole leaking window — exactly the shape of bug Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases calls look-ahead bias.

directionMatchesUse it for
backward (default)the last right-row at or before the left keyalmost everything in research — "what did we know by then?"
forwardthe first right-row at or after the left keyrare: e.g. "the next scheduled rebalance after this signal"
nearestwhichever right-row is closest either waynever for point-in-time joins — it can match on the future

A tolerance matters just as much as direction: without one, a backward join carries a value forward forever once a source stops reporting — a delisted company's last known price, an index constituent that quietly stopped filing. The lab drops two consecutive quarters to open a real 180-plus-day gap and shows that a 200-day tolerance turns 51 rows that would otherwise have silently reused a stale number into an honest, explicit missing value. The same discipline works in SQL: DuckDB's native ASOF LEFT JOIN — this unit's research engine, alongside polars — reproduces the pandas result exactly, over Arrow-backed tables it can query where they already sit.

DuckDB's ASOF JOIN requires exactly one inequality on the ordering column (px.date >= fx.available_time above) and, in its plain form, keeps only the closest preceding match per left row; ASOF LEFT JOIN additionally keeps left rows with no match yet — the SQL equivalent of merge_asof's left-join default. nearest and forward are not simply wrong, either: aligning a trade print to the prevailing quote a few milliseconds either side of it is a nearest problem, because the question is "what price was live," not "what could I have known." The point-in-time discipline bites specifically when the answer to a join changes who could have legally acted on it — every signal-construction join, and few pure microstructure-alignment ones.

🧭 Margin note — merge inputs must already be sorted

merge_asof (and DuckDB's ASOF JOIN) require both sides sorted on the join column; an unsorted key does not raise an error, it silently returns wrong matches. Sort explicitly, every time, even when you are fairly sure the data already is.

4.4.4 The math: event time, thresholds & why sampling changes the shape

A tick stream is a sequence of events, not a function of clock time. A bar is just a rule for cutting that sequence into pieces — and different rules produce different pieces.

Write the tick stream as triples (τi,pi,vi)(\tau_i, p_i, v_i) — the clock time, price and size of trade ii. A bar-construction rule partitions the index i=1,2,…i = 1, 2, \ldots into consecutive runs, each run becoming one bar's open/high/low/close/volume. A time bar cuts every Δt\Delta t seconds of clock time, however many or few ticks land inside. A volume bar or dollar bar cuts the first time cumulative size (shares, or price times shares) crosses a threshold ν\nu — a bar ends at the smallest TT with

∑i=1Tvi≥ν\sum_{i=1}^{T} v_i \ge \nu

An imbalance bar tracks not size but direction. The tick rule assigns each trade a sign bt=sign(pt−pt−1)b_t = \mathrm{sign}(p_t - p_{t-1}), carrying the previous sign forward on a zero tick. A bar ends at the first TT for which the running signed sum θT\theta_T outruns an adaptive threshold:

θT=∑i=1Tbi,|θT|≥𝔼0[T]|𝔼0[b]|\theta_T = \sum_{i=1}^{T} b_i, \qquad |\theta_T| \ge \E_0[T]\,\bigl|\E_0[b]\bigr|

where 𝔼0[T]\E_0[T] and 𝔼0[b]\E_0[b] are running estimates (an exponentially weighted average of previous bars) of the expected bar length and expected imbalance per tick. A symmetric random walk needs on the order of threshold2\text{threshold}^2 ticks to drift that far, by the √n rule, while a run of same-signed ticks reaches it in a number of ticks proportional to the threshold itself — so the bar clock runs fast exactly when order flow is one-sided, and crawls when it is noise.

🧭 Margin note — why sampling by activity looks more normal

If returns are a Brownian motion running on its own internal "activity clock" — Clark's (1973) subordinated-process idea — then sampling in fixed clock time re-mixes periods of different activity into bars of equal size, which fattens the tails of the resulting distribution (a mixture of different-variance normals is never itself normal). Sampling in volume or dollar time instead approximately undoes the mixing; Session 5.1 — Stylized Facts of Returns returns to this when it catalogues the stylized facts every return series shares.

4.4.5 In code: building four kinds of bars

The lab simulates one trading day of ticks — a mild busier-at-the-open-and-close shape plus a 20-minute "news" burst with a strong buy imbalance — with polars, then cuts it four ways.

Time bars are a straight group_by on floor-divided clock time; volume and dollar bars are a vectorised cumulative-sum trick, no explicit loop required:

# a new bar every time cumulative activity (shares, or price*shares) crosses `threshold`
def threshold_bars(t, price, volume, activity, threshold):
    bar_id = (np.cumsum(activity) // threshold).astype(int)
    return pd.DataFrame({"t": t, "price": price, "bar": bar_id}) \
             .groupby("bar").agg(open=("price", "first"), close=("price", "last"))

Tick imbalance bars need the running threshold from §4.4.4, so a single pass over the ticks does the accounting — fast enough that thirty independent trading days run in a few seconds, well inside the lab's time budget. One representative session turns 45,678 ticks into 195 time bars, 195 volume bars (deliberately count-matched, so the comparison in §4.4.7 is fair), 196 dollar bars, and only 36 tick-imbalance bars — most of the session is quiet enough that an imbalance bar takes a long time to close.

The tick rule that imbalance bars depend on is a single vectorised pass, not a loop: take the sign of each price change, and where two consecutive prints share a price — about 15% of ticks in the lab's synthetic stream — carry the previous sign forward instead of recording a meaningless zero.

# b_t = sign(P_t - P_t-1); a repeated price carries the previous sign forward
def tick_rule(price):
    diffs = np.diff(price, prepend=price[0])
    b = np.sign(diffs)
    b[0] = 1.0
    return pd.Series(np.where(b == 0.0, np.nan, b)).ffill().to_numpy()

Two knobs keep the bar-cutting recursion honest: a warm-up window (200 ticks in the lab) that seeds the very first threshold before any bar has closed, and a minimum bar length (5 ticks) that stops one lucky run of same-signed prints from closing a one-tick bar and destabilising the next threshold estimate.

💡 Tip — pick the threshold from the data, not a guess

Set the volume or dollar threshold to (total activity) / (bars you would have gotten with a chosen time-bar resolution). That count-matches the sampling rules so a comparison between them — like §4.4.7's — measures the shape of the returns, not just a difference in sample size.

4.4.6 Where people go wrong: the join that peeks

The bug in §4.4.3 is invisible in the code and catastrophic in the backtest — it makes an untradeable strategy look like the best idea you have ever had.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the date on the row is close enough"

A quarter's row is dated by the period it describes, not by when anyone could read it. Joining on period_end feels harmless — it is a real date attached to real data — which is exactly why the bug survives code review. The fix is not more care; it is a different column: join on the timestamp the source system stamped when the value became public, and if your data vendor does not give you one, treat every number as available only from a conservative, dated assumption about typical reporting lag.

The same discipline extends past fundamentals: any table that describes "as of period X" but was recorded or corrected later — restated financials, delisting notices, revised macro releases — needs its availability timestamp preserved through every join, or a backtest built on it is quietly grading itself with the answer key. Session 4.2 — Point-in-Time Data & Data Biases is the session that catalogues these biases in depth; this one is where you fix the join that causes them.

A second, easily missed version of the same bug hides in cross-market joins. Aligning a US-listed ADR to its home-market ordinary share by calendar date alone breaks whenever the two exchanges disagree about which days are trading days — a US holiday is rarely a UK or Japanese one — or whenever "the close" on each side lands on a different UTC instant because the two markets keep different hours (§4.4.2). The fix is the same discipline as §4.4.3, generalised: convert every source to UTC, resolve each side against its own exchange calendar, and join on availability, never on a shared-looking date string that only coincidentally lines up most of the time.

4.4.7 Fixed-time bars vs information-driven bars, compared

Sample by the clock and a burst of real activity gets crammed into one giant bar or spread thin across many empty ones. Sample by activity instead and the bar boundaries move with the market.

The lab measures this rather than just illustrating it. Over 30 independent simulated trading days (a fresh random stream per day), the excess kurtosis of bar log returns — zero for a perfectly normal distribution, higher for fatter tails — averages 16.0 for time bars, −0.06 for count-matched volume bars, and 1.10 for dollar bars. Time bars are more leptokurtic than volume bars by 16.1 ± 0.06 (four standard errors clears this easily), and more leptokurtic than dollar bars by 14.9 ± 0.06 — a real, repeatable effect across 30 independent sessions, not a one-seed accident. The mechanism is exactly the handful of bars that straddle the news burst: swollen by extra ticks and an extra-strong move, they are the outliers that fatten the whole distribution's tail (§4.4.4).

Tick imbalance bars respond to a different feature of the same session: direction, not size. Averaged over the same 30 days, a bar closes in about 94 seconds when a majority of its ticks fall inside the informed-trading burst, against 1188 seconds — nearly twenty minutes — when they do not: a gap of 1094 ± 104 seconds, again comfortably past four standard errors. A one-sided run of ticks reaches the threshold in a number of ticks proportional to the threshold itself; a symmetric random walk needs the threshold squared — the √n rule from §4.4.4, working in your favour or against it depending on whether anyone is actually trying to move the price.

Which clock to sample by is mostly a question of what the story behind a signal actually runs on. If the phenomenon itself is clock-driven — an option's time decay, a funding rate accruing overnight — time bars are the right sampling clock, because the thing you are measuring runs on the clock too. If the phenomenon is about information arriving in the market — an order-flow signal, a microstructure feature, anything that should behave similarly whether the session was quiet or frantic — sample by volume, dollar value, or imbalance instead, and expect the number of bars produced per day to vary a lot from one day to the next: that variation is the whole point, not a bug to smooth away.

4.4.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Timestamp — a reading from a clock; meaningless for comparison or joining until its time zone (or UTC offset) is known.
Time zone — a named, dated rule for converting between UTC and a local wall clock that a jurisdiction can change (including twice a year for daylight saving).
Exchange calendar — the sessions, holidays, half days and hours that define which instants are inside a market's trading day.
As-of join — a join that attaches to each row the most recent matching row of another table as of its timestamp, never a later one.
Time bars — bars formed by cutting a tick stream at fixed intervals of clock time, regardless of how much activity occurred.
Volume bars — bars formed by cutting a tick stream whenever cumulative traded volume crosses a threshold.
Dollar bars — bars formed by cutting a tick stream whenever cumulative traded dollar value crosses a threshold.
Imbalance bars — bars formed by cutting a tick stream whenever the running signed order flow outruns an adaptive expected threshold.

Check yourself

1. A scheduler converts "NYSE open, 09:30 ET" to UTC by always adding 5 hours. From November to March it fires exactly on time; from March to November it is consistently an hour off. What's wrong, and what's the fix?
The scheduler hard-codes winter's UTC−5 offset and ignores that America/New_York moves to UTC−4 under daylight saving. The fix is to store and convert with a real time-zone-aware timestamp (backed by the IANA tz database), never a fixed-integer offset, so the conversion is automatically correct on both sides of the two DST changeover days each year.
2. Your backtest treats every weekday as a full trading day. On 2026-11-27 it computes a whole day's return for a session that actually closed at 1:00pm. What breaks, and how do you fix it?
Any bar, signal or fill timestamped after 1:00pm ET on that date is being generated for a session that was not open — a fabricated fact that cannot appear in live trading. The fix is an exchange calendar that knows about early closes (§4.4.2), not just holidays, and a data pipeline that queries it before treating a weekday as a normal session.
3. A colleague joins daily prices to quarterly EPS with merge_asof(direction="backward") on the fiscal quarter's end date, and reports an excellent backtest Sharpe ratio. What is wrong, and what would you change?
The join key is the period the number describes, not the moment it became public — the classic look-ahead bias. On every day between the quarter's end and the actual filing date, the backtest is trading on information from the future. The fix is to join on the filing/availability timestamp instead, adding a conservative assumed lag if the vendor does not supply one.
4. Why add a tolerance to an as-of join at all, if direction="backward" already prevents look-ahead?
Backward direction stops the join from reaching into the future, but with no tolerance it will happily reach arbitrarily far into the past — silently carrying forward a stale value forever once a source stops reporting (a delisted name, a company that missed a filing). A tolerance turns "too old to trust" into an explicit missing value instead of a quiet guess.
5. A single large news event happens in the last few minutes of a fixed 5-minute time bar, which then shows an extreme return. Is that a genuine fat tail in the return distribution, or partly a sampling artifact — and what would a dollar bar have done differently?
Partly an artifact of the sampling clock: the fixed window lumped an unusually large amount of activity into one bar, inflating both its size and its return. A dollar bar would have closed several narrower bars during the burst instead of one wide one, spreading the same price move across bars whose returns look more alike in scale — which is exactly why time-bar returns show far higher excess kurtosis than count-matched dollar-bar returns in §4.4.7.
