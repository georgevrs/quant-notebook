Unit 1 · The Quant Landscape — Session 01 of 05

What Quants Actually Do

"Quant" is not one job. It is at least five jobs, spread across half a dozen kinds of firm that make money in completely different ways: a market maker earns a sliver on a million trades, a trend follower makes a few big bets a year, a bank prices products for clients, an asset manager charges a small fee on a very large pile. Each business has its own P&L shape, its own way of dying, and its own idea of a good quant. The core move: follow the money — who pays the firm, for what, and what can kill it — and the job description follows.

1.1.1 The four jobs in every quant business

Strip away the firm names and every quant business has to answer the same four questions, every day. The job titles are just who answers which one.

A quant uses mathematics, statistics and code to make trading and investment decisions, or builds the machinery that makes them. The forecaster, the person sizing the bet, the person whose code sends the orders and the person whose job is to say "no" are all quants. They sit around one thing: a book, the set of positions a firm holds, with its P&L (profit and loss) and its risk.

A quant researcher (QR) asks what will happen: they turn data and hypotheses into a signal — a number that forecasts something — and prove, as honestly as they can, that it works. A quant trader (QT) asks what do we do, and how big: sizing, execution, hedging, and living with the P&L. A quant developer (QD) asks can the machine do it: data pipelines, backtester, trading platform. Around them sit risk and model-validation quants, who ask what could kill us and can stop things, and the portfolio manager (PM), who owns the book, its capital and the final decision.

📌 Key idea — a role is the number you answer for

Titles vary wildly between firms; accountability does not. The researcher answers for whether the forecast was right, the trader for the P&L, the developer for whether the system did what it was told, and the risk quant for the loss nobody saw coming. When you read a job advert, ignore the title and ask which number you will be judged on. ("Quant" at a bank often means a pricing-model specialist; "quant trader" at an electronic firm, someone who runs automated strategies.)

1.1.2 How an idea becomes a position

Roles only make sense in motion. Follow one idea from a researcher's notebook to a live position and you meet every seat in the firm — and every place things go wrong.

At a large firm each box is a different team; on your own you are every box, and the handoffs become checklists that are easy to skip. Session 1.3 — The Quant Research Lifecycle turns the top half of this picture into a research process with explicit gates; Unit 15 builds the bottom half as software.

Engineers tend to underrate step 3 and overrate step 1. A mediocre signal run by a correct, well-monitored system usually beats a brilliant one run by a leaky system, which is why the course spends as long on data (Unit 4), honest backtesting (Unit 6) and production systems (Unit 15) as on forecasting.

1.1.3 The map: buy side, sell side & the firms between

Every trade has two sides. The industry is organised around which side you are on, whose money you use, and who pays you for what.

The buy side invests capital and buys trading services to do it: asset managers, hedge funds, and the pensions, endowments and insurers whose money most of them manage. The sell side — mostly banks and brokers — sells those services: execution, research, financing, derivatives, a price whenever a client asks. Orders flow from the buy side through the sell side to exchanges and other venues; money flows back as fees, commissions and spreads. Session 2.1 — Market Anatomy & the Life of an Order follows one order through that plumbing.

Some firms fit neither label. A proprietary trading firm trades only its own capital: it manages no client money (though it may have shareholders, even a listing), keeps every dollar it makes and eats every dollar it loses. Many of the best known are market makers, firms that continuously quote a price to buy and a price to sell on every venue that will have them. The table names a few well-known firms of each type, as of 2026, purely for orientation.

Engineers often overlook the sell side. Banks split trading into flow — standardised, high-volume products such as government bonds, currencies and plain swaps, priced fast and automatically for a small spread — and exotics, bespoke structured products sold at a higher margin, where the risk is a wrong pricing model. Bank quants build and hedge those models (Unit 10) and compute XVA, the valuation adjustments for counterparty credit, funding and capital.

🧭 Margin note — why prop trading left the banks (US, as of 2026)

The Volcker Rule — section 619 of the Dodd-Frank Act, with compliance required from 21 July 2015 and simplified by amendments in 2019–2020 — bars US banks from most short-term proprietary trading while exempting market making for clients and hedging, which is one reason prop trading now lives mainly in independent firms.

1.1.4 Follow the money: business models & fees

One question explains more about a quant job than any title: where does the firm's revenue come from, and what does it have to risk to earn it?

Firms that keep their own P&L

A market maker is paid by people who want to trade now: it keeps the spread between its bid and offer, often collects a rebate for posting quotes (maker-taker fees), and risks trading with someone who knows more — adverse selection, formalised by Glosten & Milgrom (1985). Done well, its P&L is astonishingly smooth: Virtu's 2014 IPO filing reported one losing trading day in 1,238 (2009–2013). Menkveld (2013) found both halves of the business in one large high-frequency market maker: about €1.55 per trade earned on the spread, net of fees, and €0.68 lost on the positions left behind (figures from the 2011 working-paper version). The scale now rivals banks: Jane Street's 2024 net trading revenue, $20.5 billion, exceeded Citigroup's and Bank of America's trading revenue, according to documents seen by Bloomberg (April 2025).

Firms paid by investors

A hedge fund — a private fund for professional investors that can go long and short and use leverage — charges a management fee on assets and a performance fee on profits, the latter only above its high-water mark, the highest value the investor's stake has reached. The headline is "2 and 20"; HFR's industry averages for the first quarter of 2026 were 1.32% and 15.78%. Multi-manager funds ("pod shops") run dozens of PM teams, each with its own capital and tight loss limits, and most bill investors a pass-through of the fund's costs, staff pay included, plus a performance fee. An asset manager charges a small percentage of a very large pile — in 2025 the asset-weighted average was 0.14% a year for US index equity ETFs and 0.40% for US equity mutual funds (ICI) — so its quants build index, factor and risk products where precision and cost matter most.

In words: the investor gets the gross return minus the management fee, minus costs passed through, minus a share of whatever is left if it is positive. The pass-through row is illustrative, but the pattern is documented: Bloomberg reported that at Balyasny a 15.2% gross return in 2023 became a 2.8% gain for investors, and a BNP Paribas estimate put investors' share of multi-strategy profits at 41 cents per dollar in 2023, down from 54 in 2021. Defenders reply that net returns stay competitive — a 2022 Barclays study of 300 funds found pass-through funds returned 11.8% after fees against 6.4% for peers (both estimates via eFinancialCareers) — so treat it as contested.

⚠️ Pitfall — "the fund made 10%, so its investors made 10%"

Only the net return reaches the investor, and the gap is set by the business model, not by skill. Ask of every performance figure: gross or net, net of which costs, and measured from where relative to the high-water mark. Ask the same of your own backtests: a strategy that only works before realistic costs does not work (Session 6.5).

Bank quants, finally, are judged on whether prices and hedges hold up, because clients, not market direction, pay the bank. Crypto trading firms live off spreads, funding payments on perpetual futures and gaps between venues, and carry a risk traditional firms hand to clearing houses: the venue itself can fail with your money on it — counterparty risk made real by FTX's collapse in November 2022 (Session 2.6).

1.1.5 Two businesses, one market

Business models are easy to describe and hard to feel, so the lab builds two tiny quant businesses and lets them trade the same market with the same money for ten years.

The lab simulates one liquid instrument for 10 years — annualised volatility 16%, no built-in risk premium — as a slow hidden drift that wanders up and down (the trends), plus noise, plus news jumps. Each business gets $1,000,000 and keeps trading that amount; profits are not reinvested.

  • The market maker quotes 2 basis points either side of the mid-price, earns a 0.2 bp rebate, and trades with about 400 clients a day, $10,000 each. 5% of them are informed — they know where the price is about to go — and on news days more arrive.
  • The trend follower is long when a fast moving average of the price is above a slow one and short otherwise, sizes to 15% annual volatility (at most 3× capital), and pays 5 bp per dollar traded.

The core of each toy is a few lines:

# market maker: every trade earns half the spread plus the rebate;
# an informed client takes back the move they knew was coming
edge = HALF_SPREAD + REBATE
informed = flow["u"] < informed_share
per_trade = np.where(informed, edge - flow["informed_move"], edge - flow["side"] * flow["hold_move"])

# trend follower: direction from two moving averages, size from volatility
direction = np.sign(log_price.ewm(span=FAST_SPAN).mean() - log_price.ewm(span=SLOW_SPAN).mean())
vol_est = market["ret"].ewm(span=VOL_SPAN).std() * np.sqrt(PERIODS_PER_YEAR)
weight = (direction * TARGET_VOL / vol_est).clip(-MAX_LEVERAGE, MAX_LEVERAGE).fillna(0.0)
held = weight.shift(1).fillna(0.0)                      # decided at yesterday's close

Look at the shapes before the numbers. The market maker's curve is almost a straight line with small dents on loud news days ($126,154 a year on this path). The trend follower's lurches — long flat or falling stretches, then a few trends that make the decade ($72,894 a year) — while $1 simply held in the market became $1.02 (the toy market has no built-in risk premium). The market maker is a classic negative-skew business. The trend follower is its mirror image: it loses most trades but wins several times as much when it wins — on this path its three best trades earned 60% of its gross gains, the option-like profile Fung & Hsieh (2001) found in real trend-following funds. Their daily P&L is typically uncorrelated (median correlation 0.00), which is the logic behind multi-strategy firms.

📌 Key idea — the business model sets the return shape

A market maker sells immediacy and is paid a little on almost every trade; a trend follower bets that moves continue and is paid a lot on a few. The first wins most days and occasionally loses badly; the second loses most trades and occasionally wins big. Know the shape of your P&L before you judge a single number in it — a bad month means something different in each business, and so do the risk limits.

1.1.6 Thin edges & lucky paths

A typical Sharpe ratio of 11 looks like free money, and a trend follower that typically spends 762 trading days under water looks broken. Neither impression survives a closer look — and the reasons are what quants in each business spend their days on.

The market maker's edge is thinner than it looks

In words: every everyday trade earns the half-spread hh plus the rebate bb; a fraction qq of clients are informed and each takes back the move they knew was coming, |Δ|\lvert\Delta\rvert on average; and news days cost a roughly fixed ℓ\ell per everyday trade. So the expected P&L per trade, and the break-even informed share, are

𝔼[P&L per trade]=(h+b)−q𝔼|Δ|−ℓ,q⋆=h+b−ℓ𝔼|Δ|\E[\text{P\&L per trade}] = (h + b) - q\,\E\lvert\Delta\rvert - \ell, \qquad q^{\star} = \frac{h + b - \ell}{\E\lvert\Delta\rvert}

Here h+bh + b is 2.2 bp and 𝔼|Δ|\E\lvert\Delta\rvert is 7.98 bp. Without news (ℓ=0\ell = 0) the business would break even at 27.6% informed clients; with the measured ℓ\ell of 0.55 bp the formula predicts 20.7% and the simulation finds 20.7%. The lab keeps the client flow fixed and turns up only the informed share:

At 5% informed the toy earns $126,154 a year; at 15% it earns $45,984; at 25% it loses $34,790. That is adverse selection: the spread is the fee for trading with you, and it has to pay for the clients who know more than you do. It is what market-making quants obsess over — widening quotes before news, cancelling stale quotes fast (why latency matters), managing inventory risk, and prizing flow they believe is uninformed. Session 14.2 — Microstructure Models gives the models; Session 14.5 — Market Making builds a real quoting strategy.

⚠️ Pitfall — "a Sharpe ratio of 11? Market making must be the best business"

The toy leaves out what makes the edge expensive — the technology race, fees, colocation, staff, and competitors who narrow the spread until the marginal firm barely breaks even — and hides the capacity limit: profit comes from client flow, not capital, so twice the money earns the same dollars. Menkveld's market maker had an annualised Sharpe ratio of about 9 in the 2011 working-paper version; the published version stresses that the figure depends heavily on how much capital you assume it ties up. A high Sharpe says the edge is reliable, not that it is available to you.

The trend follower's edge takes decades to prove

Across the 200 worlds, the market maker's Sharpe ratio lands between 8.8 and 13.0 in 80% of them and is never negative. The trend follower — whose edge is real by construction — lands anywhere between 0.04 and 1.27 and loses money over a whole decade in 7.5% of worlds. The paths in the charts are ordinary draws, at percentiles 33 (market maker) and 34 (trend follower).

This changes how quants are judged. A market-making team can test a change to its quoting in days, because 100,805 trades a year make even a tiny difference visible. A trend-following team cannot: with a few trades a year its P&L is mostly luck over any horizon a manager has patience for, so it is judged on process and on evidence pooled across many markets and decades — which is where the real case for trend following comes from (Session 8.1 — Time-Series Momentum & Trend Following). How many years a Sharpe ratio needs before you can believe it is Session 5.2's question; Session 1.2 — Where Returns Come From names each toy's source of edge.

1.1.7 Choosing your game

You know the seats and the businesses. Now match them to what you are good at, what you enjoy, and what you can realistically get into.

Match the game to your strengths. Love fast, correct systems? Electronic market making and quant development. Love finding structure in noisy data and proving it honestly? Research at a systematic fund. Like deciding under pressure? Trading. Instinct to break other people's models? Risk and validation. And if you want to trade your own money, pick games where being small is not a handicap — slower strategies with modest turnover — because §1.1.6 showed why you cannot win the market maker's game from a laptop: speed and flow decide your informed share.

Be clear-eyed about the funnel, too: the famous seats are few — one veteran buy-side quant, Giuseppe Paleologo, informally estimated in 2024 that there are about 700 new buy-side quant jobs a year worldwide. Session 16.1 — Career Paths & the Quant Job Market covers the job market properly, Session 16.4 — Running Your Own Capital covers trading your own book, and Session 1.4 — The Engineer's Edge, Blind Spots & Honest Odds gives the evidence on who actually makes money.

⚠️ Pitfall — "I'll aim for whichever seat pays most"

The highest-paid seats are paid out of P&L, and P&L-linked seats are the least forgiving: a pod PM who breaches a loss limit can lose their capital in weeks. Pick the seat whose daily loop you would enjoy even in a bad year — the forecast, the P&L, the machine or the veto — because the job is mostly that loop, repeated for years.

1.1.9 Glossary · Check yourself · Go deeper

Quant researcher — finds, tests and documents predictive signals; judged on out-of-sample forecast quality and the P&L it leads to.
Quant trader — turns forecasts into positions (sizing, execution, hedging); judged on P&L and risk-adjusted return.
Quant developer — builds the data, research and trading infrastructure; judged on correctness, uptime and speed.
Portfolio manager — the person accountable for a book's P&L, who decides what to hold and how much risk to take.
Buy side — firms that invest capital and buy trading services to do it: asset managers, hedge funds, pensions, insurers.
Sell side — firms that sell trading services to the buy side — execution, market making, research, financing, derivatives; mostly banks and brokers.
Proprietary trading firm — a firm that trades only its own capital — it manages no client money; it keeps all its P&L and bears all its losses.
Market maker — a firm that continuously quotes both a bid and an ask, earning the spread and bearing inventory and adverse-selection risk.
Hedge fund — a private fund for professional investors that can go long and short and use leverage; paid a management fee and a performance fee.
Asset manager — a firm that invests pooled or segregated client money, mostly long-only, for a fee that is a percentage of assets.
Management fee — an annual fee charged as a percentage of the assets managed, paid whatever the performance.
Performance fee — a share of the profits paid to the manager, usually only on gains above the high-water mark.
High-water mark — the highest value an investor's stake has reached; performance fees are paid only on gains above it.

Check yourself

1. A friend's new job is "quant at a bank, building pricing models for the exotic-options desk." Buy side or sell side — and what will they be judged on?
Sell side. Clients pay the bank for the products, so your friend is judged on whether prices and hedges hold up and survive independent validation — not on predicting where the market goes.
2. In the lab's world, a rival starts capturing the market maker's uninformed clients, so the informed share of what is left rises from 5% to 15%. What happens to annual P&L, and why?
It falls from about $126,154 to about $45,984, and break-even is near 21%. Each informed trade costs the move the client knew about, about 8.0 bp against 2.2 bp earned — so who you trade with matters more than how often. That is adverse selection.
3. A hedge fund makes +10% gross. What does its investor get under "2 and 20", and under 6% pass-through costs plus a 20% performance fee? And in a +4% year?
+10%: 6.40% and 3.20%. +4%: 1.60% and −2.00%. Pass-through costs are charged whether or not the fund makes money, so in a lean year the fund can be up while its clients are down.
4. A trend follower has spent two and a half years below its previous peak, and the boss wants to shut it down. What would you check before agreeing?
Whether it is inside what the strategy's own evidence calls normal: in the lab the median longest spell under water is 762 trading days (about three years), and a genuinely profitable trend follower still loses money over a whole decade in 7.5% of worlds. Judge the process: is it trading as specified, and are costs as modelled? A market maker that long under water would be broken — its typical worst spell is 30 trading days.
5. A trend follower wins only 31% of its trades. Is it a bad strategy?
Not on that evidence. Its average win is typically 5.1 times its average loss. A hit rate means nothing without the payoff ratio — the market maker wins 65% of its trades with the opposite shape.
